Metalloptosis

1. Definition and Classification

Metalloptosis is best classified as an emerging umbrella concept encompassing regulated cell-death mechanisms in which metal-ion dyshomeostasis is a causal upstream determinant of cellular death. It should not currently be treated as a single independent execution pathway with one universally conserved sensor, signaling cascade, commitment point, executor, or terminal lesion [1-4].

The concept of a "metal death code" dictates cellular fate in response to specific metal stresses, reflecting crosstalk and divergence among death modalities [4].

Within the supplied evidence, the most mechanistically established members are ferroptosis and cuproptosis. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent regulated cell-death modality driven by phospholipid peroxidation [5-8]. Cuproptosis is a copper-dependent death mechanism centered on mitochondrial copper accumulation, copper binding to lipoylated tricarboxylic-acid-cycle proteins, protein aggregation, Fe–S-protein destabilization, and mitochondrial proteotoxic stress [9-12].

Other metal-associated routes included within the emerging metalloptosis framework are less standardized as members of a unified superfamily. Lysozincrosis describes a lysosome-centered Zn2+-dependent route in which TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Zn2+ release causes rapid mitochondrial dysfunction, ATP depletion, and non-apoptotic death [13]. Ca2+-overload/MPT-driven death involves mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, persistent mitochondrial permeability transition, bioenergetic collapse, swelling, and regulated necrosis [14-17].

The critical conceptual distinction is therefore:

metalloptosis ≠ one execution pathway;

metalloptosis = proposed family of metal-dependent regulated death mechanisms.

Accordingly, the term should identify a mechanistic category based on metal dependence rather than imply that iron-, copper-, zinc-, and calcium-associated deaths converge on one common terminal executor.

For RCDome, Metalloptosis is most defensibly represented as an emerging RCD-related category/concept or proposed superfamily rather than an independent modality equivalent to ferroptosis or cuproptosis.

2. Initiation and Triggers

Because metalloptosis encompasses several mechanistically distinct routes, no universal initiating receptor or sensor exists.

The common initiating principle is disruption of physiological metal-ion homeostasis to a degree that activates a metal-specific lethal biochemical reaction.

Iron-associated initiation is best represented by ferroptosis.

Major initiating mechanisms include inhibition of system xc−, cystine deprivation, glutathione depletion, direct inhibition or loss of GPX4, expansion of the labile iron pool, ferritin degradation, and metabolic conditions that increase oxidizable phospholipid substrates [5-8,18].

A canonical ferroptotic initiation route is:

system xc− inhibition → reduced cystine uptake → reduced cysteine availability → reduced glutathione synthesis → impaired GPX4-dependent phospholipid hydroperoxide detoxification → accumulation of oxidized phospholipids.

A second route bypasses cystine metabolism:

direct GPX4 inhibition → failure to reduce phospholipid hydroperoxides → lipid-radical propagation.

Iron contributes by supporting redox chemistry capable of generating and propagating lipid radicals [19-20].

Copper-associated initiation is mechanistically different.

Cuproptosis begins when copper accumulates within mitochondria under conditions in which mitochondrial respiration and protein lipoylation provide susceptible targets [9-12].

FDX1 is an important determinant of this vulnerability and is linked to Cu2+ reduction and the lipoylation-dependent machinery that enables copper to interact with lipoylated mitochondrial proteins [10-12].

Thus:

mitochondrial copper accumulation → FDX1-associated copper chemistry → copper binding to lipoylated TCA-cycle proteins → abnormal protein oligomerization/aggregation.

Zinc-associated initiation can occur through lysosomal zinc mobilization.

In the strongest lysozincrosis prototype:

TRPML1 activation → release of lysosomal Zn2+ → pathological intracellular Zn2+ redistribution → rapid mitochondrial dysfunction [13].

Additional zinc-associated deaths have been proposed to arise from extracellular zinc overload, lysosomal zinc accumulation, nanoparticles, or changes in zinc transport; in some models, these do not necessarily terminate through lysozincrosis and may instead engage ferroptosis, apoptosis, or autophagy-associated death [54].

Ca2+-associated metalloptotic models converge on mitochondrial Ca2+ overload.

A representative sequence is:

increased cytosolic Ca2+ → mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation + ROS → sensitization of the mitochondrial permeability-transition machinery → sustained high-conductance MPT → energetic collapse [14-17].

Therefore, the shared upstream principle is metal dyshomeostasis, while the immediate molecular sensor differs by route.

3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

Metalloptosis has no single universal core machinery.

Instead, each metal-dependent route is defined by its own biochemical core.

For ferroptosis, the central machinery couples lipid metabolism, iron availability, and antioxidant defense.

ACSL4 promotes activation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, including arachidonic and adrenic acids, to their acyl-CoA derivatives. LPCAT3 facilitates incorporation of these polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids, creating oxidizable substrates [25-27].

The core vulnerable substrate is therefore a PUFA-containing membrane phospholipid.

GPX4 provides a major protective reaction by reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides to their corresponding alcohols using glutathione [6,18,28].

When GPX4-dependent detoxification fails:

PUFA-phospholipid hydroperoxides → lipid radicals → chain-propagating phospholipid oxidation → membrane damage.

Iron supports this chemistry through redox-active Fe2+ and iron-dependent reactions that amplify lipid oxidation [19-20].

For cuproptosis, the core machinery is mitochondrial and proteotoxic.

FDX1-associated copper chemistry facilitates copper interaction with lipoylated mitochondrial proteins, particularly components of the TCA-cycle machinery such as DLAT [9-12].

The mechanistic sequence is:

mitochondrial Cu accumulation → interaction with lipoylated proteins → abnormal lipoylated-protein oligomerization/aggregation → Fe–S-protein destabilization → mitochondrial proteotoxic stress → metabolic collapse.

This is fundamentally different from ferroptosis because membrane lipid oxidation is not the defining initial lesion.

For lysozincrosis, the core machinery is the lysosome–Zn2+–mitochondria axis [13].

TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Zn2+ release produces a rapid zinc-dependent mitochondrial lesion associated with swelling and ATP loss.

For Ca2+/MPT-associated death, the core event is persistent opening of the mitochondrial permeability-transition pore, resulting in inner-membrane depolarization, oxidative-phosphorylation failure, matrix swelling, and bioenergetic collapse [14-17].

Thus, the metalloptosis family contains several distinct biochemical cores:

iron → phospholipid-peroxidation machinery;

copper → lipoylated mitochondrial-protein proteotoxicity;

zinc → lysosomal Zn2+ redistribution and mitochondrial injury;

calcium → mitochondrial permeability transition.

4. Propagation and Amplification

Propagation is route-specific.

In ferroptosis, propagation depends on self-amplifying phospholipid-peroxidation chemistry.

In the presence of labile iron, hydroperoxides undergo reductive cleavage to regenerate alkoxyl radicals that attack neighboring acyl chains, propagating the peroxidative cascade across the lipid bilayer [52].

ACSL4 and LPCAT3 promote susceptibility by ensuring the presence of oxidizable membrane substrates [25-27].

Ferritinophagy can amplify this process. NCOA4-dependent delivery of ferritin to autophagic degradation increases the redox-active intracellular iron pool, thereby favoring lipid-peroxidation chemistry [20,29].

Lipoxygenases can promote lipid oxidation in some systems, but the supplied evidence does not support universal obligate ALOX dependence across ferroptosis [6,25,28].

Therefore, ALOX enzymes should be classified as context-dependent promoters rather than universal ferroptosis executors.

In cuproptosis, propagation occurs primarily through mitochondrial proteotoxicity rather than lipid-radical chain reactions.

Increasing mitochondrial copper–protein interactions promote accumulation of aggregated lipoylated proteins and loss or destabilization of Fe–S proteins [9-12].

These alterations compromise mitochondrial metabolic functions and create an escalating proteotoxic and energetic stress.

In lysozincrosis, propagation is dominated by lysosome-to-mitochondria zinc toxicity.

TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Zn2+ release rapidly causes mitochondrial swelling, dysfunction, and ATP decline [13].

In Ca2+/MPT death, propagation occurs through sustained permeability transition.

Persistent mPTP opening causes mitochondrial depolarization, cessation of oxidative phosphorylation, osmotic matrix swelling, and progressive ATP depletion [14-17].

ROS can amplify several metalloptotic routes, but ROS is not a universal terminal executor.

ROS can enhance iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, promote zinc-associated mitochondrial injury, sensitize the mPTP to Ca2+, and interact with copper-associated metabolic stress [3-4].

Therefore, oxidative stress represents a common amplifier across multiple metal-dependent deaths while remaining mechanistically subordinate to the route-specific lethal chemistry.

5. Protective / Negative Regulators

Because metalloptosis is a family concept, no single protective system antagonizes every route.

In ferroptosis, the best-established defense is the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis [5-8,18].

SLC7A11 supports cystine uptake through system xc−. Cystine-derived cysteine supports glutathione synthesis, and glutathione enables GPX4 to detoxify phospholipid hydroperoxides.

Thus:

SLC7A11 → cystine/cysteine → GSH → GPX4 → reduction of phospholipid hydroperoxides.

Multiple parallel antioxidant systems can suppress ferroptosis independently of GPX4.

These include FSP1–CoQ10, mitochondrial DHODH–CoQ10, GCH1–BH4, and NRF2-associated antioxidant and iron-homeostasis programs [30-32].

ESCRT-III functions downstream of lipid-peroxidative membrane injury as a membrane-repair system. It can delay plasma-membrane failure and death kinetics but is not part of the initiating ferroptosis machinery [33-35].

Cuproptosis uses different protective chemistry.

Glutathione can bind and buffer copper, thereby limiting copper access to vulnerable mitochondrial proteins [36].

NFE2L2/NRF2 can increase GSH-associated defenses, while SLC25A39 supports mitochondrial glutathione availability [36].

Therefore, although GSH protects both ferroptosis and cuproptosis, its physical role differs:

ferroptosis → GSH supports GPX4-mediated peroxide reduction;

cuproptosis → GSH contributes to copper buffering/chelation.

In lysozincrosis, protective mechanisms include restriction of pathological TRPML1 activation and maintenance of zinc buffering and intracellular zinc homeostasis [13,37].

In Ca2+/MPT death, protective mechanisms include Ca2+ buffering, limitation of mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, redox control, and suppression or desensitization of sustained mPTP opening [14-17].

These differences reinforce that Metalloptosis does not possess a single anti-death checkpoint.

6. Commitment / Point of No Return

There is no universal point of no return for Metalloptosis.

Each route reaches commitment through a different irreversible lesion.

Spatial spread of ferroptosis occurs only when the local oxidative burden exceeds the combined redox-buffering and membrane repair capacity of neighboring cells, converting localized damage into expanding tissue injury [50].

Before this threshold, radical-trapping antioxidants, iron chelation, GPX4 activity, parallel antioxidant systems, and ESCRT-mediated repair can interrupt progression.

When oxidative damage to the plasma membrane overwhelms cellular antioxidant and repair defenses, increased membrane permeability, loss of ionic gradients and osmotic balance, and irreversible cell injury can follow [49].

For cuproptosis, commitment is better represented by accumulation of copper-induced lipoylated-protein aggregates together with Fe–S-protein destabilization sufficient to produce irreversible mitochondrial proteotoxic and metabolic dysfunction [9-12].

For lysozincrosis, commitment occurs when pathological lysosomal Zn2+ redistribution produces severe mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion from which the cell cannot recover [13].

For Ca2+/MPT death, commitment corresponds most closely to persistent high-conductance permeability transition affecting sufficient mitochondrial mass to cause irreversible oxidative-phosphorylation failure [14-17,53].

Thus, a family-level statement is possible only at a higher level:

metal-specific damage → failure of metal buffering and route-specific protective systems → self-propagating biochemical or bioenergetic injury → irreversible loss of cellular homeostasis.

7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

What physically kills the cell in Metalloptosis?

There is no single answer because Metalloptosis does not have a universal executor.

In ferroptosis, the defining lethal chemistry is uncontrolled phospholipid peroxidation.

As oxidized phospholipids accumulate, membrane permeability increases. Plasma-membrane nanopore-like defects and Ca2+ influx develop, followed by osmotic imbalance, swelling, and eventual plasma-membrane rupture [33,38-39].

Therefore, the physical terminal sequence is:

iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation → membrane structural failure → ion flux → osmotic swelling → plasma-membrane rupture.

No dedicated protein pore analogous to GSDMD or MLKL has been established as the universal ferroptosis executor.

In cuproptosis, execution is primarily mitochondrial, proteotoxic, and bioenergetic.

Copper binding to lipoylated TCA-cycle proteins promotes abnormal aggregation, while Fe–S-protein loss disrupts mitochondrial function [9-12].

The proximate terminal lesion is therefore best described as irreversible mitochondrial proteotoxic/metabolic collapse.

A dedicated pore-forming cuproptosis executor has not been established.

In lysozincrosis, the strongest supplied evidence supports:

lysosomal Zn2+ release → rapid mitochondrial injury/swelling → ATP depletion → bioenergetic failure → necrotic death [13].

Therefore, energetic collapse is the most defensible proximate lethal mechanism.

In Ca2+/MPT death:

mitochondrial Ca2+ overload → persistent mPTP opening → Δψm collapse → oxidative-phosphorylation arrest → ATP depletion + matrix swelling → ion-homeostasis failure → membrane rupture [14-17].

Thus, the terminal lesion is route-specific:

ferroptosis → lipid-peroxidative membrane catastrophe;

cuproptosis → mitochondrial proteotoxic/metabolic collapse;

lysozincrosis → zinc-driven mitochondrial energetic collapse;

Ca2+/MPT death → irreversible mitochondrial permeability transition and bioenergetic collapse.

This route-specific execution is the strongest reason not to describe Metalloptosis itself as a single independent execution pathway.

8. Morphological Sequence

Metalloptosis has no universal morphology.

Each subtype produces morphology associated with its own biochemical lesion.

Ferroptotic cells can initially remain morphologically intact while lipid peroxidation increases. Mitochondria often become smaller and electron dense, with reduced or altered cristae. As plasma-membrane damage progresses, ion flux and osmotic imbalance produce swelling and terminal membrane rupture without the classical apoptotic sequence of caspase-dependent fragmentation and apoptotic-body formation [7,39-41].

A simplified sequence is:

lipid-peroxidation accumulation → mitochondrial structural alterations → plasma-membrane permeability → cellular swelling → membrane rupture.

Cuproptosis shows a different organellar phenotype. Reported alterations include mitochondrial structural abnormalities, cristae disturbance, endoplasmic-reticulum dilation in some systems, DLAT oligomerization, and loss of Fe–S proteins [10-12].

However, the diagnostically important features are biochemical rather than morphological.

Lysozincrosis produces lysosomal Zn2+ redistribution followed by rapid mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction [13].

Ca2+/MPT death is associated with mitochondrial depolarization, pronounced matrix swelling, outer-membrane failure, cellular swelling, and necrotic membrane rupture [14-17].

Therefore, morphology alone cannot establish Metalloptosis or determine its subtype.

Metal-specific causal evidence is required.

9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

The major challenge in Metalloptosis is distinguishing true mechanistic convergence from simple overlap in metal biology, ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, or terminal lysis.

Copper provides a clear example.

Copper can promote cuproptosis when it accumulates in mitochondria and targets lipoylated proteins.

However, copper can also promote ferroptosis by affecting GPX4. Copper binding to GPX4 has been associated with GPX4 ubiquitination, aggregation, TAX1BP1-dependent autophagic degradation, and increased ferroptotic susceptibility [42].

In that context, copper is an upstream regulator of ferroptosis rather than evidence that ferroptosis and cuproptosis are the same pathway.

Conversely, ferroptosis-inducing conditions can enhance cuproptosis, including through changes in glutathione-dependent copper buffering and increased stress on lipoylated mitochondrial proteins [43].

Again, this represents crosstalk rather than a shared executor.

Zinc provides another example.

Zn2+ toxicity can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, autophagy-associated death, apoptosis, or ferroptosis depending on the biological context [21-24,44].

Only when lysosomal Zn2+ mobilization and the associated lysosome–mitochondria energetic-collapse mechanism are experimentally demonstrated should a narrow lysozincrosis classification be used.

Calcium is even more broadly involved across RCD mechanisms. Ca2+ elevation can occur during apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, and other stress responses.

Therefore, Ca2+ elevation alone does not define the Ca2+/MPT metalloptotic route. Persistent mitochondrial permeability transition and causal dependence on the MPT machinery must be demonstrated [14-17].

Disulfidptosis should also remain mechanistically distinct from Metalloptosis in the present framework. Its supplied literature defines death through disulfide stress and actin-cytoskeleton collapse rather than a metal-ion-specific lethal chemistry [45-48].

Thus, shared oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, GSH metabolism, or cellular lysis does not by itself justify grouping pathways as one execution mechanism.

10. Experimental Validation

Metalloptosis cannot be validated experimentally with one universal biomarker.

The appropriate standard is:

demonstrate metal dependence + identify the metal-specific causal lesion + show rescue through perturbation of the corresponding route.

Lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidants protect against ferroptosis by inhibiting lipid oxidation, supporting a lipid-peroxidation-dependent ferroptosis-associated cell-death process [51].

Additional causal evidence can include perturbation of GPX4, SLC7A11, ACSL4, LPCAT3, NCOA4, FSP1, DHODH, GCH1, or relevant membrane-repair pathways.

Importantly, iron accumulation or ROS production alone is insufficient to diagnose ferroptosis.

For cuproptosis, strong evidence requires copper dependence together with mitochondrial respiration dependence, FDX1-associated susceptibility, aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins, and Fe–S-protein loss [9-12].

Copper chelation should reduce the phenotype.

Suppression of mitochondrial respiration can provide additional mechanistic discrimination because cuproptosis is closely linked to respiration-dependent mitochondrial metabolism.

Excessive activation of lysosomal ML1/TRPML1 triggers lysosomal Zn2+ release, mitochondrial swelling/dysfunction, ATP depletion, and rapid nonapoptotic necrotic death in metastatic melanoma cells, termed lysozincrosis .

For Ca2+/MPT death, validation should demonstrate mitochondrial Ca2+ loading, persistent permeability transition, mitochondrial depolarization and swelling, ATP collapse, and functional rescue by reducing MPT susceptibility [14-17].

Therefore, merely exposing cells to a metal and observing death is not sufficient evidence for Metalloptosis.

Likewise, a transcriptional signature cannot establish active Metalloptosis.

Expression of iron-handling genes, GPX4/SLC7A11, copper transport genes, FDX1, MCOLN1/TRPML1, zinc transporters, Ca2+-handling genes, or mitochondrial-stress genes can indicate a metal-homeostasis-associated state or susceptibility state.

It cannot establish the execution of ferroptosis, cuproptosis, lysozincrosis, or MPT-driven necrosis without direct biochemical or functional evidence.

11. Molecular Hierarchy

Family-level initiators:

metal overload, metal deprivation when it secondarily disrupts homeostasis, abnormal metal transport, altered metal storage, metal-directed pharmacological agents, oxidative stress, metabolic stress, ionophore exposure, and organelle-specific metal redistribution.

Ferroptosis initiators:

system xc− inhibition, cystine restriction, GSH depletion, GPX4 inhibition, iron accumulation, ferritinophagy, increased oxidizable PUFA-phospholipid availability.

Ferroptosis core machinery:

SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis, labile iron, ACSL4, LPCAT3, PUFA-containing phospholipids.

Ferroptosis execution chemistry:

self-propagating phospholipid peroxidation → membrane permeability → osmotic failure → rupture.

Ferroptosis protective factors:

GPX4, FSP1–CoQ10, DHODH–CoQ10, GCH1–BH4, NRF2-associated defenses, iron buffering, ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair.

Cuproptosis initiators:

mitochondrial copper accumulation, copper ionophores, copper-transport states favoring mitochondrial Cu availability.

Cuproptosis core machinery:

FDX1-associated copper chemistry, lipoylated TCA-cycle proteins including DLAT, Fe–S proteins.

Cuproptosis execution:

protein aggregation + Fe–S-protein destabilization → mitochondrial proteotoxic/metabolic collapse.

Cuproptosis protective factors:

GSH-mediated copper buffering, NRF2-associated GSH metabolism, SLC25A39-dependent mitochondrial GSH availability.

Lysozincrosis initiator:

TRPML1/MCOLN1-dependent lysosomal Zn2+ release.

Lysozincrosis core machinery:

lysosomal Zn2+ pool → pathological Zn2+ redistribution → mitochondria.

Lysozincrosis execution:

mitochondrial dysfunction → ATP depletion → necrotic homeostatic collapse.

Ca2+/MPT initiators:

cytosolic/mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and ROS.

Ca2+/MPT core machinery:

mitochondrial Ca2+ handling + mPTP.

Ca2+/MPT execution:

persistent MPT → Δψm loss → OXPHOS failure → ATP depletion → mitochondrial/cellular swelling → lysis.

Family-level executor:

none.

Family-level biomarker:

none.

12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

Metalloptosis is best represented as a branching rather than linear pathway.

General entry:

metal dyshomeostasis or metal-directed stress → failure of physiological metal buffering, transport, storage, or compartmentalization → metal-specific intracellular lesion.

Iron branch:

iron-dependent redox imbalance + GPX4-defense failure → ACSL4/LPCAT3-supported PUFA-phospholipid susceptibility → phospholipid hydroperoxide accumulation → self-propagating lipid oxidation → membrane permeability → Ca2+/ion flux → osmotic swelling → plasma-membrane rupture → ferroptosis.

Copper branch:

mitochondrial copper accumulation → FDX1-associated copper chemistry → copper binding to lipoylated TCA-cycle proteins → abnormal protein aggregation + Fe–S-protein destabilization → mitochondrial proteotoxic stress → metabolic collapse → cuproptosis.

Zinc branch:

pathological lysosomal Zn2+ mobilization → TRPML1-mediated Zn2+ release → mitochondrial Zn2+-associated injury → mitochondrial swelling/dysfunction → ATP depletion → homeostatic failure → necrotic death/lysozincrosis.

Calcium branch:

cytosolic Ca2+ elevation → mitochondrial Ca2+ overload + ROS → mPTP sensitization → persistent high-conductance MPT → Δψm collapse → oxidative-phosphorylation arrest → ATP depletion → matrix and cellular swelling → membrane failure → MPT-driven necrosis.

Shared amplifiers can include ROS, glutathione depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, altered autophagy, failed organelle repair, and metabolic stress.

However:

shared amplifier ≠ shared execution mechanism.

The integrated family model is therefore:

metal dyshomeostasis → compartment-specific accumulation or redistribution → metal-specific biochemical lesion → route-specific amplification → route-specific commitment → route-specific physical execution → cell death.

13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, Metalloptosis should not be classified as a single independent regulated-cell-death modality with one core molecular pathway.

The supplied evidence supports a more conservative and biologically coherent interpretation: Metalloptosis is an emerging umbrella concept or proposed superfamily grouping mechanistically distinct regulated deaths whose initiating logic depends on metal-ion homeostasis.

Its strongest established members are ferroptosis and cuproptosis.

Ferroptosis is defined by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation.

Cuproptosis is defined by copper-dependent mitochondrial lipoylated-protein aggregation and Fe–S-protein destabilization.

Lysozincrosis provides a zinc-centered lysosome-to-mitochondria death mechanism, but the named entity remains less standardized.

Ca2+/MPT-driven necrosis provides a well-established mitochondrial permeability-transition death mechanism, although its inclusion within the broader “metalloptosis” terminology reflects the emerging superfamily concept rather than a reclassification of MPT-driven necrosis itself.

The most important mechanistic principle is therefore that the metal defines the upstream vulnerability, but the physical executor is route-specific.

There is no universal metalloptosis executor.

There is no universal metalloptosis point of no return.

There is no universal metalloptosis morphology.

There is no universal metalloptosis biomarker.

Consequently, an experimental study should not conclude “metalloptosis” solely from altered Fe, Cu, Zn, Ca2+, ROS, mitochondrial dysfunction, or metal-responsive gene expression.

The specific branch must be demonstrated.

The principal unresolved question is conceptual rather than merely molecular: whether Metalloptosis will mature into a standardized RCD superfamily with agreed inclusion criteria and subtype definitions, or remain a useful descriptive umbrella for several independently defined metal-dependent death mechanisms.

For RCDome, the safest operational annotation is therefore:

classification: emerging umbrella concept / proposed metal-dependent RCD superfamily;

core shared principle: lethal metal-ion dyshomeostasis;

universal core machinery: none;

universal executor: none;

terminal cause: subtype-dependent;

strongest established components: ferroptosis and cuproptosis;

additional included/emerging routes: lysozincrosis and Ca2+/MPT-associated death;

transcriptomic interpretation: metalloptosis-associated metal-homeostasis or susceptibility state, never direct evidence of active Metalloptosis.
