## Alkaliptosis — Mechanistic Pathway

Alkaliptosis is an emerging form of regulated cell death characterized by a pathological and sustained increase in intracellular pH (pHi) that ultimately results in necrosis-like, lytic cellular destruction. The phenomenon was initially defined through the study of JTC801, a small molecule originally characterized pharmacologically as an antagonist of the nociceptin receptor OPRL1, which was found to selectively kill several cancer cell types through a pH-dependent mechanism [1–3]. Importantly, the cytotoxic activity of JTC801 does not depend on OPRL1, indicating that OPRL1 inhibition itself is not the molecular trigger of alkaliptosis [1–3]. Instead, current evidence supports a broader model in which JTC801 disrupts the intracellular distribution and buffering of protons through at least two interconnected signaling axes: the IKK–NF-κB–CA9 pathway and the ATP6V0D1–STAT3 lysosomal pathway. These mechanisms converge on progressive cytosolic alkalinization and disruption of the normal pH gradient between the cytosol and acidic intracellular organelles, particularly lysosomes [3,4].

Under normal conditions, cancer cells maintain a distinctive pH architecture consisting of a relatively alkaline cytosol combined with an acidic extracellular microenvironment. This inverted proton gradient supports glycolytic metabolism, proliferation, resistance to cell death, and adaptation to hypoxic and metabolically stressful environments. Multiple proton transporters, carbonic anhydrases, and intracellular organelles cooperate to prevent intracellular pH from leaving the range compatible with cell survival. Alkaliptosis occurs when these homeostatic mechanisms are pushed beyond their compensatory capacity, producing an excessive intracellular alkaline shift that becomes incompatible with cellular integrity [1,3,13]. Thus, alkaliptosis should not be understood simply as exposure to an alkaline extracellular environment, but rather as a regulated process in which intracellular and compartmental proton homeostasis is actively disrupted.

One of the best-characterized upstream events following JTC801 exposure is activation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway. JTC801 promotes activation of the IκB kinase complex, including IKKβ, leading to phosphorylation and subsequent degradation of IκBα. Removal of the inhibitory IκB proteins permits the NF-κB transcriptional machinery, particularly the RELA/p65-containing complex together with NFKB1/p50, to accumulate in the nucleus [1–3]. In the context of alkaliptosis, this NF-κB activation does not function predominantly as the classical prosurvival inflammatory response observed in many other settings. Instead, it establishes a transcriptional state that promotes pH-dependent cell death.

A critical downstream consequence of this response is NF-κB-dependent suppression of carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) [1–3]. CA9 is an important regulator of tumor acid-base homeostasis. By catalyzing the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide into bicarbonate and protons, CA9 participates in the recycling of bicarbonate for intracellular buffering while facilitating extracellular acidification. This mechanism enables tumor cells to sustain a relatively alkaline intracellular environment without allowing pHi to rise uncontrollably. During JTC801-induced alkaliptosis, suppression of CA9 removes an important component of this buffering system. Experimentally, loss or knockdown of CA9 increases sensitivity to JTC801 and can reproduce important aspects of the alkaliptotic phenotype, whereas preservation of CA9 activity opposes lethal intracellular alkalinization [1,3]. CA9 therefore acts as a negative regulator of alkaliptosis, positioned downstream of NF-κB activation and upstream of the decisive pH alteration.

The NF-κB–CA9 branch is further reinforced by metabolic regulation through acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2). ACSS2 converts acetate into acetyl-CoA and thereby connects acetate metabolism with transcriptional and epigenetic regulation. In pancreatic cancer cells undergoing alkaliptosis, ACSS2 promotes NF-κB activity by increasing the availability of acetyl-CoA required for protein and histone acetylation [6]. Increased acetylation reinforces NF-κB-dependent transcriptional signaling and facilitates the suppression of CA9, amplifying intracellular alkalinization. Consistent with this mechanism, disruption of ACSS2 reduces JTC801-induced alkaliptosis, whereas conditions that favor acetylation can strengthen the response [6]. ACSS2 is therefore best regarded as a metabolic amplifier of the canonical NF-κB–CA9 alkaliptotic pathway, rather than an independent executioner of cell death.

In parallel with the transcriptional NF-κB–CA9 pathway, JTC801 engages a second mechanistic axis centered on the lysosomal V-type proton ATPase component ATP6V0D1 [4]. V-ATPases are multisubunit proton pumps responsible for acidifying intracellular organelles, including lysosomes, by transferring protons from the cytosol into the organellar lumen. ATP6V0D1 is a component of the membrane-associated V0 domain of this machinery and has emerged as a direct molecular target of JTC801. Biochemical and functional experiments indicate that JTC801 interacts with ATP6V0D1, increases its stability, and promotes its association with STAT3 [3,4].

STAT3 has an important non-transcriptional role in intracellular pH regulation because it can physically associate with V-ATPase and contribute to coordination between cytosolic and lysosomal pH [5]. In cancer cells, this system helps maintain an alkaline but physiologically tolerated cytosol while preserving the strongly acidic lumen required for lysosomal function. JTC801-induced engagement of ATP6V0D1 alters the ATP6V0D1–STAT3 interaction and disrupts normal STAT3-mediated lysosomal pH homeostasis [3–5]. The resulting redistribution of protons strengthens the pH difference between the cytosol and lysosomal compartments: proton sequestration into lysosomal compartments is favored while the cytosol becomes progressively more alkaline. Consequently, the ATP6V0D1–STAT3 axis provides a mechanistic connection between JTC801 exposure, altered lysosomal proton handling, and the defining increase in intracellular pH.

The two major branches therefore converge functionally. The NF-κB–CA9 pathway reduces the capacity of the cell to buffer intracellular pH, whereas the ATP6V0D1–STAT3 pathway modifies the subcellular distribution of protons and lysosomal pH homeostasis [1,3,4]. Their combined activity produces sustained cytosolic alkalinization together with abnormal lysosome–cytosol pH disequilibrium. This compartmental interpretation is important because current models of alkaliptosis have expanded beyond the original concept of a simple increase in bulk cytosolic pH. Alkaliptosis is increasingly considered a failure of the entire intracellular proton-distribution network in which the cytosol, lysosomes, Golgi apparatus, extracellular space, bicarbonate-buffering systems, and membrane proton transporters participate [3,13].

The causal importance of intracellular alkalinization is supported by rescue experiments. JTC801 produces a dose- and time-dependent elevation of pHi, measurable with fluorescent pH-sensitive probes and genetically encoded reporters such as RpHluorin2 [1,11,12]. Importantly, interventions that reverse or attenuate intracellular alkalinization markedly suppress cell death. Acidification of the extracellular medium and treatment with compounds capable of modifying the alkalinizing response, including N-acetylcysteine and N-acetyl alanine in the original experimental systems, protect cells against JTC801-induced death [1–3,14]. These experiments establish the increase in intracellular pH as a causal biochemical requirement, rather than a secondary consequence of dying cells.

Several additional pathways can modify the threshold for alkaliptotic death without constituting the core execution machinery. One such resistance mechanism involves the ubiquitin ligase ITCH, the transcriptional regulator YAP1, and the monocarboxylate transporter SLC16A1/MCT1. ITCH promotes YAP1-dependent expression of SLC16A1, which facilitates proton-linked monocarboxylate transport and contributes to maintenance of intracellular acid-base homeostasis. Activation of the ITCH–YAP1–SLC16A1 axis therefore opposes JTC801-induced alkaliptosis, whereas disruption of this pathway increases alkaliptotic sensitivity [7].

Metabolic nutrient-acquisition pathways can also provide protection. Macropinocytosis, particularly through its contribution to fatty-acid uptake, suppresses alkaliptosis in pancreatic cancer cells by helping maintain metabolic and lysosomal homeostasis [8]. Pharmacological inhibition of macropinocytosis or disruption of associated regulators such as RAC1 enhances JTC801-induced cell death. These findings indicate that cellular metabolic plasticity can compensate for the proton-homeostasis stress produced during alkaliptosis.

A further resistance mechanism involves the CYP51A1–SREBF2–TMEM175 axis [9]. CYP51A1-dependent sterol metabolism contributes to lysosomal cholesterol homeostasis, while TMEM175 functions as a lysosomal ion channel capable of participating in proton and ion homeostasis. Disruption of CYP51A1 results in lysosomal cholesterol accumulation and impairs compensatory lysosomal proton handling, thereby increasing sensitivity to pH-dependent cell death. Conversely, intact CYP51A1/SREBF2/TMEM175 signaling provides a mechanism through which cancer cells can partially tolerate the lysosomal stress associated with JTC801 treatment [9]. Together, the ITCH–YAP1–SLC16A1 pathway, macropinocytosis, and CYP51A1-dependent lysosomal regulation illustrate that resistance to alkaliptosis can emerge at several levels of the cellular pH-control network.

Other organelles can additionally function as proton reservoirs. The Golgi apparatus, for example, can act as an intracellular “proton sink.” NHE7-dependent proton transport contributes to Golgi acidification and cytosolic pH regulation, and perturbation of this mechanism alters the distribution of protons between the cytosol and Golgi lumen [23,24]. Although NHE7 has not been established as a core component of canonical JTC801-induced alkaliptosis, these observations provide an important conceptual framework for understanding how disruption of organellar proton sequestration can influence the threshold at which intracellular alkalinity becomes toxic.

The transition from reversible pH disturbance to irreversible cell death is less completely understood. Before catastrophic cellular failure, alkaliptosis can still be prevented by interventions that restore pH homeostasis or interrupt upstream NF-κB, CA9, ACSS2, ATP6V0D1, or STAT3-associated signaling [1,3,4,6]. However, once sustained cytosolic alkalinization and compartmental pH disequilibrium exceed the adaptive capacity of the cell, widespread metabolic and structural dysfunction develops. The exact quantitative pHi threshold and the precise molecular event constituting the point of no return have not yet been established [3,12,13].

Morphologically, advanced alkaliptosis displays features of regulated necrotic or necrosis-like cell death, including cellular swelling, progressive organelle dysfunction, loss of plasma-membrane integrity, release of cytosolic components, and eventual cellular lysis [1,14–16]. Importantly, however, no dedicated terminal alkaliptotic executioner comparable to the caspases of apoptosis, MLKL of necroptosis, or gasdermins of pyroptosis has been identified. The molecular mechanism physically responsible for the final rupture of the plasma membrane therefore remains one of the major unresolved questions in alkaliptosis research [3,12–14].

The membrane protein NINJ1 provides an interesting possible connection to this late phase because NINJ1 can mediate plasma-membrane rupture downstream of cellular swelling in several forms of regulated necrosis [20]. Nevertheless, a direct requirement for NINJ1 in canonical JTC801-induced alkaliptosis has not been established. It should therefore be considered a candidate mechanism for late membrane permeabilization, not a validated alkaliptotic executor.

Mitochondrial dysfunction should likewise be distinguished from the canonical mechanism. The original JTC801 studies did not identify oxidative stress or a classical mitochondrial death pathway as essential for alkaliptosis [1,14]. In contrast, experimentally induced cellular alkalinization in other models, particularly bicarbonate-treated hepatocellular carcinoma cells, can disrupt mitochondrial proton motive force, increase AMP, activate AMPK, impair lysosomal function and autophagic flux, and eventually produce cell death [21]. These observations demonstrate that severe alkalinization can affect mitochondria and autophagy but do not establish mitochondrial collapse as the canonical execution mechanism of JTC801-induced alkaliptosis.

Alkaliptosis is also experimentally distinguished from other major forms of regulated cell death by extensive negative-rescue and genetic-exclusion experiments. JTC801-induced death is not prevented by classical inhibition of caspase-dependent apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, or autophagy-dependent cell death. Similarly, disruption of major components such as BAX/BAK, RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL, GPX4, or essential autophagy-related genes does not abolish the alkaliptotic phenotype in the original systems [1–3]. In contrast, preventing intracellular alkalinization or interfering with the NF-κB–CA9 or ATP6V0D1-associated pH-control pathways markedly alters cell survival. Thus, the defining mechanistic criterion for alkaliptosis is not simply necrotic morphology but a causal dependence on dysregulated intracellular alkalinization.

Intracellular alkalinization can nevertheless interact with other regulated cell-death pathways. For example, alterations in proton transport can activate RIPK3–MLKL-dependent necroptosis under particular osmotic or metabolic conditions, and experimentally imposed intracellular alkalinization can reprogram TNF-α signaling away from NF-κB-mediated inflammatory or survival responses toward RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptosis [22]. Such observations demonstrate that intracellular pH can act as an upstream determinant of cell-death pathway selection. However, these processes should be classified as pH-driven pathway switching or crosstalk, rather than as evidence that MLKL-mediated necroptosis forms part of the alkaliptosis execution machinery.

Following loss of plasma-membrane integrity, alkaliptotic cells can generate inflammatory signals. One of the best-characterized consequences is the release of the damage-associated molecular pattern HMGB1 [10]. Extracellular HMGB1 can interact with the receptor AGER/RAGE on macrophages and stimulate STING1-dependent inflammatory signaling, resulting in cytokine production and sterile inflammation. FANCD2 can restrain this response, linking DNA-damage regulation with the magnitude of HMGB1 release [10]. This HMGB1–AGER–STING1 pathway represents a downstream immunological consequence of alkaliptotic cell lysis, rather than the mechanism responsible for initiating alkaliptosis itself.

The biological relevance of the pathway extends beyond the pancreatic cancer models in which much of the mechanistic work was performed. NF-κB-dependent alkaliptosis has been exploited experimentally in venetoclax-resistant acute myeloid leukemia, supporting the ability of pH-dependent death to overcome resistance to conventional apoptosis-based therapy [17]. JTC801-induced alkaliptosis has also been investigated in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer, where ATP6V0D1-dependent mechanisms can influence ABCB1-associated drug resistance [18]. Additional studies have associated modulation of JTC801 sensitivity with kidney renal clear-cell carcinoma models [19]. These observations suggest that the vulnerability represented by excessive intracellular alkalinization is not restricted to one tumor lineage, although the detailed molecular requirements and relative importance of the different regulatory branches may vary according to cancer type.

Overall, the current mechanistic model can be summarized as follows: JTC801 exposure → OPRL1-independent signaling and direct engagement of ATP6V0D1 → activation of the IKK–NF-κB pathway in parallel with ATP6V0D1–STAT3 lysosomal signaling → IκBα degradation and NF-κB nuclear activity together with disruption of STAT3-mediated lysosomal pH regulation → NF-κB-dependent suppression of CA9 and altered lysosome–cytosol proton partitioning → progressive loss of intracellular buffering capacity and sustained cytosolic alkalinization → compartmental pH disequilibrium, metabolic and organellar dysfunction, cellular swelling, and loss of plasma-membrane integrity → release of HMGB1 and other damage-associated molecules → irreversible lytic cell death [1,3,4,10,13]. The identity of a dedicated molecular executor responsible for the final membrane-rupture step remains unknown. Accordingly, sustained pathological intracellular alkalinization and disruption of compartmental proton homeostasis, rather than activation of a known pore-forming death protein, currently constitute the defining mechanistic features of alkaliptosis.
