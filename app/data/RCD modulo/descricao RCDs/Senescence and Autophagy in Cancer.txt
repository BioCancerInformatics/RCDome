# Senescence and Autophagy in Cancer

## 1. Definition and Classification

Senescence and autophagy in cancer should not be classified as a single regulated cell death modality. Rather, it represents an RCD-related concept describing the context-dependent interaction between two biologically distinct processes: cellular senescence, an established stress-response state characterized by durable proliferative arrest, and autophagy, an established lysosome-dependent degradation and recycling pathway [1-4].

Cellular senescence is defined primarily by stable withdrawal from the cell cycle despite the persistence of mitogenic signals. This arrest is commonly enforced through p53–p21 and/or p16–RB-associated circuitry together with extensive transcriptional, metabolic, chromatin, and secretory remodeling [1,5-7]. Senescent cells usually remain viable, metabolically active, and capable of prolonged secretion. Therefore, senescence itself is not a death-execution mechanism.

Autophagy is likewise not intrinsically a cell-death program. Canonical macroautophagy normally preserves cellular fitness by sequestering damaged proteins, organelles, and other cytoplasmic material within autophagosomes, delivering this cargo to lysosomes, and recycling the resulting metabolites [2-3,8-9,52].

The relationship between the two processes is bidirectional and stage dependent. Although autophagy was initially thought to counteract cellular senescence by removing damaged macromolecules or organelles, autophagy upregulation was required for the implementation of oncogene-induced senescence [10].

The term autophagic cell death should therefore be reserved for situations in which inhibition or genetic disruption of the autophagy machinery prevents death, rather than situations in which autophagosomes merely accumulate in dying cells [3,11].

For RCDome, Senescence and Autophagy in Cancer is therefore best treated as an RCD-related concept or process interaction, not as an established autonomous RCD modality.

## 2. Initiation and Triggers

There is no universal initiating stimulus for the combined senescence–autophagy state.

Senescence can be initiated by oncogenic hyperproliferation, persistent DNA damage, telomere dysfunction, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, chromosomal instability, and anticancer therapy [1,5,12-14,55].

In oncogene-induced senescence, activated oncogenic RAS can drive excessive DNA replication, replication-fork perturbation, and DNA hyper-replication. The resulting lesions activate the DNA damage response, including ATM/ATR-associated checkpoint signaling, leading to downstream stabilization of p53 and induction of p21 [12-13].

Genotoxic chemotherapy and irradiation similarly activate DNA-damage-responsive signaling. p53-dependent induction of p21 inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases and promotes RB-associated repression of E2F-dependent cell-cycle genes [5].

A parallel senescence-enforcing route operates through p16INK4A or related INK4-family inhibitors, which restrain CDK4/6, maintain RB in a growth-suppressive state, and reinforce proliferative arrest [1,5].

Genomic instability can additionally produce micronuclei and cytoplasmic chromatin fragments. Cytosolic DNA is recognized by cGAS, leading to cGAMP production, STING activation, inflammatory signaling, and reinforcement of senescence-associated phenotypes [15-18,51].

Mitochondrial dysfunction can contribute through ROS generation, altered metabolite production, and mitochondria-to-nucleus signaling that promotes cytoplasmic chromatin formation and inflammatory signaling [19].

Autophagy has a distinct initiation system. Energy or nutrient stress can activate AMPK and relieve mTOR-dependent suppression of the ULK1 complex. ULK1 activation then initiates the autophagy program and promotes Beclin1–VPS34-dependent phagophore nucleation [8,20-21].

ROS or lysosomal stress can instead alter autophagic flux by destabilizing lysosomal membranes or impairing autophagosome degradation [22-23]. In these contexts, an initially protective autophagic response may progressively become dysfunctional or lethal.

Thus, senescence and autophagy have different proximal sensors and initiating events, despite extensive downstream interaction.

## 3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

The central machinery of senescence is a proliferative-arrest network rather than a death-execution complex.

Following p53 activation, p21 expression is upregulated, and high p21 levels cause RB-E2F complex formation and downregulation of many cell cycle genes, leading to cell cycle arrest [5].

The p16–CDK4/6–RB axis provides an additional arrest mechanism. Increased p16 activity restrains CDK4/6, limits RB phosphorylation, suppresses E2F-dependent transcription, and stabilizes exit from the cell cycle [1,24].

DREAM-associated transcriptional repression can further reinforce the suppression of proliferation-associated genes [5,25].

Autophagy uses a distinct molecular architecture. The canonical sequence begins with ULK1–ATG13-associated initiation, followed by activation of Beclin1–VPS34 complexes, generation of the phagophore, ATG conjugation reactions, LC3-associated membrane expansion, autophagosome completion, fusion with lysosomes, and lysosomal degradation of cargo [8-9,20].

ULK1 can phosphorylate Beclin1 and stimulate ATG14-containing VPS34 complexes, directly connecting nutrient and energy sensing to autophagosome nucleation [20].

Beclin1 is also regulated by BCL-2-family proteins. BCL-2 and BCL-XL can bind the BH3-like region of Beclin1 and restrict its autophagic activity, whereas disruption of this interaction can release Beclin1 and facilitate autophagy [26-28,43].

The central mechanistic conclusion is therefore that senescence and autophagy possess separate core machineries that communicate extensively but should not be merged into one linear pathway.

## 4. Propagation and Amplification

Once senescence begins, several mechanisms stabilize and propagate the state.

Persistent DNA-damage signaling reinforces p53–p21-dependent arrest, while p16–RB signaling can provide an additional barrier to cell-cycle re-entry [1,5].

Chromatin architecture also changes extensively. Senescence-associated heterochromatin foci can form in some settings and contribute to repression of proliferation-associated regions, although SAHF are neither universal nor required for all forms of senescence [42,53].

Cytoplasmic chromatin provides another amplification route. cGAS recognizes cytosolic chromatin fragments in senescent cells, and its activation triggers SASP factor production via STING, promoting paracrine senescence [15].

An important amplification program is the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. Senescent cells can secrete cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, proteases, and other extracellular mediators. Mechanisms and functions of cellular senescence involve regulatory networks and heterogeneity of the SASP [47].

Autophagy can support this secretory phenotype rather than eliminate the senescent cell.

A specialized spatial arrangement termed the TOR–autophagy spatial coupling compartment, or TASCC, brings autolysosomal compartments and mTOR-associated signaling into proximity with the Golgi apparatus. Recycling of intracellular material provides amino acids that can sustain mTOR activity and support production of SASP components such as IL-6 and IL-8 [32-34,49].

Thus, autophagy can shift from a damage-limiting homeostatic process before senescence to a metabolic support system for the established senescent phenotype.

## 5. Protective/Negative Regulators

Autophagy frequently acts as a protective mechanism before stable senescence develops.

Removal of dysfunctional mitochondria and damaged cellular components can reduce ROS production, proteotoxic stress, and other signals capable of activating senescence [2,4]. In this context, efficient autophagic flux increases cellular stress tolerance and can delay entry into senescence.

Failure of autophagic or lysosomal function can have the opposite effect. Accumulation of damaged organelles, ROS, and undegraded material can reinforce stress signaling and promote senescence-associated phenotypes [22-23].

The p53–p21 and p16–RB pathways should not be considered negative regulators of senescence; they are central effectors maintaining the arrested state. Conversely, loss or suppression of these pathways can facilitate escape from senescence in appropriate cellular backgrounds [5,35].

Autophagy itself also contains inhibitory checkpoints. mTOR suppresses ULK1-dependent autophagy under nutrient-replete conditions, whereas BCL-2/BCL-XL binding restrains Beclin1-dependent autophagosome nucleation [20,26-28].

At the lysosomal level, preservation of lysosomal membrane integrity prevents uncontrolled release of lysosomal hydrolases. Lysosomal membrane permeabilization through Bax/Bak is absolutely required for regulated autophagic cell death, a mechanism separate from the early micro- and macro-autophagy induced during starvation to maintain cell viability, though lysosomal and autolysosomal expansion during those states likely primes cells for transition to autophagic killing as these vesicular structures permeabilize, lowering intracellular pH and/or releasing cathepsins [11,54].

The protective role of autophagy is consequently highly dependent on the stage and intensity of stress.

## 6. Commitment / Point of No Return

There is no universal point of no return for Senescence and Autophagy in Cancer as a combined entity.

Senescence is commonly described as stable or durable arrest, but this does not mean that every senescent state is absolutely irreversible. Escape has been documented following derepression of hTERT gene expression [35].

Therefore, activation of p21, p16, SA-β-gal, SASP production, or acquisition of senescence-associated morphology cannot individually be regarded as an irreversible molecular commitment point.

A more defensible operational criterion for established senescence is persistent failure to re-enter the cell cycle after removal of the initiating stress, together with evidence for functional engagement of cell-cycle arrest circuitry.

For autophagic cell death, commitment is completely different.

During replicative crisis, evidence supports a point at which sustained autophagy becomes required for elimination of genomically unstable cells. shRNA against ATG3, ATG5, or ATG7 promoted a bypass of the crisis, continued cell proliferation, and increased genome instability [3].

In lysosomal membrane permeabilization-associated death, commitment occurs when lysosomal disruption becomes sufficiently extensive to release destructive lysosomal contents and produce irreversible cellular damage [40].

Thus, no single commitment step can be assigned to the combined senescence–autophagy concept.

## 7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

Senescence itself has no canonical molecular executor because senescence normally does not kill the cell.

The usual terminal outcome of senescence is continued survival in a non-proliferative state, followed potentially by immune-mediated clearance, persistence, therapeutic elimination by senolytic strategies, or eventual escape in selected contexts [1,6,36].

Therefore, p21, p16, RB, SASP factors, SAHF, and cGAS–STING should not be labeled as death executors.

When true autophagy-dependent cell death occurs, the physical cause of death depends on the particular context.

One supported mechanism occurs during replicative crisis. Telomere dysfunction and genomic instability engage an autophagy-dependent elimination program. Suppression of ATG3, ATG5, or ATG7 can permit cells to survive crisis, demonstrating that functional autophagy machinery contributes causally to death [3].

In this setting, the lethal process is best interpreted as sustained destructive autophagic activity leading to irreversible depletion or degradation of cellular components required for survival, rather than activation of a single pore-forming execution protein [3-4].

A second mechanistically supported route involves lysosomal membrane permeabilization. Bax and Bak can contribute to lysosomal membrane permeabilization during autophagic cell death, and lysosome-targeted Bax can restore cell killing in resistant experimental systems [40].

During lysosomal membrane permeabilization, cathepsins are released into the cytosol where they induce caspase-dependent apoptosis or directly lead to necrosis [11].

Accordingly, the proximate terminal lesion in these LMP-associated contexts is destructive lysosomal permeabilization followed by lysosomal failure and protease-associated collapse.

Neither mechanism should be generalized to all senescent cells or all situations in which autophagy is activated.

## 8. Morphological Sequence

Senescence and autophagic death have distinct morphological patterns.

Cells entering senescence commonly become enlarged and flattened and may show expanded lysosomal compartments, increased vacuolization, altered nuclear architecture, and loss of Lamin B1 [4,29-31].

Senescence-associated heterochromatin foci may appear as dense nuclear chromatin domains in some oncogene-induced models, but their formation is strongly dependent on cell type and stimulus and is not universal [29-31].

Senescent cells may also show increased lysosomal mass, contributing to commonly used SA-β-gal-associated phenotypes, but this remains an associated feature rather than a unique mechanistic definition.

These changes occur while the cell remains viable.

Autophagic cell death is morphologically characterized by vacuolization of the cytoplasm, without chromatin condensation as seen in apoptosis or organelle swelling as observed in necrosis [11].

Thus, an enlarged flattened cell with abundant lysosomes is more consistent with a senescent state than with death.

Conversely, extensive autophagic vacuolization in a dying cell does not prove autophagic cell death unless autophagy is causally required for death.

## 9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

The most important differential diagnosis is between senescence and cell death.

Senescent cells are non-proliferative but alive. Loss of proliferation, increased SA-β-gal activity, p16 expression, p21 expression, enlarged morphology, or SASP production should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that the cell is dying [1,4,47-48].

Senescence must also be distinguished from transient quiescence. The key distinction is durability of the proliferative arrest and engagement of senescence-associated molecular remodeling rather than simple temporary withdrawal from the cell cycle.

Autophagy must be distinguished from autophagic cell death. Increased LC3 puncta, LC3-II accumulation, autophagosomes, or expression of ATG genes can reflect either increased autophagic flux or blocked degradation and do not demonstrate that autophagy executes death [3,8,46].

Autophagy and apoptosis intersect extensively through the BCL-2 family. Bax is a pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family member proposed to mediate a mechanism in cell death [11].

Caspase-mediated Beclin1 processing and autophagy-dependent modulation of caspase signaling provide additional points at which apoptotic and autophagic outcomes can influence each other [37-38].

Autophagy can also modify ferroptotic susceptibility. Autophagic degradation of iron-storage systems can increase the labile iron pool and favor lipid peroxidation in appropriate contexts. This represents autophagy-assisted ferroptotic susceptibility rather than evidence that ferroptosis and autophagic cell death are the same mechanism [3].

Therapy-induced senescence provides another important distinction. Anticancer therapy may cause stable arrest instead of immediate death, leaving viable tumor cells that can influence neighboring cells through SASP production [39]. Subsequent senolytic killing of these cells represents a secondary death mechanism and should not be described as execution by senescence itself.

## 10. Experimental Validation

Senescence should be established through convergent functional evidence rather than a single marker.

A particularly important criterion is durable proliferative arrest after removal of the initiating stimulus. This can be tested through long-term proliferation assays, cell-cycle re-entry assays, and clonogenic recovery experiments.

Molecular evidence should establish engagement of relevant arrest circuitry, including p53–p21 and/or p16–RB-associated mechanisms depending on the model [1,5].

Reduced expression of proliferation-associated genes provides additional support.

SA-β-gal activity, Lamin B1 loss, enlarged flattened morphology, SAHF, lysosomal expansion, p16 expression, p21 expression, and individual SASP components can support the classification but none should be used alone to diagnose senescence [1,4,29-31].

Autophagic activity requires flux measurements rather than static measurement of autophagosome abundance. LC3-II and p62/SQSTM1 behavior should be examined under conditions that distinguish increased autophagosome formation from impaired lysosomal degradation [3,8,44].

True autophagic cell death requires causal evidence that the autophagy machinery is necessary for death.

Strong evidence includes rescue of viability after genetic disruption of core ATG proteins or relevant upstream autophagy machinery such as ULK1, Beclin1, or VPS34 [3,21,48].

Knockdown of ATG3, ATG5, or ATG7 promotes bypass of the crisis, continued cell proliferation, and increased genome instability [3].

Transcriptomic data can support identification of senescence-associated or autophagy-associated cellular states but cannot demonstrate active autophagic cell death. Expression of CDKN1A, CDKN2A, ATG genes, BECN1, ULK1, lysosomal genes, or SASP-associated transcripts does not establish execution.

## 11. Initiating stresses for senescence include oncogenic hyper-replication, persistent DNA damage, telomere dysfunction, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, chromosomal instability, and therapy-associated stress [41].

Sensors/transducers = ATM/ATR-associated DDR machinery, checkpoint kinases, cGAS–STING signaling in response to cytosolic DNA, and stress-responsive signaling networks [12-18].

Core state-establishing machinery = p53–p21–CDK–RB and p16–CDK4/6–RB-associated pathways, together with transcriptional and chromatin remodeling [1,5].

Output = durable proliferative arrest.

NF-κB and C/EBP-β are positive regulators of SASP factor expression [45].

Death executor = none intrinsic to senescence.

For autophagy:

Initiators = nutrient limitation, energy stress, damaged organelles, proteotoxic stress, and related cellular stressors.

Core machinery = ULK1–ATG13-associated initiation → Beclin1–VPS34-dependent nucleation → ATG conjugation and LC3-associated expansion → autophagosome formation → autophagosome–lysosome fusion → lysosomal degradation [8-9,20].

Negative regulators = mTOR-dependent suppression of ULK1 and BCL-2/BCL-XL-dependent restraint of Beclin1 in appropriate contexts [20,26-28].

Normal outcome = adaptation, quality control, and survival.

Autophagy activation is required for cell death and the elimination of precancerous cells during replicative crisis caused by telomere dysfunction [3].

During autophagic cell death, monomeric Bax and Bak localize to lysosomes and mediate an increase in lysosomal permeability without being overtly activated [11].

No universal executor exists across all conditions described as senescence-associated or autophagy-associated death.

## 12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

Because Senescence and Autophagy in Cancer is not a single death pathway, the most defensible integrated sequence is fate branched rather than linear.

Cancer-associated stress such as oncogenic hyper-replication, persistent DNA damage, telomere dysfunction, ROS, mitochondrial dysfunction, chromosomal instability, or anticancer therapy → activation of DDR signaling and/or sensing of extranuclear DNA → p53–p21 and/or p16–RB-associated cell-cycle suppression → transcriptional and chromatin remodeling → establishment of a durable senescence-associated state.

In parallel:

cellular stress and altered nutrient/energy signaling → AMPK/mTOR-dependent regulation of ULK1 → Beclin1–VPS34 activation → ATG-dependent autophagosome formation → autophagosome–lysosome fusion → cargo degradation and recycling.

From this point, at least three major outcomes are supported.

Adaptive route:

effective autophagic quality control → removal of damaged organelles and macromolecules → reduced ROS and cellular damage → delayed or attenuated senescence.

Senescence-supporting route:

stable proliferative arrest → autophagy rewiring and TASCC-associated recycling → provision of metabolites supporting SASP synthesis → persistent viable senescent state → subsequent immune clearance, persistence, therapeutic senolysis, or occasional escape.

Lethal route:

severe telomeric/genomic crisis or destructive autophagy–lysosome dysfunction → requirement for ATG machinery and/or extensive lysosomal membrane permeabilization → depletion of survival-essential cellular material or cytosolic release of lysosomal hydrolases → irreversible loss of cellular homeostasis → death.

The crucial mechanistic branch point is therefore not “autophagy activation” itself but whether autophagy remains protective/supportive or becomes causally required for cellular destruction.

## 13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, Senescence and Autophagy in Cancer should not be represented as an established RCD modality with a canonical trigger-to-executor sequence.

Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest that can be induced by a variety of potentially oncogenic stimuli, including DNA damage [6,50].

Autophagy is principally an established homeostatic degradation pathway in which ULK1-, Beclin1/VPS34-, ATG-, autophagosome-, and lysosome-dependent machinery recycles cellular material [8-9,20].

Their relationship is strongly context dependent.

Before senescence, efficient autophagy can reduce mitochondrial and oxidative damage and thereby delay entry into senescence. After senescence is established, autophagy can support survival and the energetic demands of the SASP. Under other conditions, inhibition or collapse of autophagic–lysosomal function can reinforce senescence [2,4,22].

True death should only be invoked when causal evidence demonstrates execution.

Cell death can be classified mechanistically, distinguishing essential mechanisms from correlative markers [48].

In the first, the autophagy machinery itself becomes required for elimination of unstable cells. In the second, lysosomal membrane permeabilization and release of lysosomal enzymes produce irreversible cellular damage.

Therefore, there is no universal death executor, point of no return, or terminal mechanism for “senescence and autophagy” as a combined entity.

The major conceptual shift is recognition that autophagy is fate-divergent and stage dependent: it can prevent senescence, sustain established senescent cells, or, in restricted contexts, become part of a lethal mechanism.

The principal unresolved question is where the transition occurs between protective or senescence-supporting autophagy and genuinely executional autophagy–lysosomal death in cancer cells.

For transcriptomic applications, RCDome should describe high senescence- or autophagy-associated expression as a senescence-associated state, autophagy-associated state, or senescence–autophagy-associated transcriptional program. Such signatures cannot demonstrate active autophagic cell death or prove that senescent cells are dying.
