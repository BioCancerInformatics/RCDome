Mitochondrial permeability transition

1. Definition and Classification

Mitochondrial permeability transition (MPT) is an established mitochondrial permeability process in which the inner mitochondrial membrane undergoes a sudden increase in permeability to small solutes through activation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). When pore opening is sustained and reaches a high-conductance state, MPT causes rapid dissipation of the mitochondrial membrane potential, osmotic matrix swelling, failure of oxidative phosphorylation, ATP depletion, loss of cellular ion homeostasis, and predominantly necrotic cell death [1-6].

An important mechanistic distinction must be maintained between MPT itself and MPT-driven necrosis.

MPT describes the mitochondrial permeability event.

MPT-driven necrosis describes the lethal regulated-cell-death outcome produced when this permeability transition becomes sustained, sufficiently extensive, and bioenergetically irreversible.

This distinction is necessary because brief or low-conductance mPTP openings can be reversible and may participate in physiological Ca2+ and redox signaling without committing the cell to death [1,7-8].

Accordingly:

transient/low-conductance MPT → potentially reversible mitochondrial signaling;

sustained/high-conductance MPT → mitochondrial energetic collapse → regulated necrotic death.

The defining lesion occurs at the inner mitochondrial membrane and is therefore mechanistically distinct from BAX/BAK-mediated mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilization during intrinsic apoptosis [31].

It is also distinct from ferroptosis, whose lethal chemistry is phospholipid peroxidation; necroptosis, whose canonical executor is MLKL; and pyroptosis, whose terminal membrane permeabilization depends on gasdermins [10-13].

For RCDome, because MPT-driven necrosis is already represented as a separate entity, the most internally coherent annotation is to treat Mitochondrial permeability transition primarily as the mechanistic mitochondrial process that can be reversible or lethal, while MPT-driven necrosis represents its sustained death-execution state.

2. Initiation and Triggers

The best-established triggers of pathological MPT are mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and oxidative stress [1-4].

The canonical sequence is:

severe cellular stress → cytosolic and/or ER Ca2+ mobilization → mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake → matrix Ca2+ accumulation + ROS → mPTP sensitization → pore opening.

The ER–mitochondria interface is an important upstream signaling platform.

At mitochondria-associated membrane contact sites, IP3R-mediated ER Ca2+ release can be functionally coupled through GRP75 and VDAC1 to mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake pathways, including MCU [14-15,32].

Thus:

ER Ca2+ release → IP3R–GRP75–VDAC1 signaling → mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake → matrix Ca2+ elevation → increased probability of MPT.

This does not mean that the IP3R–GRP75–VDAC1–MCU axis is universally required. Rather, it is one major route by which diverse cellular signals are translated into mitochondrial Ca2+ loading.

Pathological triggers include ischemia–reperfusion, hypoxia, toxic injury, oxidative stress, DNA damage, nutrient excess, inflammatory stress, and other conditions that impair oxidative phosphorylation or destabilize cellular ion homeostasis [3,5,16].

Ischemia–reperfusion is among the best-established pathological contexts.

During ischemia:

reduced oxygen → impaired oxidative phosphorylation → ATP depletion → ion-pump failure → cytosolic Ca2+ disturbance + acidosis + mitochondrial metabolic stress.

During reperfusion:

oxygen restoration → rapid ROS generation + pH normalization + persistent Ca2+ stress → increased susceptibility to sustained mPTP opening.

Low intracellular pH during ischemia can suppress pore opening, while normalization of pH after reperfusion removes this inhibitory restraint [1,5,17].

ROS acts both as an upstream trigger and as an amplifier.

Oxidative modification of mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and redox-sensitive regulatory systems can lower the threshold for pore opening [1,7].

Therefore, MPT is not triggered by one conventional receptor. The mPTP behaves more like a mitochondrial stress-integration system that responds to the combined state of Ca2+, redox balance, pH, phosphate, adenine nucleotides, membrane potential, and mitochondrial architecture.

3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

The defining biochemical event is increased permeability of the inner mitochondrial membrane through a high-conductance, poorly selective permeability pathway [1,18].

The pore can permit passage of solutes of approximately ≤1.5 kDa, collapsing the normal ionic and proton gradients maintained across the inner mitochondrial membrane [1,18].

The central sequence is:

mPTP opening → proton-gradient dissipation → Δψm loss → oxidative-phosphorylation failure.

Cyclophilin D, encoded by PPIF, is the best-established molecular regulator of MPT [2,19-20].

Cyclophilin D is located in the mitochondrial matrix and facilitates pore opening under Ca2+, oxidative, and metabolic stress.

However:

Cyclophilin D is not established as the pore-forming structure itself.

Its role is regulatory.

Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of Cyclophilin D increases the threshold required for pore opening and protects against several forms of necrotic tissue injury [2,19-20].

The structural identity of the mPTP remains unresolved.

Two major structural concepts dominate the literature.

One involves conformational states of F1FO-ATP synthase [1,21,34].

Another assigns an important role to adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT) proteins [22-23].

Evidence supporting ATP-synthase-associated pore formation is substantial, but a single universal ATP-synthase-derived pore has not been conclusively demonstrated.

Similarly, ANT clearly regulates and can contribute to permeability-transition behavior, but it does not fully account for all mPTP activity [22-23].

The most defensible current interpretation is therefore that more than one high-conductance permeability pathway may exist, or that different protein assemblies contribute depending on tissue, species, metabolic state, or inducing stimulus [1,18,21-23].

Thus:

MPT = established phenomenon;

mPTP high-conductance activity = established;

Cyclophilin D regulation = strongly established;

single universal molecular pore structure = unresolved.

4. Propagation and Amplification

Once a high-conductance pore opens, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential occurs rapidly.

Δψm collapse immediately compromises oxidative phosphorylation because the proton-motive force required for ATP synthesis is lost.

Thus:

mPTP opening → Δψm loss → ATP-synthesis arrest.

ATP synthase may then operate in reverse, hydrolyzing ATP in an attempt to sustain mitochondrial membrane potential [24].

This behavior accelerates rather than corrects energetic collapse.

Therefore:

OXPHOS failure + ATP-synthase reversal → progressively deeper ATP depletion.

A second amplification mechanism is osmotic.

Loss of inner-membrane selectivity allows solute equilibration between the mitochondrial matrix and cytosol.

Water subsequently enters the matrix:

solute equilibration → osmotic water entry → matrix swelling.

Severe matrix swelling distorts cristae, disrupts inner-membrane architecture, and can mechanically compromise the outer mitochondrial membrane [1,3,25].

A third amplification mechanism is ROS-induced ROS release.

Mitochondrial dysfunction can increase ROS generation. ROS can further sensitize mPTP opening in the same or adjacent mitochondria, creating a positive-feedback circuit [7,26].

Thus:

initial MPT → respiratory/redox dysfunction → additional ROS → further MPT sensitization → propagation across the mitochondrial population.

Ca2+ can participate in a similar feedback loop.

Mitochondrial dysfunction alters cellular Ca2+ handling, while continued Ca2+ accumulation increases pore-opening probability.

Context-dependent signaling can enhance this process.

For example, mitochondrial JNK–SAB signaling has been linked to sustained mitochondrial ROS generation and increased vulnerability to MPT-associated injury in liver models [27].

These amplification loops help explain why isolated reversible pore openings can transition into widespread mitochondrial failure.

5. Protective / Negative Regulators

Normal mitochondria maintain a relatively high threshold for sustained permeability transition.

The first protective mechanism is regulation of mitochondrial Ca2+.

Controlled transfer of Ca2+ between the ER, cytosol, and mitochondria prevents excessive matrix accumulation [14-15,33].

Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is physiologically important for metabolism, so protection is not achieved by eliminating mitochondrial Ca2+ signaling entirely but by preventing excessive or persistent accumulation.

A second protective mechanism is maintenance of redox homeostasis.

Antioxidant systems reduce oxidative modifications that sensitize the pore.

A third regulatory layer is intracellular pH.

Acidic matrix pH desensitizes the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, whereas high matrix pH sensitizes it [2].

A fourth protective factor is preservation of adenine nucleotides and bioenergetic state.

ATP and ADP availability influences mitochondrial conformation and pore sensitivity.

Severe nucleotide depletion therefore favors transition toward irreversible failure.

Cyclophilin D is a major positive regulator of opening and therefore represents an important therapeutic checkpoint.

Cyclophilin D inhibition or deletion increases resistance to Ca2+- and ROS-triggered MPT [19-20].

Cyclosporine-associated strategies and other Cyclophilin D-directed approaches have consequently been explored as methods for raising the pore-opening threshold.

ANT also regulates permeability-transition sensitivity [22-23].

F1FO-ATP synthase contributes both to normal bioenergetics and potentially to pore behavior, although the exact relationship remains controversial [1,21,24].

Mitochondrial quality-control processes, including mitophagy and maintenance of mitochondrial dynamics, can also influence the amount of damaged mitochondrial mass available to undergo pathological transition [8,28].

Thus, protection occurs primarily by maintaining:

controlled mitochondrial Ca2+;

redox balance;

appropriate pH;

adenine-nucleotide availability;

mitochondrial membrane organization;

normal mitochondrial quality control;

and a high threshold for Cyclophilin D-dependent mPTP opening.

6. Commitment / Point of No Return

MPT is unusual among RCD-related mechanisms because pore opening is not intrinsically equivalent to irreversible death.

Transient pore openings can close.

Mitochondria can repolarize.

Ca2+ and ROS can be released without catastrophic energetic failure [1,7-8].

Thus:

mPTP opening ≠ automatic commitment.

The key distinction is between transient/localized and persistent/widespread permeability transition.

The best-supported commitment state is reached when sustained high-conductance opening affects enough of the mitochondrial population that Δψm cannot be re-established and critical matrix metabolites are lost [7,29].

At this stage:

persistent MPT → irreversible depolarization → failure of respiratory recovery → inability to restore ATP synthesis.

Once ATP availability falls below the level needed to sustain essential ion pumps and metabolic processes, cellular recovery becomes increasingly impossible.

Therefore, the operational point of no return is not one molecular cleavage or phosphorylation event.

It is a systems-level mitochondrial threshold:

sustained high-conductance permeability across sufficient mitochondrial mass → irreversible loss of mitochondrial energetic competence.

The precise quantitative threshold remains unresolved and probably varies between cell types according to mitochondrial reserve capacity, glycolytic capacity, metabolic demand, and mitochondrial abundance.

This remains one of the major open questions in the field.

7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

What physically kills the cell after lethal mitochondrial permeability transition?

The immediate molecular event is sustained high-conductance permeabilization of the inner mitochondrial membrane.

However, pore opening itself kills the cell indirectly through catastrophic bioenergetic failure.

The physical sequence is:

sustained mPTP opening → Δψm collapse → oxidative-phosphorylation arrest → ATP-synthesis failure → ATP hydrolysis → severe ATP depletion → ion-pump failure → ionic and osmotic disequilibrium → cellular swelling → loss of membrane integrity → death.

Therefore, the dominant terminal lesion is not a dedicated plasma-membrane protein pore analogous to GSDMD or MLKL.

Instead, the cell loses the energetic capacity required to maintain itself.

Plasma-membrane ion pumps depend on ATP.

When ATP becomes critically depleted:

Na+ and other ions accumulate intracellularly → water follows osmotically → the cell swells → plasma-membrane integrity eventually fails.

At the mitochondrial level, osmotic matrix swelling damages cristae and can cause outer-membrane rupture.

At the cellular level, the resulting energy deficit converts mitochondrial injury into global homeostatic collapse.

The most defensible answer to “what physically kills the cell?” is therefore:

irreversible mitochondrial bioenergetic collapse eliminates ATP-dependent ionic homeostasis, producing osmotic cellular failure and terminal membrane rupture.

This is the lethal execution mechanism when MPT progresses to MPT-driven necrosis.

8. Morphological Sequence

Early MPT may produce minimal visible structural change.

Transient pore flickering can occur in mitochondria that subsequently repolarize [1,7].

As permeability transition becomes sustained, mitochondria lose Δψm and begin to swell.

The morphological progression is:

polarized intact mitochondria → mitochondrial depolarization → matrix swelling → cristae distortion/disorganization → severe mitochondrial swelling → outer-membrane disruption → whole-cell swelling → plasma-membrane rupture.

Similarly, mitochondrial swelling alone cannot establish that mPTP opening caused death.

Terminal cell morphology is predominantly necrotic.

Cells become swollen, organelle architecture deteriorates, and plasma-membrane integrity is lost.

This differs from canonical apoptosis, in which cells typically shrink and undergo ordered caspase-dependent dismantling, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and apoptotic-body formation.

Therefore, morphology supports but does not establish MPT.

Functional pore measurements are required.

9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

MPT has important crosstalk with apoptosis.

The key distinction lies primarily in the membrane involved and the metabolic consequence.

Intrinsic apoptosis is classically initiated by BAX/BAK-mediated outer mitochondrial membrane permeabilization [9] .

MPT begins with permeability transition of the inner mitochondrial membrane.

However, extensive matrix swelling after MPT can secondarily disrupt the outer mitochondrial membrane and release intermembrane-space proteins.

If ATP remains sufficiently available, cytochrome c release may permit apoptosome assembly and caspase activation.

Thus, under some conditions:

MPT → mitochondrial outer-membrane disruption → cytochrome c release → apoptosis.

Under more severe energetic collapse:

MPT → ATP depletion → inability to sustain apoptotic execution → necrotic death.

Therefore, MPT can contribute to apoptosis in some contexts without being equivalent to intrinsic apoptosis.

Necroptosis must also be distinguished carefully.

Some experimental models connect RIPK3 and CaMKII signaling to mitochondrial permeability transition, especially in cardiovascular injury [30].

Thus:

RIPK3–MLKL-dependent plasma-membrane execution → canonical necroptosis;

sustained inner-membrane permeability transition → mitochondrial energetic collapse → MPT-driven necrosis.

Ferroptosis also shares several upstream features with MPT, including ROS, mitochondrial dysfunction, iron-associated oxidative stress, and metabolic injury.

MPT can therefore occur secondarily during ferroptotic stress without redefining the death modality.

Similarly, secondary mitochondrial depolarization during another RCD modality should not automatically be interpreted as primary MPT-driven death.

The key diagnostic question is:

does sustained MPT causally execute the death, or is it merely a downstream consequence/amplifier?

10. Experimental Validation

Strong evidence for MPT requires direct functional measurement of mitochondrial permeability transition.

A single marker is insufficient.

The strongest experimental design combines multiple orthogonal readouts.

First, demonstrate mitochondrial depolarization.

Δψm-sensitive probes can establish the timing and extent of mitochondrial membrane-potential loss.

Second, directly assess permeability transition.

Calcein-based assays, mitochondrial permeability measurements, and isolated-mitochondria approaches can demonstrate increased inner-membrane permeability.

Third, measure mitochondrial swelling.

Large-amplitude matrix swelling is an important downstream consequence of sustained MPT.

Fourth, assess calcium retention capacity.

The amount of Ca2+ that mitochondria can retain before undergoing permeability transition provides a functional measurement of pore susceptibility.

Fifth, test Cyclophilin D dependence.

PPIF/Cyclophilin D deletion, silencing, or pharmacological inhibition should increase resistance in Cyclophilin D-sensitive MPT models [19-20].

However, Cyclophilin D resistance should not be used as the sole diagnostic criterion because the precise pore architecture and degree of CypD dependence can vary by context.

Sixth, measure the bioenergetic consequences.

These include:

loss of Δψm;

respiratory failure;

ATP depletion;

failure to recover oxidative phosphorylation.

Seventh, establish temporal order.

The most informative sequence is:

stress → mPTP opening → Δψm loss → mitochondrial swelling → ATP depletion → membrane failure → cell death.

Eighth, perform causal rescue.

Interventions that suppress the identified upstream Ca2+/ROS trigger or desensitize mPTP opening should prevent or delay the downstream lethal sequence.

Ninth, distinguish alternative RCD mechanisms experimentally.

Caspase inhibitors can help separate classical apoptosis.

MLKL pathway analysis can distinguish necroptosis.

Lipid-peroxidation assays and ferroptosis rescue agents can distinguish ferroptosis.

Morphology alone cannot establish MPT.

Likewise, transcriptomic profiling cannot directly demonstrate MPT because pore opening is a biophysical event.

Expression changes in PPIF, ANT-family genes, F1FO-ATP synthase components, MCU-related genes, VDAC1, IP3R-associated genes, redox regulators, or mitochondrial-stress pathways can indicate susceptibility to MPT-associated states but cannot prove active permeability transition.

bioenergetic collapse → ion-homeostasis failure → osmotic swelling → plasma-membrane failure.

12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

A representative physiological stress route is:

severe cellular stress → ER/cytosolic Ca2+ mobilization → IP3R/GRP75/VDAC1-associated transfer toward mitochondria → mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake → matrix Ca2+ overload.

Simultaneously:

oxidative/metabolic stress → mitochondrial ROS elevation + ATP/nucleotide disturbances + redox imbalance.

These signals converge:

matrix Ca2+ + ROS + metabolic stress → Cyclophilin D-dependent sensitization of mPTP machinery → permeability-transition opening.

If opening is brief:

transient MPT → limited Ca2+/ROS release → pore closure → mitochondrial repolarization → survival.

If opening becomes sustained:

high-conductance MPT → proton-gradient collapse → Δψm loss → oxidative-phosphorylation arrest.

Then:

ATP synthase may reverse → ATP hydrolysis → accelerated ATP depletion.

Simultaneously:

solute equilibration → osmotic matrix water influx → mitochondrial swelling → cristae disruption → outer-membrane damage.

Propagation follows:

mitochondrial dysfunction → additional ROS/Ca2+ dysregulation → additional pore opening → increasing fraction of mitochondrial network becomes depolarized.

Commitment occurs when:

MPT becomes prolonged and widespread → Δψm cannot recover → matrix metabolites are lost → electron transport and ATP synthesis cannot be re-established.

Terminal progression:

irreversible mitochondrial energetic failure → ATP exhaustion → cellular ion-pump failure → Na+/water accumulation → cellular swelling → loss of plasma-membrane integrity → regulated necrotic death.

13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, Mitochondrial permeability transition should be separated conceptually from MPT-Driven Necrosis even though the two entities are tightly linked.

Mitochondrial permeability transition is the underlying mitochondrial process:

a stress-sensitive increase in inner mitochondrial membrane permeability mediated by the mPTP.

This process can exist in at least two functional regimes.

Transient MPT:

short-lived or low-conductance opening → potentially reversible Ca2+/ROS signaling and mitochondrial adaptation.

Lethal MPT:

persistent high-conductance opening → irreversible mitochondrial depolarization → oxidative-phosphorylation failure → swelling → ATP collapse → MPT-driven necrosis.

Thus, MPT is not synonymous with cell death in every instance.

The decisive variable is persistence and extent.

Cyclophilin D/PPIF is the strongest established molecular regulator but should not be annotated as the physical pore-forming executor.

The exact structural identity of the pore remains the largest unresolved issue.

F1FO-ATP synthase-associated models and ANT-associated models both have substantial experimental support, and the evidence remains compatible with more than one molecular permeability pathway.

The field is therefore mechanistically asymmetric:

the biological sequence from stress to permeability transition to bioenergetic collapse is well established;

the atomic identity of the pore is not.

When MPT becomes lethal, the actual physical cause of death is mitochondrial bioenergetic collapse.

The central causal chain is:

sustained mPTP opening → Δψm collapse → oxidative-phosphorylation failure → ATP depletion → ion-homeostasis failure → osmotic swelling → plasma-membrane failure.

This sequence should be distinguished from secondary MPT occurring during apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, or other stresses.

For RCDome annotation, a useful operational distinction is:

entity type: mitochondrial mechanism/process;

core event: inner mitochondrial membrane permeability transition;

major regulator: PPIF/Cyclophilin D;

structural pore identity: unresolved;

reversible state: transient/low-conductance opening;

lethal state: sustained/high-conductance opening;

associated death modality: MPT-driven necrosis;

terminal lesion when lethal: irreversible mitochondrial bioenergetic collapse;

transcriptomic interpretation: MPT-associated mitochondrial stress or susceptibility state only, never evidence of active pore opening.

The main unresolved questions are which molecular structures generate the lethal high-conductance pore, what measurable event precisely defines irreversible commitment in intact cells, and when MPT acts as a primary executor versus a secondary amplifier of another RCD program.
