## Ammonia-Induced Cell Death

Ammonia-induced cell death describes cellular death caused by toxic accumulation or abnormal intracellular handling of ammonia. Current evidence supports two mechanistically distinct levels of interpretation. First, recent work in proliferating mammalian cells, particularly effector CD8+ T cells, supports an emerging organelle-centered death mechanism in which endogenous ammonia production exceeds the buffering capacity of lysosomes, causing lysosomal alkalinization, defective autophagic flux, secondary mitochondrial injury, and ultimately collapse of cellular homeostasis [1-4]. Hyperammonemia induces programmed liver cell death, including apoptosis and necroptosis, and causes lysosomal and mitochondrial damage [2,5].

Therefore, ammonia exposure alone is not sufficient to define a distinct regulated cell-death modality. The strongest evidence for an ammonia-specific pathway requires a causal lysosome–mitochondria sequence in which reducing ammonia production or preventing ammonia-associated lysosomal dysfunction protects cells from death [1-3]. By contrast, if ammonia ultimately activates BAX/BAK-dependent mitochondrial apoptosis, RIPK1–RIPK3–MLKL-dependent necroptosis, NLRP3–caspase-1–GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis, or lipid-peroxidation-driven ferroptosis, the terminal death mechanism should be assigned to that established modality.

The most mechanistically developed ammonia-specific pathway begins with cellular glutamine metabolism.

Glutamine is an important nitrogen and carbon source for rapidly proliferating cells. During glutaminolysis, glutamine is metabolized to glutamate and subsequently contributes carbon to the tricarboxylic acid cycle while releasing nitrogen in the form of ammonia [1,8,33].

Ammonia produced by glutaminolysis is not necessarily toxic. At physiological concentrations, ammonia can diffuse between cellular compartments and participate in metabolic signaling. Indeed, glutaminolysis-derived ammonia can regulate autophagy and other metabolic responses without causing cell death [8].

Toxicity emerges when the rate of ammonia generation exceeds the capacity of cellular detoxification, transport, and compartmental buffering systems.

This produces the first general step:

high glutamine catabolism or extracellular hyperammonemia → intracellular NH3/NH4+ accumulation → redistribution of ammonia among cytosolic, mitochondrial, and lysosomal compartments → increasing disturbance of pH, nitrogen metabolism, and organelle homeostasis.

In proliferating effector CD8+ T cells, endogenous ammonia generation through glutaminolysis appears particularly important [1]. These metabolically active cells produce substantial amounts of ammonia while simultaneously requiring efficient mitochondrial metabolism and autophagic quality control.

The initiating sequence can therefore be represented as high proliferative and metabolic demand → increased glutaminolysis → increased endogenous ammonia generation → increasing mitochondrial and intracellular ammonia burden.

In this pathway, glutaminase activity represents an upstream metabolic source rather than a death executor. Inhibition of glutaminolysis or reduction of GLS1-dependent ammonia production decreases intracellular ammonia accumulation and protects cells from ammonia-associated death [1-3].

Thus, the earliest experimentally tractable checkpoint is:

glutamine → glutaminolysis/GLS-dependent metabolism → ammonia generation.

The next critical mechanistic step involves ammonia trafficking between mitochondria and lysosomes.

Recent evidence indicates that lysosomes participate in intracellular ammonia handling. Under moderate ammonia production, ammonia can be transported or redistributed toward lysosomal compartments, where the acidic lysosomal environment contributes to its sequestration [1-3].

This provides an intracellular buffering mechanism that protects metabolically active cells from ammonia accumulation.

The protective sequence is therefore:

mitochondrial/metabolic ammonia generation → transfer toward lysosomal compartments → lysosomal ammonia sequestration → maintenance of mitochondrial ammonia below a toxic threshold.

In long-term activated effector CD8+ T cells, ammonia generated by mitochondrial glutaminolysis is released into the cytoplasm and subsequently enters lysosomes via the ammonium transporter Rhesus C glycoprotein (RHCG) [2].

RHCG should not be described as a classical extracellular death receptor. Instead, it participates in ammonia transport and compartmental handling and thereby influences whether ammonia remains adequately buffered or accumulates to toxic concentrations.

The lysosome becomes the first major organelle checkpoint.

Normal lysosomal degradation requires maintenance of a strongly acidic lumen. Acidification allows lysosomal hydrolases to function efficiently and supports degradation of autophagic cargo.

When ammonia accumulation exceeds lysosomal buffering capacity, lysosomal pH increases [1-3].

The sequence becomes:

excess ammonia delivery to lysosome → progressive lysosomal alkalinization → loss of optimal acidic lysosomal environment → impaired lysosomal hydrolase function → defective degradation of autophagic cargo.

Importantly, lysosomal alkalinization is mechanistically different from lysosomal membrane permeabilization.

The emerging ammonia-death mechanism is not currently defined by primary rupture of lysosomal membranes and massive cathepsin release. Instead, the major early lesion is failure of lysosomal acid-dependent function.

Thus, the core lysosomal defect is functional alkalinization rather than an established cathepsin-dependent lysosomal death program.

Because lysosomes are the terminal degradative compartments of macroautophagy, their alkalinization immediately affects autophagic flux.

Autophagosomes can continue to form, but their contents cannot be efficiently degraded if lysosomal function becomes compromised.

Consequently, ammonia accumulation can produce an apparent increase in autophagic structures while simultaneously reducing effective autophagic flux.

This distinction is critical.

LC3 accumulation or increased autophagosome number does not necessarily indicate increased productive autophagy. Under ammonia stress, such accumulation may instead indicate failure of late-stage degradation.

The sequence can therefore be represented as:

lysosomal alkalinization → impaired autolysosomal degradation → failure of autophagic flux → accumulation of undegraded cellular material and damaged organelles.

Mitochondria are particularly affected because mitophagy and related quality-control mechanisms normally remove dysfunctional mitochondria.

When lysosomal degradation becomes inefficient, damaged mitochondria cannot be cleared appropriately.

Thus:

lysosomal alkalinization → defective autophagic/mitophagic clearance → accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria → increasing mitochondrial stress.

At the same time, the inability of lysosomes to continue buffering intracellular ammonia causes ammonia to remain available for redistribution into other cellular compartments.

The current organelle-centered model proposes a damaging feedback loop between mitochondria and lysosomes.

Mitochondrial metabolism generates ammonia.

Lysosomes initially buffer part of this ammonia load.

Excess ammonia alkalinizes lysosomes.

Alkalinized lysosomes lose degradative activity.

Autophagic clearance fails.

Ammonia buffering becomes progressively less effective.

Ammonia then accumulates again in mitochondria and other compartments.

Mitochondrial damage consequently intensifies.

The central amplification loop can therefore be represented as:

mitochondrial ammonia generation → lysosomal sequestration → lysosomal ammonia overload → lysosomal alkalinization → loss of lysosomal degradation → defective autophagic flux → impaired mitochondrial quality control → mitochondrial damage → failure of ammonia handling → renewed mitochondrial ammonia accumulation.

This lysosome–mitochondria feedback loop has been proposed as a distinctive mechanistic feature in some models of ammonia-induced cell death as an independent phenomenon [2].

Mitochondrial swelling is one of the major downstream structural abnormalities associated with this pathway [40].

As ammonia accumulates and mitochondrial quality control deteriorates, mitochondria undergo progressive structural and functional damage [1-4].

This can involve altered mitochondrial morphology, swelling, impaired oxidative metabolism, loss of respiratory efficiency, and eventually inability to maintain sufficient bioenergetic output.

The precise sequence linking ammonia accumulation to mitochondrial swelling has not yet been fully resolved.

Therefore, mitochondrial swelling should be treated as a strongly associated downstream organelle lesion rather than as a molecular executor analogous to MLKL or GSDMD.

The mitochondrial dysfunction generated by ammonia is nevertheless supported by a much broader literature.

Hyperammonemia rapidly alters mitochondrial energy metabolism in astrocytes [9].

One important mechanism involves glutamate dehydrogenase 2.

Under ammonia excess, GDH2 can drive reductive amination of α-ketoglutarate to glutamate [9].

This reaction consumes α-ketoglutarate that would otherwise support the TCA cycle.

The corresponding metabolic sequence is:

excess ammonia → increased GDH2-dependent reductive amination of α-ketoglutarate → increased glutamate production → depletion of TCA-cycle intermediates → reduced oxidative metabolic capacity.

This represents a form of metabolic cataplerosis.

Because α-ketoglutarate is a central TCA-cycle intermediate, excessive diversion of α-ketoglutarate away from oxidative metabolism decreases TCA-cycle capacity and limits substrate availability for mitochondrial respiration [9].

The same general principle has been observed in skeletal muscle during hyperammonemia, where depletion of TCA intermediates accompanies altered NAD+/NADH balance, reduced ATP production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress [10].

Thus, ammonia can simultaneously impair mitochondria through two mechanistic levels:

failure of lysosome-dependent mitochondrial quality control and direct metabolic depletion of TCA-cycle intermediates.

The metabolic sequence can therefore be represented as:

ammonia accumulation → altered nitrogen fixation and glutamate metabolism → α-ketoglutarate depletion → TCA-cycle dysfunction → reduced respiratory capacity → energetic stress.

Mitochondrial electron transport can consequently become inefficient.

Impaired respiration increases the probability of electron leakage and reactive oxygen species production [10-12,36].

ROS and reactive nitrogen species are therefore important amplifiers of ammonia toxicity.

However, ROS should not be treated as the universal defining executor of ammonia-induced cell death.

Oxidative stress is observed across multiple ammonia-associated injury models, but its downstream consequence depends on cellular context.

In some cells, ROS amplifies mitochondrial damage without directly defining the death modality.

In others, ROS activates apoptosis.

In microglia, mitochondrial ROS can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis.

In selected tissues, oxidative changes contribute to ferroptosis.

Thus, ROS occupies an amplification layer rather than a universal terminal execution layer.

Mitochondrial permeability transition represents another important ammonia-associated mechanism, especially in astrocytes.

Ammonia can induce mitochondrial permeability transition in cultured astrocytes [11-12].

Oxidative and nitrosative stress contribute to this process.

The sequence in this context is:

hyperammonemia → mitochondrial and redox stress → mitochondrial permeability transition → loss of normal mitochondrial electrochemical function → impaired ATP generation and mitochondrial homeostasis.

Pharmacological inhibition of mitochondrial permeability transition can reduce aspects of ammonia-associated mitochondrial injury, supporting a causal role in particular models [11-12].

However, mitochondrial permeability transition should not automatically be inserted into the core emerging CD8+ T-cell ammonia-death pathway because the strongest recent model emphasizes lysosomal alkalinization, autophagic failure, and secondary mitochondrial damage rather than a universally demonstrated permeability-transition pore.

The mitochondrial permeability transition therefore represents a context-dependent downstream route.

A historically important ammonia-toxicity model involves astrocytes and glutamine.

Astrocytes detoxify ammonia through glutamine synthetase, converting ammonia and glutamate to glutamine.

This reaction initially protects the extracellular and neural environment from excess ammonia.

However, excessive glutamine production can itself become problematic.

The “Trojan horse” model proposes that glutamine generated during hyperammonemia enters mitochondria, where mitochondrial glutaminase activity releases ammonia again [13-14].

This produces localized mitochondrial ammonia accumulation.

The sequence is:

extracellular ammonia → astrocytic glutamine synthetase → glutamine accumulation → mitochondrial glutamine transport → intramitochondrial glutamine metabolism → renewed ammonia generation → mitochondrial dysfunction.

This mechanism differs from the more recent lysosome–mitochondria pathway but converges on the same broader principle: cellular detoxification can paradoxically redistribute ammonia into mitochondrial compartments where it becomes toxic.

Interference with glutamine synthesis or mitochondrial glutamine transport can reduce mitochondrial stress and astrocyte swelling in these models [13-15,37].

Thus, ammonia detoxification is highly compartment dependent.

The same molecule can be safely handled in one compartment and become toxic after redistribution to another.

In neural tissues, ammonia also engages NMDA-receptor-dependent signaling.

Hyperammonemia alters glutamatergic neurotransmission and can increase NMDA-receptor-associated signaling [16-18].

Excessive NMDA-receptor activity promotes Ca2+ entry and downstream redox stress.

The pathway can be represented as:

hyperammonemia → altered glutamate/NMDA signaling → NMDA-receptor activation → intracellular Ca2+ elevation → mitochondrial stress and ROS/RNS generation → neuronal dysfunction and death susceptibility.

Protection by NMDA-receptor antagonists such as MK-801 or APV in experimental systems supports a causal role for this route [16-17].

Nevertheless, NMDA receptors are not universal ammonia sensors.

Their importance is primarily restricted to neural and neurovascular contexts.

Ammonia-associated astrocyte swelling provides another distinct outcome.

Astrocytes exposed to high ammonia concentrations develop osmotic and ion-regulatory abnormalities, leading to cell swelling and contributing to cerebral edema [18-21].

NKCC1, the Na+-K+-2Cl− cotransporter, contributes to this process.

Oxidative stress and intracellular signaling can increase NKCC1-associated ion accumulation, promoting water entry into astrocytes [20].

The sequence can therefore be represented as:

hyperammonemia → oxidative/osmotic signaling → altered ion transport including NKCC1 → intracellular ion accumulation → water influx → astrocyte swelling.

Astrocytic K+ buffering is also impaired during hyperammonemia, contributing to neuronal disinhibition and seizures [19].

These effects are physiologically important but should not automatically be classified as the execution phase of ammonia-induced cell death.

Astrocyte swelling and cerebral edema can cause severe tissue dysfunction without requiring immediate intrinsic death of every swollen astrocyte.

Thus, ammonia-associated edema represents an important toxicity branch but is not synonymous with the emerging ammonia-specific regulated death mechanism.

The strongest current ammonia-specific model instead focuses on proliferating cells in which lysosomal ammonia handling becomes overwhelmed.

The corresponding canonical candidate sequence is:

high glutaminolysis → excessive intracellular ammonia generation → RHCG-associated delivery or handling of ammonia at lysosomes → progressive lysosomal alkalinization → loss of acidic lysosomal degradative activity → autophagic-flux blockade → inability to clear damaged mitochondria → ammonia redistribution/reflux toward mitochondria → mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction → failure of mitochondrial and lysosomal homeostasis → loss of viability [1-3].

This sequence is supported most strongly in effector CD8+ T cells [1].

Recent renal studies provide additional evidence that a similar process can occur outside lymphocytes [39].

This observation is important because it suggests that the lysosome–mitochondria mechanism may not be restricted entirely to activated T cells.

However, evidence across mammalian tissues remains limited.

It is therefore premature to assume that every ammonia-sensitive cell employs the same death machinery.

The mechanistic category should remain explicitly context dependent.

A major unresolved question is whether there is a dedicated molecular ammonia sensor initiating the organelle-centered pathway.

Current evidence does not identify an ammonia receptor analogous to Fas in extrinsic apoptosis or a dedicated execution sensor analogous to the inflammasome.

Instead, sensing appears physicochemical and metabolic.

Cells respond to the concentration and compartmental distribution of ammonia through altered lysosomal pH, nitrogen metabolism, mitochondrial function, and ion homeostasis.

RHCG participates in ammonia transport rather than functioning as a classical signaling receptor.

GLS1 controls ammonia production.

Lysosomes function as buffering compartments.

Mitochondria represent both a metabolic ammonia source and a major downstream target.

Therefore, the architecture of this pathway is better understood as metabolic production → organelle transport → physicochemical buffering failure → organelle dysfunction rather than receptor → kinase cascade → executor.

Protective mechanisms are correspondingly concentrated upstream.

Reducing ammonia production is one of the strongest protective interventions.

Inhibition of glutaminolysis or GLS-dependent metabolism decreases intracellular ammonia generation and protects effector T cells from the lysosome–mitochondria death program [1-3].

This establishes the first resistance branch:

GLS inhibition → reduced glutaminolysis-derived ammonia → preserved lysosomal buffering capacity → maintained autophagic flux → reduced mitochondrial injury → survival.

Preserving lysosomal function represents a second protective mechanism.

Lysosomes require adequate acidification for efficient degradation.

Treatment with C381, a small molecule targeting vacuolar ATPase to reduce lysosomal pH, can restore impaired autophagic flux and reduce the death of activated CD8+ T effector cells [2].

The protective sequence becomes:

maintenance of lysosomal acidity → preservation of lysosomal enzyme activity → sustained autophagic flux → continued clearance of damaged mitochondria → prevention of the mitochondrial damage feedback loop.

Autophagy therefore has a predominantly protective role in the emerging ammonia-specific pathway.

This distinction is important because increased autophagosome abundance during ammonia exposure should not be misinterpreted as autophagy-dependent cell death.

The central problem is failure of productive autophagic flux resulting from lysosomal dysfunction.

In several experimental systems, enhancement or preservation of autophagy reduces ammonia-induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis [23].

Thus, the mechanistic logic differs from autosis and other forms of autophagy-dependent death.

In ammonia-induced organelle collapse:

functional autophagy is protective,

whereas impaired autophagic clearance contributes to death.

Antioxidant defenses constitute another major resistance layer.

Glutathione, N-acetylcysteine, mitochondrial antioxidants such as MitoTEMPO, and endogenous Nrf2-associated responses can reduce ammonia-induced oxidative injury in various experimental systems [6-7,24].

However, antioxidant rescue is context dependent.

Glutamine mediates ammonia neurotoxicity, involving oxidative stress and astrocyte swelling [14].

This suggests that redox failure sensitizes cells rather than functioning as the sole initiating lesion.

Ion and pH regulation provide additional protective mechanisms.

NHE1, the sodium–hydrogen exchanger 1, contributes to cellular pH and ion homeostasis. In hyperammonemia-exposed hepatocytes, inhibition of NHE1 worsens ATP depletion and apoptosis [25].

Thus:

NHE1 activity → improved pH/ion homeostasis → reduced mitochondrial and energetic stress → protection from downstream apoptosis.

In neural ammonia toxicity, reducing mitochondrial glutamine entry through histidine-sensitive mechanisms can limit oxidative stress, mitochondrial permeability transition, and brain edema [15].

Again, these protective mechanisms emphasize the importance of ammonia compartmentalization.

There is no universal experimentally established point of no return for ammonia-induced cell death.

During early ammonia accumulation, the system remains potentially reversible.

Reducing ammonia production, restoring lysosomal function, blocking mitochondrial ammonia accumulation, correcting redox stress, or inhibiting context-specific signaling can protect cells.

The candidate commitment transition in the emerging organelle-centered pathway occurs when lysosomal ammonia-buffering capacity is persistently exceeded.

At this stage:

lysosomal pH remains elevated,

autophagic degradation remains impaired,

damaged mitochondria accumulate,

mitochondrial ammonia exposure increases,

and mitochondrial swelling and metabolic dysfunction become progressive [1,22,35].

The transition can therefore be represented as:

reversible ammonia overload → sustained lysosomal alkalinization → persistent autophagic-flux failure → progressive mitochondrial injury → inability to restore organelle quality control.

However, this remains a candidate rather than a precisely demonstrated universal molecular checkpoint.

No experiment currently establishes one discrete event after which all ammonia-stressed cells inevitably die.

The terminal execution chemistry of the emerging pathway is also incompletely resolved.

No dedicated ammonia-specific protease, membrane pore, or terminal executor has been identified.

The strongest model is catastrophic failure of lysosomal and mitochondrial homeostasis.

Lysosomes lose acid-dependent degradative capacity.

Autophagic turnover fails.

Damaged mitochondria accumulate.

Mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction intensify.

Oxidative metabolism declines.

ATP-generating capacity becomes insufficient.

Cellular quality-control mechanisms can no longer compensate.

The proximate lethal sequence can therefore be represented as:

persistent lysosomal dysfunction + failed autophagic clearance + progressive mitochondrial injury → mitochondrial bioenergetic failure → failure of organelle and cellular homeostasis → irreversible loss of viability.

This differs from apoptosis because death is not primarily executed through caspase-dependent proteolytic dismantling.

It differs from necroptosis because MLKL-mediated plasma-membrane permeabilization is not required as the defining event.

It differs from pyroptosis because gasdermin pores are not the central mechanism.

It differs from ferroptosis because iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation is not the defining biochemical lesion.

However, ammonia can activate every one of these pathways under other conditions.

Therefore, alternative ammonia-triggered routes must be kept mechanistically separate.

The apoptotic route is one of the best documented.

Ammonia can generate mitochondrial ROS, impair mitochondrial membrane potential, induce DNA damage, activate ER stress, alter BCL-2-family signaling, and stimulate death-receptor-associated pathways [5-7,26-28].

The intrinsic apoptotic branch can be represented as:

ammonia accumulation → ROS/metabolic/ER stress → increased pro-apoptotic BAX/BAK activity relative to BCL-2-family protection → mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization → cytochrome c release → APAF1 apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → apoptotic cellular dismantling.

In these cases, ammonia is the upstream insult, but apoptosis is the actual death modality.

Experimental ammonia-induced apoptosis has been demonstrated in intestinal epithelial cells, hepatocytes, lymphocytes, immune tissues, and multiple aquatic and agricultural species [6-7,26-28].

ER stress can provide another entry point.

Ammonia-associated disruption of cellular metabolism and redox homeostasis activates unfolded-protein-response pathways, including PERK-, IRE1-, ATF4-, and CHOP-associated signaling in selected models [7].

Persistent ER stress can increase pro-apoptotic signaling and converge on mitochondrial apoptosis.

The corresponding sequence is:

ammonia stress → ER protein-homeostasis disturbance → UPR activation → PERK/ATF4/CHOP and related signaling → altered BCL-2-family balance → mitochondrial apoptosis.

Death-receptor signaling may also participate in selected ammonia-exposed immune cells [27].

Thus, ammonia-induced apoptosis may contain intrinsic, extrinsic, or mixed signaling depending on tissue and experimental conditions.

A BAX-associated ammonia-death phenotype has also been reported in clear-cell renal-cell carcinoma [29].

This observation is therapeutically interesting but does not replace the lysosome–mitochondria pathway as the defining candidate ammonia-specific mechanism.

If BAX-mediated mitochondrial permeabilization is causally responsible for death, the terminal process remains an apoptotic or apoptosis-like mitochondrial death route.

The distinction is important for RCD classification.

Another ammonia-associated terminal pathway is necroptosis.

In hyperammonemia models, programmed liver cell death involves apoptosis and necroptosis, with RIPK1 and RIPK3 implicated; MLKL involvement requires direct measurement [5].

The sequence is:

hyperammonemia plus appropriate inflammatory/death-receptor context → RIPK1/RIPK3 necrosome formation → MLKL phosphorylation and oligomerization → plasma-membrane permeabilization → necroptotic death.

In this setting, ammonia acts upstream as a metabolic stressor capable of promoting the inflammatory environment in which necroptosis becomes activated.

MLKL, not ammonia itself, executes terminal membrane failure.

Therefore, this should be described as ammonia-triggered necroptosis rather than the core ammonia-specific organelle death program.

Pyroptosis represents another context-specific route.

In microglia, hyperammonemia can increase mitochondrial oxidative stress and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [30].

The canonical sequence is:

hyperammonemia → mitochondrial dysfunction and mtROS → NLRP3 activation → NLRP3–ASC inflammasome assembly → caspase-1 activation → GSDMD cleavage → GSDMD-N pore formation → inflammatory membrane permeabilization and pyroptotic death.

The inflammatory consequences include maturation and release of inflammasome-associated cytokines and amplification of neuroinflammation.

Again, ammonia is the upstream stimulus; NLRP3–caspase-1–GSDMD is the actual execution machinery.

Therefore, pyroptotic ammonia injury must not be merged mechanistically with lysosomal alkalinization-mediated ammonia death.

Ferroptosis has also been observed after acute ammonia exposure in selected organisms and tissues [31,34].

In this route, ammonia-associated metabolic and oxidative disturbances promote iron-dependent lipid-peroxidation chemistry.

The terminal mechanism therefore becomes:

ammonia stress → iron/redox imbalance → insufficient lipid-peroxide detoxification → phospholipid peroxidation → ferroptotic membrane injury → death.

If GPX4 failure and iron-dependent phospholipid oxidation define the terminal lesion, the cell is undergoing ferroptosis initiated or sensitized by ammonia rather than a separate ammonia-specific terminal mechanism.

Ammonia can therefore be positioned upstream of multiple RCD modalities.

The mechanistic decision depends on which downstream machinery is causally necessary.

This yields a central classification rule:

ammonia exposure + lysosomal alkalinization + autophagic-flux failure + secondary mitochondrial collapse, without dependence on another established death executor → candidate distinct ammonia-induced cell death.

Ammonia exposure + BAX/BAK–caspase dependence → ammonia-triggered apoptosis.

Ammonia exposure + RIPK1/RIPK3–MLKL dependence → ammonia-triggered necroptosis.

Ammonia exposure + NLRP3–caspase-1–GSDMD dependence → ammonia-triggered pyroptosis.

Ammonia exposure + iron-dependent lipid-peroxidation dependence → ammonia-triggered ferroptosis.

This distinction should be maintained both experimentally and in pathway annotation.

Morphology alone cannot make the classification.

The emerging organelle-centered ammonia-death phenotype is associated with lysosomal abnormalities, impaired autophagic flux, mitochondrial swelling, progressive mitochondrial injury, and eventual cellular loss [1-3,22].

However, these features are not individually unique.

Mitochondrial swelling can occur during many forms of metabolic injury.

Autophagosome accumulation can arise from either increased autophagy or blocked autophagic degradation.

Loss of mitochondrial membrane potential occurs during several different RCD modalities.

Therefore, no single morphological feature currently diagnoses ammonia-induced cell death.

Astrocyte-dominant hyperammonemia has a different morphological pattern.

Cells develop watery cytoplasm, increased cellular and nuclear dimensions, swollen processes and endfeet, altered mitochondria, and tissue edema [13-14,18-21,32,36].

These changes reflect osmotic, ionic, metabolic, and mitochondrial dysfunction rather than a unique ammonia-specific cellular execution morphology.

Ammonia induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, and cell death mechanisms should be classified mechanistically, distinguishing essential mechanisms from correlative markers [36,38].

Pyroptotic or necroptotic ammonia injury displays the membrane-destructive phenotype of those pathways.

Thus, morphology must always be interpreted together with the causal molecular pathway.

Experimental demonstration of the emerging ammonia-specific route should begin with direct evidence that ammonia is required.

Intracellular ammonia concentration, extracellular ammonia exposure, or ammonia generation through glutaminolysis should be manipulated and related quantitatively to cell survival.

Reducing ammonia generation through inhibition of glutaminolysis or GLS1 should protect the cell if endogenous ammonia production is causally upstream [1-3].

The second requirement is demonstration of lysosomal alkalinization.

Direct measurement of lysosomal pH should show that ammonia accumulation increases lysosomal pH before terminal cell death.

The third requirement is demonstration that lysosomal dysfunction causes impaired autophagic flux.

This requires distinguishing autophagosome accumulation from productive autophagy.

LC3 abundance alone is insufficient.

Flux-sensitive assays should demonstrate that autophagic cargo is no longer efficiently degraded after lysosomal alkalinization.

The fourth requirement is evidence of downstream mitochondrial injury.

This can include mitochondrial swelling, structural abnormalities, altered respiration, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP production, or accumulation of damaged mitochondria.

Temporal analysis should place lysosomal alkalinization and autophagic-flux disruption upstream of severe mitochondrial injury in the proposed distinct pathway.

The fifth requirement is rescue.

Reducing ammonia production or preventing the lysosomal defect should suppress mitochondrial injury and improve cell survival [1-3].

This causal sequence is much stronger evidence than simultaneous observation of ammonia accumulation, LC3 changes, and cell death.

The sixth requirement is discrimination from established RCD mechanisms.

Caspase inhibitors and BAX/BAK manipulation can test apoptosis.

RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL inhibition or genetic perturbation can test necroptosis.

NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD interventions can test pyroptosis.

Ferroptosis-specific rescue experiments can test whether iron-dependent phospholipid oxidation is actually responsible for death.

Only after these alternatives have been excluded should ammonia-associated organelle collapse be considered evidence for a distinct ammonia-induced cell-death mechanism.

The molecular hierarchy of the emerging pathway is therefore unusual.

There is no established classical receptor.

The initiating metabolic machinery consists principally of glutamine metabolism and ammonia-generating processes such as glutaminolysis.

GLS1 controls an important source of endogenous ammonia in metabolically active cells.

Ammonia transport and compartmentalization machinery, including RHCG-associated handling, controls intracellular distribution.

The lysosome functions as the major buffering organelle.

Lysosomal acidification and the autophagy–lysosome system constitute the first protective machinery.

Loss of lysosomal acidity converts a buffering compartment into a dysfunctional organelle.

Autophagic-flux failure then propagates the damage.

Mitochondria represent both an ammonia-producing metabolic compartment and a major target of the subsequent injury.

Mitochondrial swelling, metabolic dysfunction, impaired respiration, and insufficient ATP generation constitute major late events.

However, none of these proteins or organelles has yet been identified as a dedicated molecular executor analogous to MLKL or GSDMD.

The most appropriate description of the execution machinery is therefore combined lysosomal–mitochondrial homeostatic failure.

The candidate core pathway can be summarized as:

high metabolic demand → glutaminolysis → endogenous ammonia generation → intracellular ammonia redistribution → RHCG-associated lysosomal ammonia handling → lysosomal ammonia accumulation → lysosomal alkalinization → reduced acid-dependent lysosomal degradation → autophagic-flux blockade → impaired removal of damaged mitochondria → ammonia redistribution back toward mitochondria and progressive mitochondrial ammonia burden → mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction → impaired TCA/OXPHOS metabolism and ATP production → collapse of lysosomal and mitochondrial quality-control homeostasis → irreversible loss of viability.

An exogenous hyperammonemia route can be represented as:

elevated extracellular ammonia → NH3/NH4+ cellular entry and redistribution → intracellular pH and nitrogen-metabolism disturbance → overload of glutamine-, lysosome-, and mitochondrial-handling systems → lysosomal and/or mitochondrial dysfunction → redox, metabolic, ionic, and organelle stress → either direct organelle collapse or engagement of an established RCD pathway.

An astrocytic metabolic route can be represented as:

hyperammonemia → glutamine synthetase-dependent ammonia incorporation into glutamine → glutamine accumulation → mitochondrial glutamine metabolism → renewed intramitochondrial ammonia generation → mitochondrial permeability transition, oxidative stress, ion dysregulation, and impaired energy metabolism → swelling, neural dysfunction, and context-dependent cell death.

An ammonia-induced apoptosis branch can be represented as:

ammonia accumulation → ROS/ER/DNA/mitochondrial stress → BAX/BAK activation and/or death-receptor signaling → MOMP → cytochrome c → apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → apoptotic death.

An ammonia-induced necroptosis branch can be represented as:

ammonia-associated inflammatory/death-receptor signaling → RIPK1–RIPK3 activation → MLKL phosphorylation → MLKL-mediated membrane permeabilization → necroptosis.

An ammonia-induced pyroptosis branch can be represented as:

hyperammonemia → mitochondrial oxidative stress → NLRP3 inflammasome → ASC → caspase-1 → GSDMD cleavage → inflammatory membrane permeabilization → pyroptosis.

An ammonia-induced ferroptosis branch can be represented as:

ammonia-associated metabolic/redox stress → iron and antioxidant imbalance → phospholipid peroxide accumulation → ferroptotic membrane damage → death.

The integrated mechanistic model is therefore ammonia accumulation generated either extracellularly or through intracellular glutaminolysis → redistribution of NH3/NH4+ across cellular compartments → activation and eventual overload of ammonia-detoxification and organelle-buffering systems → lysosomal alkalinization and/or mitochondrial ammonia accumulation → impaired lysosomal degradation and autophagic flux → accumulation of damaged mitochondria together with direct disturbances of TCA metabolism, respiration, redox state, Ca2+ handling, and ion homeostasis → progressive mitochondrial swelling and bioenergetic dysfunction → either combined lysosome–mitochondria homeostatic collapse or activation of a context-specific established RCD executor → irreversible loss of cellular viability.

The most conservative definition of ammonia-induced cell death as a distinct candidate modality should therefore be restricted to the lysosome–mitochondria mechanism.

Its mechanistic signature is not simply hyperammonemia, ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, or autophagosome accumulation.

The strongest signature is:

excess ammonia generation or accumulation + lysosomal alkalinization + loss of productive autophagic flux + secondary mitochondrial accumulation/swelling/dysfunction + rescue by reducing ammonia production or correcting the lysosomal defect.

The principal molecular novelty is that a metabolic waste product becomes lethal through failure of intracellular compartmental buffering.

Unlike conventional receptor-driven RCD pathways, the system appears to operate through a progressive physicochemical failure of metabolic and organellar homeostasis.

The lysosome initially protects the cell by buffering ammonia.

When this capacity is exceeded, the same lysosome becomes dysfunctional.

Autophagic quality control then fails.

Mitochondrial damage accumulates.

The mitochondria subsequently become both a source and a target of ammonia-associated toxicity.

This produces a self-amplifying organelle loop capable of converting metabolic stress into irreversible cell death.

However, the final molecular execution step remains unresolved.

No dedicated ammonia-induced pore-forming protein, protease, lipid lesion, or other single executor has been identified for the organelle-centered pathway.

Accordingly, mitochondrial swelling should not yet be labeled an executor in the same mechanistic sense as MLKL, GSDMD, or caspase-3.

The best-supported current terminal process is combined failure of lysosomal degradation, autophagic quality control, mitochondrial integrity, and bioenergetic homeostasis.

The largest unresolved question is whether this lysosome–mitochondria mechanism represents a broadly conserved regulated cell-death modality across mammalian cells or a specialized metabolic vulnerability of particular highly active cell states, such as proliferating effector T cells and selected injured renal cells.
