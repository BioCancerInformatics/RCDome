# Phagoptosis

## 1. Definition and Classification

Phagoptosis is an emerging but increasingly well-supported regulated cell-death concept in which a cell that is still viable before engulfment is killed as a direct consequence of its recognition, uptake, and destruction by a phagocytic cell [1-4]. Unlike canonical cell-autonomous RCD modalities, the defining execution machinery resides predominantly in another cell: the phagocyte. Phagoptosis has been proposed to be best understood as a non-cell-autonomous RCD-related concept, rather than as a universally codified core RCD modality equivalent to apoptosis, necroptosis, or pyroptosis, and in some models this classification may be more appropriate than treating it as a core modality [42-43].

The defining mechanistic criterion is not phagocytosis itself, because phagocytes routinely remove cells that are already irreversibly dying or dead. Instead, the essential distinction is causal: the target must remain potentially viable before engulfment, and inhibition of the phagocytic process must preserve the target or prevent its disappearance [1-2,4,42]. Thus, engulfment is not simply a downstream clearance event in phagoptosis; it constitutes the lethal mechanism. This distinction separates phagoptosis from conventional efferocytosis, in which phagocytes remove cells whose intrinsic death program has already passed an irreversible point.

The established mechanistic core comprises reversible or sublethal target-cell marking, phagocyte recognition, receptor-driven cytoskeletal engulfment, phagosome formation, phagolysosomal maturation, and degradative destruction of the still-viable target [1,4,6]. The exact upstream trigger, recognition receptor, opsonin, and target-side marking mechanism vary substantially between tissues and pathological contexts and therefore cannot be represented as universal components.

## 2. Initiation and Triggers

Phagoptosis has no single universal trigger or dedicated upstream death receptor. Diverse physiological and pathological stresses instead converge on a common functional outcome: remodeling of the surface of a viable or potentially recoverable target so that the balance of pro-phagocytic and anti-phagocytic signals shifts toward engulfment [1,12].

Inhibition of UDP/P2Y6 purinergic signaling prevents phagoptosis; TMEM16F mediates microglial phagoptosis via reversible phosphatidylserine exposure in ischemia; activated microglia desialylate their surface, stimulating CR3-dependent phagocytosis and neuron loss [6,31,42].

One of the best-defined target-side routes involves phosphatidylserine (PS). Under normal conditions, PS is strongly enriched in the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane through ATP-dependent lipid transport systems [7-8]. Stress capable of disturbing intracellular Ca2+, ATP availability, or phospholipid-translocase activity can destabilize this asymmetry and expose PS on the external membrane surface.

Importantly, PS externalization does not invariably mean that the cell has irreversibly committed to apoptosis. In canonical apoptosis, caspase-dependent activation of XKR8 and inhibition of ATP11-family flippases generate sustained PS exposure associated with the apoptotic program [8-9]. In viable stressed cells, however, Ca2+-activated TMEM16F can promote reversible phospholipid scrambling and transient PS exposure [6,9-10]. This creates a critical mechanistic window in which the cell is marked as phagocytosis-competent while remaining potentially recoverable.

PS exposure alone is also not universally sufficient to trigger phagoptosis. Viable cells can display PS constitutively without necessarily being engulfed, indicating that phagocytes integrate multiple target-side signals, receptor availability, opsonins, inhibitory signals, and physical characteristics before committing to ingestion [11,37].

Neurons in peri-infarct areas can reversibly expose phosphatidylserine and survive long-term if phagocytosis is blocked [6]. In complementary routes, stressed cells or synapses can become decorated with C1q and C3, allowing recognition by complement receptors on microglia [5,17,41]. Target-derived ATP, ADP, UDP, CX3CL1, or other chemotactic signals can additionally recruit or activate phagocytes before direct engulfment [12-13].

The earliest event is therefore context dependent. What is conserved is the development of an imbalance between signals that promote recognition and those that normally prevent a viable cell from being eaten.

## 3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

The core machinery of phagoptosis can be divided into target marking, phagocyte recognition, engulfment, and degradative execution.

Pro-phagocytic signals can arise from naturally occurring molecules such as calreticulin or from Fc receptor-derived signals generated by immunoglobulins, including administered monoclonal antibodies like rituximab [40]. These changes do not themselves constitute execution. They alter how the phagocyte interprets the target.

PS can be recognized either directly or through bridging molecules. The supplied evidence describes multiple recognition systems, reflecting substantial redundancy rather than a single universal receptor. Bridging molecules including Gas6 and Protein S can simultaneously bind exposed PS and TAM-family receptor tyrosine kinases such as MerTK or Axl on the phagocyte [14-16].

TAM receptor activation represents one of the better-resolved engulfment modules. Ligand-dependent engagement promotes receptor dimerization and kinase activation, followed by signaling through cytoskeletal regulatory pathways involving molecules such as Vav1, Rac1, Cdc42, Rho-family signaling, FAK, and PLCγ-associated pathways [14-16]. These pathways reorganize actin around the target, extend a phagocytic cup, and ultimately produce complete enclosure.

PS can also be linked to αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins through bridging factors, whereas calreticulin can promote uptake through LRP1-associated mechanisms [12,20]. These pathways demonstrate that PS is part of a broader recognition network rather than an exclusive molecular code for phagoptosis.

Complement provides another major route, particularly in the central nervous system. C1q can associate with stressed neuronal structures, promote complement activation and C3 deposition, and facilitate recognition by microglial CR3/CD11b-dependent machinery [5,17]. Genetic or pharmacological interference with this pathway can prevent abnormal neuronal or synaptic removal in multiple experimental contexts [5,17,34-35].

After receptor engagement, actin remodeling progressively surrounds the target. Closure of the phagocytic cup creates an intracellular phagosome containing the viable or recently viable target. The phagosome then undergoes maturation through coordinated membrane trafficking and fusion with endosomal and lysosomal compartments [21].

The resulting phagolysosome is progressively acidified through V-ATPase activity and acquires lysosomal hydrolases and, in some contexts, oxidative antimicrobial or degradative chemistry [21-24]. It is this compartment, rather than a target-cell-specific pore-forming protein, that contains the best-supported execution machinery of mammalian phagoptosis.

## 4. Propagation and Amplification

Phagoptotic commitment emerges from integration of multiple positive and negative signals rather than a single linear amplification cascade.

One major amplification mechanism is the accumulation of multiple eat-me signals on a stressed target. PS externalization can cooperate with complement deposition, calreticulin exposure, desialylation, or diminished CD47-mediated protection to increase the probability that the phagocyte will initiate engulfment [1,12].

Chemotactic signals constitute another layer. Release of nucleotides or other find-me signals can increase phagocyte migration toward stressed cells, raising the local probability of target recognition [13,28]. Complement creates a particularly effective propagation mechanism in the CNS. Initial labeling of vulnerable neuronal or synaptic material with C1q can lead to downstream complement deposition and CR3-mediated microglial recognition [17,35]. Oxidative signaling can act on both sides of the interaction. Inhibition of UDP/P2Y6 purinergic signaling prevents phagocytosis, and TMEM16F mediates reversible PS exposure and phagoptosis in ischemia [6,42]. These two uses of ROS are mechanistically distinct: target-cell ROS may promote marking, whereas phagosomal ROS participates in cargo destruction.

The relative importance of these amplification mechanisms varies markedly between tissues. They should therefore be represented as alternative or cooperating modules rather than mandatory components of a single canonical pathway.

## 5. Protective/Negative Regulators

The clearest anti-phagocytic checkpoint is the CD47–SIRPα axis. CD47 expressed by the target interacts with SIRPα on the phagocyte and generates inhibitory signaling that prevents efficient engulfment despite the presence of pro-phagocytic signals [19].

SIRPα signaling interferes with integrin activation, actomyosin organization, macrophage spreading, and formation of an effective phagocytic synapse [19]. CD47 therefore functions as a major don't-eat-me signal. Reduction, loss, or therapeutic blockade of this signal lowers the threshold for phagocytosis, whereas high CD47 expression can protect viable cells, including tumor cells, from macrophage-mediated elimination [19-20].

Maintenance of phospholipid asymmetry provides a second protective mechanism. Functional ATP-dependent flippases retain PS predominantly within the cytoplasmic leaflet, thereby minimizing inappropriate recognition of viable cells [7-8]. Conversely, disruption of flippase activity or activation of scramblases increases surface PS and susceptibility to phagocytic recognition.

Sialylation-associated signaling also contributes to discrimination between protected and removable cellular material. Alterations in sialylation of microglial cell-surface glycoproteins can modulate complement receptor-dependent phagocytosis in microglial cells [31]. The exact contribution is context dependent rather than universally required.

Complement itself is subject to endogenous restraint. Neuronal pentraxin Nptx2 has been shown to restrict complement activity and microglia-mediated synaptic elimination in neurodegenerative contexts [32]. Loss of such local restraint can therefore shift the system toward inappropriate phagocytic removal.

Finally, simply eliminating or inhibiting the phagocyte is protective in experimental phagoptosis. This feature is mechanistically important because target survival after phagocyte depletion demonstrates that the target did not inevitably die through an autonomous execution program.

## 6. Commitment / Point of No Return

There is no universally defined molecular point of no return for phagoptosis.

Several models demonstrate that target-cell stress and marking remain reversible. Activated microglia can induce caspase activation and PS exposure in PC12 cells, yet blocking PS-dependent recognition, MFG-E8/integrin signaling, or P2Y6-dependent engulfment allows the stressed target population to survive [4]. Thus, caspase activity or PS externalization alone cannot be equated with irreversible execution.

Neurons in peri-infarct areas can reversibly expose phosphatidylserine and can survive long-term if phagocytosis is blocked [6]. Developing retinal ganglion cells can likewise persist when microglial elimination pathways are disrupted [5,33].

Complete phagosome closure is an attractive candidate for commitment, because it physically isolates the target from its extracellular environment. However, the evidence supplied does not establish closure alone as universally irreversible.

The strongest operational candidate for the point of no return is therefore functional maturation of the target-containing compartment into an acidic, enzyme-rich phagolysosome. In Drosophila testes, cyst cells remove progenitor germ cells through phagoptosis, with cyst cell-derived early/late endosomes and lysosomes fusing around live progenitors to acidify them before DNA fragmentation and substantial phosphatidylserine exposure on the germ cell surface [26]. Consequently, the broader commitment principle is exposure of a viable target to an irreversible phagocyte-generated lysosomal degradative environment, rather than phagosome closure as an absolute universal event.

## 7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

What physically kills the cell in phagoptosis?

In the best-supported mammalian mechanism, the target is killed by phagolysosomal destruction after engulfment [1,21-24].

Following internalization, the phagosome undergoes maturation and lysosomal fusion. V-ATPase-dependent proton transport lowers the luminal pH, lysosomal hydrolases gain access to the target, and oxidative chemistry can contribute to membrane and macromolecular damage [21-24].

The terminal lesion is therefore not activation of a dedicated target-cell pore-forming executor. Instead, the target is physically trapped inside a degradative organelle whose acidic, proteolytic, and frequently oxidative environment progressively destroys membranes, proteins, organelles, metabolic organization, and other cellular structures [1,21-24].

Experiments on phagosomal function support the importance of compartmental maturation. Effective killing depends on coordinated oxidative and non-oxidative phases, and disruption of phagosomal acidification or phagolysosomal killing can permit persistence of internalized material that would otherwise be destroyed [22-23].

Thus, acidification, lysosomal hydrolases, including cathepsin-containing degradative systems, and context-dependent oxidants are the best-supported execution machinery. They act collectively rather than through a single universal protein executor.

In Drosophila developmental phagoptosis, a related but topologically distinct mechanism has been demonstrated. Neighboring cyst cells generate early and late endosomes and then lysosomes that fuse around live spermatogonia, leading first to acidification within the germ cells and later to DNA fragmentation and degradation [26]. This is strong evidence that the defining principle is phagocyte-driven lysosomal execution rather than internalization itself.

## 8. Morphological Sequence

The most defensible chronological sequence begins with a morphologically viable cell or cellular structure.

A physiological or pathological stress then changes the target without necessarily destroying it. Presynaptic phosphatidylserine exposure is promoted by increased neuronal activity and is reversed to control levels when caspase activity is inhibited [27].

The phagocyte subsequently migrates toward the target and establishes direct contact. Recognition receptors accumulate at the contact site, actin reorganizes, and a phagocytic cup progressively extends around the target [12-16].

If the process remains reversible, interference at this stage can prevent removal and allow the target to persist [4-6].

With continued engulfment, the target becomes physically enclosed within the phagocyte. The newly formed phagosome matures, fuses with lysosomal compartments, becomes acidified, and accumulates hydrolytic and potentially oxidative activity [21-24].

Cellular architecture is then progressively degraded inside the phagocyte. Unlike many lytic RCD modalities, the target does not necessarily undergo extracellular plasma-membrane rupture as its defining terminal event. Instead, it can disappear within the degradative compartment of the phagocyte [1].

In Drosophila, live spermatogonia die by phagoptosis in which neighboring cyst cells generate endosomes and lysosomes that fuse around the live germ cells, causing acidification within them followed later by DNA fragmentation and degradation [26].

Thus, the morphology can be summarized as viable target → reversible stress marking → phagocyte contact → cup formation → engulfment or targeted extracellular lysosomal attack → acidification and enzymatic degradation → disappearance of the target.

## 9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

The most important differential diagnosis is efferocytosis.

In conventional efferocytosis, the target has already undergone irreversible intrinsic death before phagocytic clearance. Phagocytosis therefore removes the corpse but does not cause its death. Adding NSA to isolated PS-exposing necroptotic cells increased the live cell population over 24 hours [39]. This rescue criterion is the clearest mechanistic distinction.

Apoptosis presents a particularly important diagnostic challenge because PS exposure, caspase activity, and phagocytosis are all common during apoptotic clearance. However, PS exposure is not synonymous with irreversible apoptosis. TMEM16F-dependent PS externalization can occur reversibly in viable stressed cells [6,9-10], and sublethal caspase-3 activity can participate in marking neuronal material for complement-dependent phagocytosis without constituting classical apoptotic execution [27].

The distinction therefore requires determining whether target death would occur without the phagocyte. If caspase inhibition prevents death while phagocytosis continues only as clearance, the process is primarily apoptotic. Conversely, if inhibition or depletion of the phagocyte prevents target death despite persistence of sublethal stress markers, phagoptosis is supported.

Necroptotic and pyroptotic cells can also expose PS and subsequently undergo phagocytic clearance [29-30]. Consequently, Annexin V positivity or PS externalization cannot determine whether the primary execution mechanism was apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, or phagoptosis.

For necroptosis or pyroptosis, demonstration of the respective target-cell intrinsic execution machinery would indicate that phagocytosis is secondary unless blocking uptake preserves a target that otherwise remains viable. The same logic applies generally to other intrinsic forms of RCD.

Complement-mediated synaptic pruning requires additional caution. Phagocytic removal of an individual synapse is not automatically equivalent to whole-cell death. Complement-dependent synapse elimination has been proposed to be highly informative for the recognition and engulfment machinery of phagocytic elimination, but in some models it may only be classified as cellular phagoptosis when phagocyte-dependent uptake is considered causally responsible for loss of the viable cell itself [32,42].

Thus, morphology, PS exposure, complement deposition, caspase activity, or detection of a cell inside a phagocyte cannot by themselves establish phagoptosis. The essential differential criterion is causal dependence of target death on phagocytic execution.

## 10. Experimental Validation

The strongest proof of phagoptosis requires demonstrating that the target is viable before engulfment and would remain viable if engulfment were prevented.

The first requirement is therefore temporal evidence. Live-cell imaging should establish target-cell viability before and during initial phagocyte contact and demonstrate that internalization precedes irreversible target destruction. Static detection of cellular material inside macrophages or microglia is insufficient because it cannot determine whether the target died before or after uptake.

Second, the phagocyte should be experimentally removed, depleted, or functionally inhibited. If the target cell population survives or tissue function improves despite persistence of the original stress, this provides strong evidence that phagocytosis was causal rather than merely a clearance mechanism [36].

Third, target-recognition pathways should be perturbed. Depending on the experimental model, strong interventions include masking exposed PS, reducing TMEM16F-dependent PS externalization, disrupting MFG-E8/integrin signaling, inhibiting MerTK, blocking CR3, interfering with C1q/C3 signaling, or inhibiting P2Y6-dependent phagocytic activation [4-6,17-18,34].

Fourth, anti-phagocytic checkpoints can be manipulated. Enhancement of CD47–SIRPα signaling should suppress uptake, whereas blockade of this axis should lower the threshold for engulfment [19-20,38]. Such experiments test whether the balance between eat-me and don't-eat-me signals controls elimination.

Fifth, engulfment itself should be directly quantified by live imaging, microscopy, or appropriately designed flow-cytometric assays. The experiment should distinguish surface attachment from complete internalization.

Sixth, phagosome maturation should be assessed where execution is being studied. Measurements can include phagosome–lysosome fusion, compartment acidification, lysosomal activity, and oxidative chemistry [21-24]. If the model predicts intraphagolysosomal execution, inhibiting maturation or acidification should delay or reduce target destruction.

Seventh, competing intrinsic RCD mechanisms should be tested directly. Caspase activation, Annexin V staining, or PS positivity alone is insufficient. When apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, or another RCD mechanism is suspected, the relevant intrinsic machinery must be perturbed and compared with phagocytosis blockade [27,29-30].

A particularly strong experiment therefore has the following logic: the target displays reversible stress markers → remains alive before engulfment → is eliminated when an active phagocyte internalizes it → survives when phagocyte recognition or engulfment is blocked → and is destroyed following phagolysosomal maturation.

Transcriptomic enrichment of phagocytosis-associated or microglial activation genes cannot by itself demonstrate active phagoptosis. Such data indicate a phagocytosis-associated cellular state or susceptibility context but do not establish that viable targets are being killed by engulfment.

## 11. Molecular Hierarchy

Initiators/sensors: oxidative or nitrosative stress, Ca2+ elevation, ATP loss, limited caspase activity, infection, inflammation, ischemia, senescence, complement susceptibility, target-derived nucleotides, PS, calreticulin, and other context-dependent marking signals [4,6,12-13].

TMEM16F mediates reversible PS exposure and phagoptosis in ischemia; CD47–SIRPα signaling is anti-phagocytic and inhibits integrins; microglial desialylation stimulates CR3-mediated phagocytosis and neuron loss; Nptx2 regulates complement activity and restricts microglial synaptic elimination [6,19,31-32].

Phagocyte receptors and signal transducers: TAM receptors including MerTK and Axl; CR3 and other complement receptors; integrin-associated engulfment systems; LRP1; P2Y6; and other context-specific phagocytic receptors [12-18].

Core machinery: receptor-dependent actin remodeling → phagocytic cup formation → target internalization → phagosome maturation → lysosome fusion [14-16,21].

Executors: V-ATPase-dependent acidification, lysosomal hydrolases and cathepsin-associated degradation, with phagosomal ROS/RNS contributing in some contexts [21-24]. No single universal target-cell protein executor has been established.

Negative regulators: CD47–SIRPα signaling, maintenance of PS asymmetry by flippases, sialylation-associated anti-phagocytic signaling, and local complement restraint such as Nptx2 [6-8,19,31-32].

Biomarkers or supportive features: surface PS, Annexin V positivity, complement deposition, calreticulin exposure, local caspase activity, phagocyte contact, target internalization, and phagolysosomal localization. In some models, these findings have been proposed as evidence of phagoptosis, although none alone is considered sufficient to establish it [42].

## 12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

Physiological or pathological stress → reversible disturbance of the viable target cell → Ca2+ elevation, oxidative/nitrosative stress, ATP loss, limited caspase activity and/or complement susceptibility → disruption of phospholipid asymmetry and/or appearance of other pro-phagocytic surface signals → transient PS exposure through TMEM16F or other context-dependent marking mechanisms, often accompanied by calreticulin exposure, complement opsonization, desialylation or weakening of CD47-dependent protection → release of find-me signals and recruitment of a phagocyte → recognition of the marked target through TAM receptors, CR3, integrins, LRP1, P2Y6 or related pathways → Rac/Rho-family and actin-dependent phagocytic cup formation → engulfment of the still-viable target → phagosome formation → endolysosomal maturation and lysosome fusion → V-ATPase-dependent acidification plus hydrolase and context-dependent oxidant activity → progressive destruction of target membranes, organelles, metabolism and ionic homeostasis → irreversible loss of cellular integrity inside the phagolysosome → target-cell death and degradation [1,4,6,12-16,21-24].

In specialized Drosophila developmental contexts, the sequence can diverge after recognition: phagocyte-associated lysosomal machinery → extracellular/pericellular acidification and cathepsin exposure → target destruction → subsequent engulfment [25-26].

## 13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, phagoptosis should be operationally defined as an emerging, non-cell-autonomous regulated cell-death concept in which a cell that remains viable or recoverable before phagocytic attack is killed because a neighboring phagocyte recognizes, engulfs, and degrades it [1-4].

Its mechanistic identity is established by causality rather than by a unique biomarker. In phagoptosis, experimental inhibition of target recognition, phagocyte recruitment, engulfment, or phagocytic execution can preserve target cells that would otherwise be eliminated by phagocytes [36].

The canonical mammalian mechanism begins with reversible target-cell stress marking. PS exposure is particularly important and can arise transiently through Ca2+-activated TMEM16F, although PS is neither universally required nor sufficient by itself [6,9-11]. Additional eat-me signals, complement opsonization, calreticulin, receptor context, and loss of anti-phagocytic CD47 signaling determine whether a phagocyte commits to engulfment [12,17,19-20].

Following TAM receptor apoptotic cell tethering, cytoskeletal rearrangements induce phagocytosis [14]. The strongest current candidate for irreversible commitment is subsequent establishment of a mature degradative compartment rather than the appearance of PS or early phagocytic contact.

The terminal lesion in canonical mammalian phagoptosis is phagolysosomal destruction. Acidification, lysosomal hydrolases, and context-dependent oxidative chemistry progressively eliminate membrane integrity, ion gradients, metabolism, and organellar structure within the engulfed target [1,21-24]. Thus, the phagocyte itself supplies the principal execution machinery.

Evidence is strongest in mammalian microglia–neuron systems, developmental neuronal elimination, ischemic injury, complement-dependent CNS remodeling, and experimentally controlled phagocyte–target cocultures, with additional mechanistically instructive examples in Drosophila development [4-6,17,25-26,33-35].

The major unresolved question is not whether phagocytosis can kill viable cells, which is well supported, but what quantitative combination of target-side eat-me signals, inhibitory don't-eat-me signals, opsonization, phagocyte activation state, and tissue context determines the transition from reversible stress marking to irreversible phagocytic execution. No universal molecular threshold or single commitment receptor has yet been established.

Accordingly, phagoptosis should never be inferred from PS exposure, complement deposition, phagocyte activation, or gene-expression signatures alone. For RCDome, the operational requirement should be evidence that phagocytic intervention itself is necessary for target-cell death.
