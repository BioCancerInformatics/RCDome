## Anoikis

Anoikis is a specialized form of apoptosis initiated when anchorage-dependent cells lose appropriate contact with the extracellular matrix (ECM) or establish adhesion to an inappropriate matrix. Its defining characteristic is therefore the nature of the initiating stimulus rather than a unique terminal execution mechanism. In canonical anoikis, loss of cell–matrix adhesion is detected through disruption of integrin-dependent focal adhesion signaling, which removes survival signals and activates the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic machinery. Depending on cell type and physiological context, death-receptor signaling can provide an additional extrinsic apoptotic input [1-5]. Thus, anoikis is best understood as matrix-detachment-induced apoptosis rather than as a completely independent biochemical execution system.

Anoikis was originally described in epithelial cells in which disruption of cell–matrix interactions induced apoptosis [1]. This observation established an important biological principle: anchorage-dependent cells continuously monitor their interaction with the surrounding ECM, and appropriate adhesion functions not merely as a structural requirement but as an active survival signal. When this matrix surveillance system fails, the balance between pro-survival and pro-apoptotic signaling is progressively shifted toward mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, caspase activation, and apoptotic dismantling [1-4].

Under physiological conditions, anoikis contributes to tissue architecture by eliminating cells that become displaced from their correct extracellular environment. It participates in epithelial homeostasis, developmental remodeling, lumen formation, endothelial integrity, and prevention of survival at ectopic sites. This function is particularly important in cancer biology because resistance to anoikis allows malignant cells to survive after detaching from the primary tumor, remain viable during dissemination, and eventually colonize distant tissues. Acquisition of anchorage-independent survival is consequently an important component of metastatic progression [4-7,53].

The earliest canonical event in anoikis is disruption of integrin-mediated communication between the extracellular matrix and the intracellular cytoskeleton. Integrins are heterodimeric transmembrane adhesion receptors that bind ECM components such as fibronectin, laminin, and collagen and connect them to intracellular structural and signaling complexes. When cells are correctly attached, ligand-bound integrins cluster within focal adhesions and recruit proteins including talin, vinculin, paxillin, focal adhesion kinase (FAK), Src-family kinases, integrin-linked kinase (ILK), and additional adaptor proteins [3-4,8]. These complexes simultaneously generate mechanical anchorage and activate intracellular survival pathways.

Correct integrin engagement therefore establishes a survival state characterized by FAK and Src activation and downstream stimulation of pathways including PI3K–AKT and RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK. These signaling networks maintain cellular metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, growth-factor cooperation, and suppression of pro-apoptotic proteins. Loss of anchorage inactivates focal complex signaling molecules including FAK and Src, disrupting pro-survival pathways such as PI3K/Akt and Raf/MEK/ERK, and is followed by disassembly of focal adhesions and destabilization of the cytoskeleton [9].

The initiating sequence can therefore be represented as loss of appropriate ECM contact → integrin disengagement → focal adhesion disassembly → reduction of FAK/Src/ILK signaling → weakening of PI3K–AKT and ERK-mediated survival signaling. This collapse removes several inhibitory mechanisms that normally restrain the apoptotic machinery.

FAK occupies a particularly important position in this matrix-surveillance network. When integrins engage the ECM, FAK undergoes activation and serves as a signaling platform that recruits Src and other effectors, allowing transmission of adhesion-dependent survival signals. FAK–Src activity promotes PI3K–AKT signaling, ERK activation, cytoskeletal organization, and resistance to apoptosis. When adhesion is lost, reduced FAK signaling contributes directly to anoikis sensitivity [4,8-10]. Conversely, persistent FAK activity is a common mechanism by which cancer cells acquire anoikis resistance.

Integrin signaling does not terminate entirely at the plasma membrane. Internalized integrins can continue signaling from endosomal compartments, and such endosomal integrin signaling can suppress anoikis [11]. This demonstrates that the critical variable is not simply whether integrins are physically present at the cell surface, but whether sufficient adhesion-associated signaling remains active to maintain the survival program.

Loss of FAK/Src signaling leads to attenuation of PI3K–AKT activity. AKT normally phosphorylates multiple pro-apoptotic targets and maintains cellular survival through both transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms. Among its effects, AKT suppresses FOXO-dependent transcription of pro-apoptotic genes, inhibits activation of selected BH3-only proteins, supports metabolism, and limits components of death-receptor signaling. Consequently, reduced AKT activity following ECM detachment shifts the cell toward mitochondrial apoptotic priming [3-4,12,51].

ERK signaling provides a parallel survival checkpoint. Integrin-mediated adhesion frequently cooperates with receptor tyrosine kinases such as EGFR to sustain RAS–ERK signaling. ERK can reduce anoikis susceptibility by controlling expression, phosphorylation, and degradation of pro-apoptotic proteins, particularly BIM. Detachment disrupts pro-survival pathways such as PI3K/Akt and Raf/MEK/ERK, and the resulting activation of apoptotic kinases including JNK increases the expression and phosphorylation of the pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins BIM and BMF [9].

Thus, the early biochemical transition toward anoikis consists not simply of the activation of a single death receptor but of the coordinated disappearance of adhesion-dependent survival signals. Integrin disengagement simultaneously reduces FAK/Src signaling, PI3K–AKT activity, ERK-dependent survival, and cytoskeletal integrity. Together, these changes allow activation of the BCL-2-family network that controls mitochondrial apoptosis.

A particularly important connection between adhesion and mitochondrial apoptosis is provided by the BH3-only proteins BIM and BMF. These proteins function as stress-responsive pro-apoptotic members of the BCL-2 family and connect cytoskeletal disruption with activation of BAX and BAK.

BMF provides one of the clearest examples of direct cytoskeletal regulation of anoikis. In attached cells, BMF can be sequestered through association with dynein light chain 2 and the myosin V actin motor complex. This localization restrains its pro-apoptotic activity. Disruption of cellular attachment and cytoskeletal organization releases BMF from this complex, allowing it to interact with anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins and promote mitochondrial apoptotic signaling [13].

BIM is similarly regulated by adhesion-dependent signaling and cytoskeletal organization. Loss of integrin and growth-factor signaling can increase BIM abundance through transcriptional mechanisms and reduced ERK-mediated degradation. In several epithelial systems, BIM therefore accumulates after detachment and contributes directly to activation of the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [49].

Stress-activated mitogen-activated protein kinases reinforce this transition. Detachment can activate JNK and p38 MAPK signaling, although the relative importance of these pathways varies according to cell type. JNK can promote anoikis by increasing transcription and activation of BH3-only proteins, including BIM and BMF [14]. p38 can also enhance BMF activity. A particularly well-characterized mechanism involves phosphorylation of BMF at Thr72 by p38 MAPK, which disrupts interaction between BMF and its cytoskeletal binding partner and promotes its release into the cytoplasm, thereby increasing its pro-apoptotic activity [15].

Cytoskeletal stress can additionally activate Rho-family signaling and ROCK-dependent pathways. In specific experimental models, impairment of integrin–FAK signaling changes RhoA regulation and activates a RhoA–ROCK–MAPK stress response that converges on BH3-only proteins and mitochondrial apoptosis [16]. These stress-kinase pathways should therefore be viewed as amplifiers that translate structural disruption into apoptotic priming rather than as universal anoikis execution systems.

Mechanical changes accompanying detachment also influence the Hippo–YAP/TAZ pathway. In adherent cells, actin organization and mechanical tension can favor nuclear activity of YAP and TAZ, transcriptional regulators that support proliferation and survival. Loss of attachment reorganizes the actin cytoskeleton and can activate Hippo signaling through LATS1/2 kinases. Activated LATS1/2 phosphorylate YAP, promoting its cytoplasmic retention and functional inhibition [17]. In non-transformed epithelial cells, this detachment-induced inhibition of YAP contributes to anoikis.

The sequence in this mechanosensitive branch can therefore be represented as ECM detachment → cytoskeletal reorganization → activation of the Hippo kinase network → LATS1/2 activation → YAP inhibition → loss of YAP-dependent survival transcription → increased susceptibility to apoptosis [17]. In cancer cells, persistent YAP/TAZ activity despite detachment can contribute to anoikis resistance.

The combined loss of AKT and ERK survival signaling, release or induction of BIM and BMF, activation of stress MAPKs, and loss of YAP-dependent transcription shift the balance of the BCL-2-family network toward apoptosis. Anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2 and BCL-XL normally restrain mitochondrial permeabilization by binding pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins and BAX/BAK. Anoikis-associated BH3-only proteins neutralize these anti-apoptotic factors and/or promote activation of BAX and BAK [3,18-20,46].

BAX and BAK then undergo conformational activation and oligomerization within the outer mitochondrial membrane. Their oligomeric assemblies generate mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), allowing proteins normally confined to the mitochondrial intermembrane space to enter the cytosol [18-20].

MOMP represents the major convergence point of the intrinsic anoikis pathway. One of its most important consequences is release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm. Cytosolic cytochrome c binds APAF1 in the presence of nucleotide cofactors, promoting formation of the apoptosome. The apoptosome recruits procaspase-9 and facilitates its activation, generating active caspase-9 [18,21,47].

Caspase-9 subsequently activates the executioner caspases, especially caspase-3 and caspase-7. These proteases cleave a large number of structural, nuclear, metabolic, and signaling proteins and initiate the characteristic cellular dismantling associated with apoptosis. Thus, the principal intrinsic anoikis sequence is integrin disengagement → loss of FAK/Src/PI3K/AKT/ERK signaling → activation of BIM/BMF and related BH3-only proteins → BAX/BAK oligomerization → MOMP → cytochrome c release → apoptosome formation → caspase-9 activation → caspase-3/7 activation → apoptotic cellular destruction.

The death-receptor pathway provides a second route into anoikis in several cell types. Detachment can modify expression, clustering, localization, or signaling competence of death receptors including FAS and death receptor 5 (DR5) [22-25]. In endothelial cells, matrix attachment can regulate sensitivity to FAS-mediated apoptosis, demonstrating that integrin signaling controls not only mitochondrial apoptosis but also death-receptor signaling [22].

In some epithelial systems, detachment activates p38 MAPK and increases expression of FAS ligand, creating an autocrine or juxtacrine death-receptor signal [23]. In colorectal carcinoma cells, DR5 has been implicated directly in anoikis, with loss of adhesion facilitating DR5-dependent activation of the apoptotic machinery [24].

After death-receptor activation, the intracellular death domain recruits the adaptor FADD, which in turn recruits procaspase-8. Assembly of this death-inducing signaling complex promotes proximity-dependent activation of caspase-8. Activated caspase-8 can then cleave and activate executioner caspases directly or amplify mitochondrial apoptosis through cleavage of the BH3-only protein BID [21-25].

Truncated BID translocates to mitochondria and promotes activation of BAX and BAK, connecting extrinsic death-receptor signaling with the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway. Full-length BID can also undergo mitochondrial translocation during anoikis in some epithelial systems, further illustrating the integration between adhesion loss, death receptors, and mitochondrial apoptosis [26].

Death-receptor signaling is nevertheless not universally required for anoikis. Classic studies demonstrated that FADD and caspase-8 can be required during detachment-induced apoptosis even in circumstances where conventional ligand-dependent FAS, DR4, or DR5 activation was not clearly demonstrated [25]. Thus, anoikis can use death-domain proteins in a context-dependent manner, but death receptors should not be considered universal upstream sensors of ECM detachment.

A second regulatory layer controls caspase-8 activation. In adherent cells, ERK signaling can maintain expression of the caspase-8 inhibitor c-FLIP, thereby limiting death-receptor-mediated apoptosis [22]. Loss of attachment and ERK activity can reduce this protective input, increasing the ability of FADD-containing complexes to activate caspase-8.

DAP3 provides another connection between adhesion-dependent survival and the extrinsic pathway. AKT-mediated phosphorylation of DAP3 restrains its pro-apoptotic activity. Following detachment and reduced AKT signaling, this inhibition can be relieved, allowing DAP3 to cooperate with FADD and promote anoikis in relevant systems [27].

The intrinsic and extrinsic branches therefore frequently cooperate rather than function as mutually exclusive mechanisms. Death-receptor-dependent caspase-8 activation can increase mitochondrial priming through BID, while mitochondrial cytochrome c release activates caspase-9. Both pathways ultimately converge on caspase-3 and caspase-7 and generate the same apoptotic execution program.

Several cellular systems actively oppose this transition and create a reversible phase during which detached cells can still survive. The dominant protective architecture consists of continued integrin/FAK/Src/PI3K/AKT/ERK signaling. Persistent activation of these pathways can suppress BIM and BMF, maintain anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family activity, restrain caspase-8 signaling, and maintain cellular metabolism. Cancer cells frequently exploit precisely these pathways to acquire anchorage-independent survival [4,6,9].

BCL-2 and BCL-XL constitute another major checkpoint. By binding activated BH3-only proteins and/or restraining BAX and BAK, these proteins prevent mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization. Experimental overexpression of BCL-2 or BCL-XL can therefore protect anoikis-sensitive cells after detachment, demonstrating that the mitochondrial pathway is a critical determinant of death commitment [1,28].

Detachment also generates substantial metabolic stress. Attached cells receive both growth-factor and matrix-derived signals that support glucose uptake, mitochondrial metabolism, ATP production, and redox homeostasis. Removal from the ECM can therefore decrease nutrient utilization efficiency and increase oxidative stress. In several epithelial and endothelial systems, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species increase after detachment [50].

The role of ROS in anoikis is complex because moderate ROS can also participate in adaptive survival signaling. Nevertheless, experimentally increasing antioxidant capacity can protect certain detached cells, demonstrating that excessive oxidative stress contributes to anoikis in some contexts [29-30]. Cancer cells that upregulate antioxidant enzymes can therefore acquire resistance to detachment-associated oxidative stress.

Autophagy is another major adaptive response. ECM detachment can rapidly activate autophagy, allowing cells to recycle intracellular components, maintain ATP levels, remove damaged organelles, and limit accumulation of reactive oxygen species [31-33]. In many experimental models, autophagy delays rather than executes anoikis.

One important regulatory pathway involves PERK. ECM detachment can activate the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 stress response, which promotes antioxidant adaptation and autophagy [31]. PERK can selectively inhibit mTORC1 during ECM detachment, further facilitating autophagic survival [32]. Consequently, PERK-driven autophagy can increase the time detached cells remain viable before mitochondrial apoptotic commitment.

The IKK complex can independently promote detachment-induced autophagy in mammary epithelial cells [33]. This provides another mechanism through which matrix-deprived cells temporarily maintain metabolic and organellar homeostasis despite loss of normal adhesion signaling.

Detachment-induced autophagy should therefore not be interpreted automatically as autophagy-dependent cell death. In anoikis-sensitive systems, autophagy frequently functions as a resistance mechanism that delays apoptosis. Inhibition of autophagy can consequently accelerate anoikis, whereas increased autophagic capacity can facilitate survival of disseminating cancer cells [31-33].

Metabolic adaptation is especially relevant in cancer. Tumor cells capable of surviving ECM detachment frequently remodel glucose metabolism, mitochondrial function, redox buffering, and antioxidant pathways. Oncogenic RAS signaling can modify these metabolic responses and promote survival under anchorage-independent conditions [34]. Similarly, cancer-associated activation of antioxidant enzymes or ROS-dependent signaling pathways can establish anoikis resistance.

There is no universally accepted anoikis-specific molecular point of no return because the terminal execution machinery is the same machinery used by intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis. BAX and BAK are BCL-2 effector proteins and MOMP executioners, and cells lacking both proteins are often unable to engage MOMP [19]. Once sufficient mitochondrial permeabilization has occurred, cytochrome c release and apoptosome assembly strongly favor complete caspase activation and cell death.

However, MOMP should not be considered absolutely irreversible at the level of every individual mitochondrion or cell. Modern apoptosis studies demonstrate that limited or incomplete mitochondrial permeabilization and sublethal cytochrome c release can occasionally occur without immediate cell death [35,44]. Consequently, commitment is better defined as sufficiently extensive MOMP combined with robust executioner-caspase activation rather than the first detectable mitochondrial permeabilization event.

Once executioner caspases are extensively activated, the terminal phase of anoikis is the conventional apoptotic dismantling program. Caspase-3 and caspase-7 cleave hundreds of cellular substrates involved in cytoskeletal architecture, nuclear organization, DNA repair, metabolism, signaling, and membrane structure.

Importantly, focal adhesion proteins themselves become caspase substrates. Integrin signaling, AKT/FAK, BMF/BIM, and anoikis mechanisms are involved in cell survival and anoikis [51]. At this stage, matrix-survival signaling can no longer be restored simply by reattachment because the structural and signaling proteins required to interpret adhesion have already been dismantled.

Morphologically, anoikis follows the characteristic progression of apoptosis. Cells first lose substrate spreading and adopt a rounded morphology because attachment-dependent cytoskeletal tension collapses. Executioner caspases mediate hallmark apoptotic features including DNA fragmentation, proteolytic cleavage of nuclear and cytoskeletal proteins, membrane blebbing, apoptotic body formation, and regulated exposure of phagocytic eat-me signals [48].

The plasma membrane usually remains sufficiently intact during early and intermediate apoptosis to prevent uncontrolled release of intracellular contents. Phosphatidylserine becomes exposed on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, providing a recognition signal for phagocytic clearance. If apoptotic cells are not efficiently removed, secondary membrane disruption may eventually occur, but this is not the primary execution mechanism of anoikis.

Anoikis is therefore fundamentally different from ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis at the terminal stage. Ferroptosis is driven by catastrophic phospholipid peroxidation, necroptosis uses RIPK3–MLKL-dependent membrane permeabilization, and pyroptosis uses gasdermin pores. Anoikis instead terminates through the proteolytic machinery of apoptosis.

Not every cell that dies after ECM detachment undergoes anoikis. This distinction is particularly important for mechanistic classification. Detachment can activate multiple forms of stress and cell death depending on cell type, genetic background, metabolic state, and interaction with neighboring cells.

Entosis represents one such alternative. Matrix-detached epithelial cells can undergo cell-in-cell invasion, in which one viable cell enters another and can subsequently be killed through lysosome-dependent mechanisms [36]. Because this process is non-apoptotic, entotic death following detachment should not be classified as anoikis.

Similarly, some mammary epithelial cell populations undergo predominantly non-apoptotic death following loss of anchorage under particular FAK-signaling conditions [37]. Therefore, detachment itself is not sufficient to establish anoikis. The term should be reserved for apoptosis that is causally triggered by inappropriate or absent matrix adhesion.

Other non-classical pathways can also influence the fate of detached cells. RIPK1-mediated signaling can alter mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy in ECM-deprived cells, affecting their survival without necessarily converting canonical anoikis into necroptosis [38,52]. Such findings illustrate the extensive crosstalk between adhesion, metabolic stress, mitochondrial function, autophagy, and regulated cell death.

The Hippo–YAP pathway provides another major resistance mechanism in cancer. Whereas detachment normally activates Hippo signaling and suppresses YAP, malignant cells can retain YAP/TAZ activity despite reduced adhesion. Persistent YAP/TAZ signaling supports proliferation, focal adhesion organization, cytoskeletal plasticity, and survival transcription, allowing tumor cells to bypass matrix surveillance [17,39].

Cancer cells can also preserve integrin-dependent signaling after physical detachment through integrin trafficking, altered ECM production, cell aggregation, growth-factor receptor activation, or constitutive FAK/Src signaling. For example, cooperation between integrins and EGFR/RAS signaling can maintain epithelial survival and architecture, whereas aberrant integrin–EGFR–FAK–AKT signaling can promote anchorage-independent growth in tumor cells [9,40].

Anoikis resistance therefore represents a multi-layered phenotype rather than inhibition of a single death protein. It can arise through persistent integrin/FAK signaling, constitutive PI3K–AKT activity, ERK activation, overexpression of BCL-2-family survival proteins, increased antioxidant capacity, enhanced autophagy, YAP/TAZ activation, metabolic reprogramming, death-receptor suppression, or combinations of these mechanisms [4-7].

Experimentally, anoikis is demonstrated by showing that loss of appropriate substrate adhesion specifically induces apoptosis. Cells are commonly placed in suspension or cultured on non-adhesive substrates such as poly-HEMA to prevent integrin-mediated attachment. Apoptosis is then assessed using complementary measurements such as Annexin V binding, executioner-caspase activation, PARP cleavage, DNA fragmentation, nuclear morphology, and apoptotic-body formation [41-43].

Annexin V positivity alone is not sufficient to establish anoikis because phosphatidylserine exposure can accompany multiple cell-death states. Similarly, detachment alone cannot define the process. Strong mechanistic evidence requires demonstrating both that apoptosis occurs and that the apoptosis is specifically caused by loss or alteration of matrix adhesion.

Causal evidence is strengthened by experimental rescue. Restoration of appropriate integrin ligation or activation of FAK, AKT, or ERK can suppress anoikis in systems dependent on these pathways. Overexpression of BCL-2 or BCL-XL can prevent mitochondrial commitment, whereas knockdown of BIM or BMF can reduce detachment-induced apoptosis in appropriate models [45].

In death-receptor-dependent systems, dominant-negative FADD or inhibition of caspase-8 can suppress anoikis [22-25]. Antioxidants can rescue cells in ROS-dependent models, while enhanced autophagy can prolong survival during ECM deprivation [29-33]. These findings illustrate that different upstream modules can dominate in different cellular contexts even though the canonical terminal pathway remains apoptotic.

The most integrated mechanistic sequence of canonical anoikis can therefore be represented as loss of appropriate ECM attachment or adhesion to an inappropriate matrix → integrin disengagement and focal adhesion disassembly → reduction of FAK/Src/ILK signaling → collapse of PI3K–AKT and ERK survival pathways together with cytoskeletal reorganization → activation and release of BH3-only proteins, particularly BIM and BMF → stress-kinase amplification through JNK and p38 and, in many non-transformed cells, Hippo/LATS-dependent suppression of YAP → neutralization of anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins and activation of BAX/BAK → mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization → cytochrome c release → APAF1 apoptosome formation → caspase-9 activation → caspase-3 and caspase-7 activation → proteolytic cleavage of focal adhesion, cytoskeletal, nuclear, and signaling proteins → cellular shrinkage, membrane blebbing, chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, and apoptotic-body formation → irreversible apoptotic cell death.

In selected cell types, an additional sequence can operate in parallel: ECM detachment → altered FAS/FASL or DR5 signaling and reduced adhesion-dependent inhibition of death-receptor pathways → FADD recruitment → caspase-8 activation → direct executioner-caspase activation and/or BID-mediated mitochondrial amplification → BAX/BAK-dependent MOMP → apoptosome and executioner-caspase activation → apoptosis.

Thus, the defining mechanistic feature of anoikis is not a unique executioner protein. Anoikis is the activation of canonical apoptotic machinery specifically because matrix-surveillance signaling has failed. Integrins and focal adhesions function as the principal sensors of appropriate anchorage; FAK, Src, PI3K, AKT, ERK, cytoskeletal complexes, and Hippo/YAP signaling determine whether detachment can be tolerated; BIM, BMF, BAX, and BAK connect loss of adhesion with mitochondrial permeabilization; FADD and caspase-8 provide an additional context-dependent extrinsic input; and caspase-9, caspase-3, and caspase-7 execute the final apoptotic program. Consequently, anoikis should be distinguished from other regulated cell-death modalities primarily by its upstream initiating context: inappropriate or absent interaction between an anchorage-dependent cell and its extracellular matrix.
