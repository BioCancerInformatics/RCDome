AUTOPHAGY


Autophagy, in the macroautophagy sense used here, is an established lysosome-dependent intracellular degradation and recycling process that maintains cellular homeostasis by sequestering cytoplasmic material within double-membrane autophagosomes and delivering that material to lysosomes for degradation [1,2].

For RCDome, canonical macroautophagy should be classified as a cellular process / RCD-related concept, not as a regulated cell-death modality. Basal macroautophagy contributes to proteostasis, organelle quality control, nutrient recycling, and removal of damaged or potentially harmful cellular material. Stress can increase autophagic activity during nutrient deprivation, energetic stress, hypoxia, infection, protein aggregation, and organelle damage, frequently as an adaptive or cytoprotective response [1–3].

This classification must be kept separate from autophagy-dependent cell death (ADCD). The Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death defines ADCD as regulated cell death that mechanistically depends on the autophagic machinery or components thereof [19]. The mere presence of autophagosomes, LC3 lipidation, p62/SQSTM1 changes, lysosomal activity, or extensive vacuolization does not establish ADCD. In most stress contexts, autophagy functions as an adaptive pathway rather than as a death mechanism [17,19,20].

Accordingly, the defining endpoint of macroautophagy is normally cargo degradation and recycling, not cellular death.


2. INITIATION AND TRIGGERS

Macroautophagy operates at basal levels and can be upregulated by nutrient withdrawal, energy stress, hypoxia, oxidative stress, infection, proteotoxic stress, and organelle damage [1–3].

Nutrient and growth-factor availability is integrated prominently at lysosomal mTORC1. Under nutrient-rich conditions, amino-acid sensing through Rag-family GTPases, Ragulator, lysosomal nutrient sensors including SLC38A9, and growth-factor-dependent RHEB signaling supports mTORC1 activity. Active mTORC1 promotes anabolic metabolism and suppresses autophagy initiation [2,3].

Upon amino-acid deprivation or related stresses, mTORC1 activity falls and inhibition of the autophagy-initiation machinery is relieved. AMPK can promote autophagy during energetic stress through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of mTORC1 and direct regulation of autophagy proteins. However, the exact relationship between AMPK, ULK1, nutrient state, and timing is context-dependent; therefore, AMPK should be described as an important regulator rather than as an obligatory universal on-switch for every form of macroautophagy [3].

The central mammalian initiation module is the ULK complex, composed of ULK1 or ULK2, FIP200/RB1CC1, ATG13, and ATG101 [1,4]. During starvation-induced macroautophagy, suppression of mTORC1 permits activation and recruitment of this complex to sites associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and nascent phagophore formation [1,4].

Recent structural work supports direct physical coupling between the ULK1 complex and class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex I (PI3KC3-C1). The human ULK1C:PI3KC3-C1 supercomplex contains extensive contacts involving the FIP200 scaffold and PI3KC3-C1 components and provides a structural model for coordinated autophagy initiation [5]. This result strengthens the physical link between the two major initiation modules, but should not be interpreted as meaning that one rigid supercomplex architecture is obligatory in every autophagy context.


3. CORE MECHANISTIC MACHINERY

Immediately downstream of initiation, the autophagy-specific class III PI3-kinase complex I generates phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) at nascent autophagic membranes.

The core mammalian PI3KC3-C1 complex consists of:
- VPS34/PIK3C3, the lipid kinase;
- VPS15/PIK3R4;
- BECN1/Beclin 1;
- ATG14.

Additional proteins such as NRBF2 and AMBRA1 regulate this machinery in specific settings, but should not be presented as equivalent stoichiometric members of the universally conserved PI3KC3-C1 core [1,4].

PI3P production recruits PI3P-binding effectors including WIPI-family proteins and promotes assembly of the machinery required for phagophore nucleation and expansion [1,4].

Membrane expansion depends on coordinated lipid supply and bilayer equilibration. ATG2 proteins act as long lipid-transfer proteins that bridge membranes and support lipid movement from the ER toward the growing phagophore. Biochemical evidence supports TMEM41B and VMP1 at the ER and ATG9 at autophagic membranes as lipid scramblases capable of re-equilibrating phospholipids between membrane leaflets [6]. This provides a mechanistic framework in which ATG2-mediated lipid transfer and scramblase activity cooperate to expand the phagophore without creating extreme leaflet imbalance.

ATG8-family lipidation is another central module. WIPI2 recruits the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 E3-like machinery to PI3P-positive phagophore membranes. ATG12 is activated by ATG7 and transferred through ATG10 before conjugation to ATG5. In parallel, ATG4 processing exposes the C-terminal glycine of LC3/GABARAP-family proteins; ATG7 and ATG3 then mediate transfer of these ATG8 proteins to membrane phospholipids, principally phosphatidylethanolamine [1,7,8].

Recent reconstitution studies directly support a multistep docking process in which WIPI2, ATG16L1, and ATG3 position the LC3 lipidation machinery at the phagophore [7]. Lipidated LC3/GABARAP-family proteins participate in membrane organization, cargo capture, and later stages of autophagosome maturation, but individual ATG8-family proteins are not functionally interchangeable in every context.


4. PROPAGATION AND AMPLIFICATION

Macroautophagy is not propagated by a single feed-forward execution cascade analogous to an RCD pathway. Instead, its progression consists of coordinated membrane growth, cargo capture, autophagosome sealing, trafficking, lysosomal fusion, and degradation.

Bulk autophagy can sequester cytoplasmic material relatively non-selectively during starvation. Selective autophagy uses dedicated receptors and adaptors to capture defined cargoes, including protein aggregates, damaged organelles, lysosomes, ER fragments, and intracellular pathogens [9].

Well-characterized ubiquitin-binding selective-autophagy receptors include p62/SQSTM1, NBR1, NDP52/CALCOCO2, OPTN, and TAX1BP1. These receptors can connect ubiquitinated cargo to ATG8-family proteins and, in some settings, recruit upstream autophagy-initiation factors directly to the cargo [9].

Mitophagy illustrates selective-autophagy logic. In PINK1–Parkin-dependent mitochondrial quality control, mitochondrial damage stabilizes PINK1 and promotes Parkin-dependent ubiquitination, which supports recruitment and activation of autophagy receptors including OPTN and NDP52 [10]. This is an important mitophagy pathway, but it is not the only mitophagy route; receptor-mediated, Parkin-independent pathways also exist. Therefore, PINK1–Parkin should be treated as a major model of selective autophagy rather than as a universal mitophagy mechanism.

Cargo recognition can therefore locally concentrate the core autophagy machinery and couple substrate selection to de novo phagophore formation. This local assembly is a major mechanistic distinction between selective and starvation-induced bulk autophagy.


5. PROTECTIVE AND NEGATIVE REGULATORS

The dominant physiological role of macroautophagy is homeostatic and frequently cytoprotective.

mTORC1 is a major negative regulator of autophagy initiation under nutrient-rich conditions [2,3]. By restraining the ULK complex and coordinating lysosomal/anabolic programs, mTORC1 prevents unnecessary activation of catabolic recycling.

Autophagy is also self-limiting through restoration of nutrient availability. Lysosomal degradation releases amino acids and other metabolites that can contribute to reactivation of nutrient-sensing pathways, including mTORC1 [2,16]. Thus, pathway termination is coupled to the metabolic success of the degradative process.

At later stages, mTORC1 can also regulate autophagosome maturation. In one experimentally defined mechanism, mTORC1-mediated phosphorylation of VAMP8 inhibits recruitment of SCFD1 and suppresses assembly of the STX17–SNAP29–VAMP8 fusion machinery [14]. This is an experimentally supported regulatory mechanism, but it should be treated as one layer of late-stage regulation rather than the universal controller of all lysosomal fusion events.

Because macroautophagy is usually adaptive, suppression of autophagy can increase rather than decrease susceptibility to stress-induced cell death. This principle is central to distinguishing canonical autophagy from ADCD [19,20].


6. COMMITMENT / POINT OF NO RETURN

A cell-death “point of no return” does not apply to canonical macroautophagy.

Macroautophagy is a dynamic degradative pathway, not by default a trajectory toward cellular demise. Individual autophagic structures undergo topological and biochemical transitions, such as phagophore closure, lysosomal fusion, and cargo degradation, but these events do not constitute commitment of the cell to death.

At the organelle level, closure of the phagophore creates a sealed double-membrane autophagosome. ESCRT machinery has a demonstrated role in autophagosome sealing in experimental systems: depletion of ESCRT components can cause accumulation of open autophagic structures [11]. However, autophagosome closure is a pathway-maturation step, not a cellular point of no return.

Accordingly, RCDome should explicitly state:

Canonical macroautophagy has no established cell-death commitment point because cellular death is not its intrinsic endpoint.

If a study proposes autophagy-dependent cell death, the commitment question must be evaluated separately within that death model and cannot be inferred from activation of canonical macroautophagy itself.


7. EXECUTION AND TERMINAL CAUSE OF DEATH

For canonical macroautophagy, “What physically kills the cell?” is not an applicable mechanistic question.

The terminal biochemical outcome of the canonical pathway is:
autophagosome–lysosome fusion → degradation of sequestered material → metabolite export/recycling → restoration or remodeling of cellular homeostasis.

After autophagosome closure, RAB-family GTPases, tethering proteins, and SNARE complexes coordinate transport, docking, and fusion with lysosomes [12]. Established mammalian fusion machinery includes STX17-containing complexes and a parallel YKT6-dependent pathway. Recent open-access primary evidence also identifies STX17–SNAP47–VAMP7/VAMP8 as a major fusion complex functioning in both selective and starvation-induced autophagy [13]. These findings demonstrate that fusion machinery is partly context-dependent rather than reducible to one universal SNARE complex.

Inside the autolysosome, lysosomal hydrolases degrade the inner autophagosomal membrane and cargo. Cathepsins function collectively and partly redundantly in lysosomal proteolysis [15]. The resulting amino acids, lipids, sugars, and other metabolites can return to the cytosol and support cellular metabolism [2].

Thus, the canonical pathway executes degradation, not death.

Autophagy-dependent cell death must be diagnosed separately. According to the NCCD, ADCD requires a mechanistic dependence of cell death on the autophagy machinery; autophagy occurring alongside death or facilitating a different RCD modality is not sufficient [19]. Genetic or otherwise specific disruption of multiple autophagy components should suppress the death phenotype before a causal ADCD interpretation is accepted [17,19,20].


8. MORPHOLOGICAL SEQUENCE

The canonical morphological sequence of macroautophagy is a membrane-remodeling sequence rather than a death morphology.

A simplified sequence is:

cytoplasm under basal or stress conditions
→ recruitment/activation of initiation machinery at ER-associated sites
→ formation of a PI3P-enriched phagophore/omegasome-associated membrane
→ expansion of the cup-shaped phagophore
→ ATG8-family lipidation and cargo capture
→ completion and sealing of the double-membrane autophagosome
→ trafficking and lysosomal docking
→ autophagosome–lysosome fusion
→ formation of an autolysosome
→ degradation of the inner autophagosomal membrane and cargo
→ metabolite release and lysosomal recovery/reformation [1,2,11,12,16].

This sequence must not be confused with cell-death morphology. Extensive vacuolization or a high number of autophagosomes can occur in stressed or dying cells without demonstrating that autophagy is the cause of death [17,19,20].

Furthermore, accumulation of autophagosomes can result either from increased formation or from failure of closure, fusion, acidification, or lysosomal degradation. Therefore, static morphology alone cannot determine whether autophagic flux has increased.


9. CROSSTALK AND DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Autophagy versus autophagy-dependent cell death:
Canonical autophagy is principally a homeostatic lysosomal recycling process. ADCD is an RCD category whose occurrence requires causal dependence of cell death on autophagy machinery [19,20]. Activation of autophagy during cell stress is not equivalent to ADCD.

Autophagy-associated cell death:
This term describes situations in which autophagy and death occur together without establishing that autophagy is the lethal mechanism. Such association should not be upgraded to ADCD without perturbational evidence [19,20].

Autophagy facilitating another RCD:
Autophagy can alter substrate availability, organelle quality, iron metabolism, inflammatory signaling, or death-receptor pathways and thereby modify another RCD modality. In such cases, the final death mechanism should be assigned to the actual executing RCD pathway rather than to autophagy itself [19].

Autosis:
Autosis is a specific proposed subtype/instance of autophagy-dependent cell death associated with distinctive morphological and mechanistic criteria, including dependence on Na+/K+-ATPase. It should not be used as a synonym for extensive autophagy or autophagic vacuolization [19].

Macroautophagy versus other autophagy pathways:
Macroautophagy uses double-membrane autophagosomes. Microautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy deliver substrates to lysosomes through different mechanisms. The present mechanistic description should therefore not be generalized to every process called “autophagy” [1].

Canonical versus noncanonical ATG functions:
Some ATG proteins participate in noncanonical membrane-trafficking or immune processes. Therefore, dependence on a single ATG protein does not necessarily establish canonical macroautophagy. This is another reason why genetic validation should target more than one mechanistically distinct autophagy component when causality is important [17].


10. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION

Autophagy should be demonstrated as dynamic flux rather than inferred from a static marker.

The 4th-edition autophagy guidelines emphasize that no individual assay is sufficient in every context and recommend complementary approaches [17].

LC3:
Increased LC3-II or LC3 puncta demonstrates increased abundance of lipidated LC3/autophagic structures, but cannot by itself distinguish increased autophagosome production from impaired downstream degradation.

p62/SQSTM1:
Changes in p62 can reflect autophagic degradation, altered synthesis, stress-induced transcription, aggregation, or other processes. p62 abundance alone is therefore not a definitive flux measurement.

Lysosomal inhibition:
Comparing LC3 turnover or cargo degradation in the presence and absence of late-stage lysosomal inhibition can help estimate dynamic flux. Experimental conditions and inhibitor choice require validation.

Tandem fluorescent reporters:
pH-sensitive tandem reporters such as mCherry-GFP-LC3 can distinguish relatively neutral autophagosomes from acidified autolysosomal structures and provide spatial information about pathway progression.

Cargo degradation:
Direct measurements of cargo degradation or generation of lysosomal degradation products can provide a closer readout of pathway throughput than autophagosome abundance alone [18].

Genetic validation:
Knockout or knockdown of core factors at distinct stages can determine whether a phenotype depends on canonical autophagy. Because individual ATG proteins can have autophagy-independent functions, targeting more than one autophagy component is preferred when establishing causal dependence [17].

Electron microscopy:
Electron microscopy can verify double-membrane morphology and distinguish phagophores/autophagosomes from other vacuoles, but morphology alone does not quantify flux.

For claims of ADCD:
The evidentiary threshold is higher. Cell death should be reduced by specific genetic disruption of the autophagy machinery, ideally at more than one independent node, long-term survival should be assessed when appropriate, and alternative executing death pathways should be excluded [19,20].


11. MOLECULAR HIERARCHY

Physiological/stress inputs:
- amino-acid availability;
- growth-factor signaling;
- energetic stress;
- hypoxia;
- oxidative/proteotoxic stress;
- infection;
- damaged organelles.

Nutrient/energy integration:
- mTORC1;
- Rag/Ragulator and lysosomal nutrient-sensing systems;
- SLC38A9;
- RHEB;
- AMPK [2,3].

Initiation:
- ULK1/ULK2;
- FIP200/RB1CC1;
- ATG13;
- ATG101 [1,4].

Nucleation:
- VPS34/PIK3C3;
- VPS15/PIK3R4;
- BECN1;
- ATG14;
- PI3P;
- WIPI-family effectors [1,4,5].

Membrane growth:
- ATG2;
- ATG9;
- TMEM41B;
- VMP1 [1,6].

ATG8 lipidation:
- ATG4;
- ATG7;
- ATG3;
- ATG12;
- ATG10;
- ATG5;
- ATG16L1;
- LC3/GABARAP-family proteins [1,7,8].

Selective cargo recognition:
- p62/SQSTM1;
- NBR1;
- OPTN;
- NDP52/CALCOCO2;
- TAX1BP1 and additional cargo-specific receptors [9,10].

Closure:
- ESCRT-associated machinery in experimentally defined systems [11].

Maturation/fusion:
- RAB7-related machinery;
- tethering factors including PLEKHM1, EPG5 and HOPS-associated machinery;
- STX17/SNAP29/VAMP7 or VAMP8;
- YKT6/SNAP29/STX7;
- STX17/SNAP47/VAMP7 or VAMP8 in defined mammalian contexts [12,13].

Degradation/recycling:
- lysosomal hydrolases including cathepsins;
- metabolite exporters;
- nutrient-sensing reactivation;
- autophagic lysosome reformation [2,15,16].

Biomarkers/readouts rather than executors:
- LC3-II accumulation;
- LC3 puncta;
- p62 abundance;
- autophagosome number;
- vacuolization.

These observations require flux interpretation and are not themselves proof of pathway throughput or cell death [17,18].


12. INTEGRATED CANONICAL SEQUENCE

Homeostatic or stress signal
→ lysosomal nutrient/energy sensing
→ reduction of mTORC1 inhibition and context-dependent AMPK input
→ ULK1/2–FIP200–ATG13–ATG101 activation/recruitment
→ engagement of PI3KC3-C1 (VPS34–VPS15–BECN1–ATG14)
→ local PI3P generation
→ recruitment of WIPI proteins and membrane-growth machinery
→ ATG2-mediated lipid transfer coordinated with ATG9/TMEM41B/VMP1 scramblase activity
→ ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 assembly and LC3/GABARAP lipidation
→ bulk or selective cargo capture
→ phagophore expansion
→ autophagosome closure
→ trafficking/tethering and SNARE-mediated lysosomal fusion
→ autolysosomal hydrolase-dependent cargo degradation
→ metabolite export
→ nutrient-sensing recovery and lysosome reformation
→ restoration/remodeling of cellular homeostasis.

This is the canonical endpoint.

The sequence must not be extended automatically to:
“… → cell death.”

If death occurs in parallel, its mechanism must be independently determined.


13. FINAL MECHANISTIC INTERPRETATION

For RCDome, Autophagy can be operationally defined as:

An established lysosome-dependent cellular degradation and recycling process, here referring specifically to macroautophagy, in which nutrient, energetic, stress, or cargo-derived signals regulate ULK and PI3KC3-C1 initiation machinery; PI3P-dependent membrane-remodeling systems generate and expand a phagophore; ATG-conjugation systems lipidate LC3/GABARAP-family proteins and support cargo capture; the membrane closes to form a double-membrane autophagosome; and RAB/tether/SNARE-dependent maturation culminates in lysosomal fusion, hydrolase-mediated degradation, metabolite recycling, and restoration or remodeling of cellular homeostasis.

Classification:
Established cellular process / RCD-related concept; not itself an RCD modality.

Established mechanistic core:
- mTORC1/ULK-centered initiation;
- PI3KC3-C1-dependent PI3P generation;
- ATG2/ATG9-associated membrane expansion;
- ATG8-family lipidation;
- autophagosome closure;
- lysosomal fusion and degradation;
- dynamic autophagic flux.

Context-dependent components:
- relative contribution of AMPK;
- exact membrane source distribution;
- selective-cargo receptor combinations;
- mitophagy route;
- exact SNARE combination used during fusion;
- contribution of individual ATG8 paralogues.

Not established:
- a universal cell-death commitment point for canonical autophagy;
- a universal lethal executor for canonical autophagy;
- equivalence between autophagosome accumulation and increased flux;
- equivalence between autophagy activation and autophagy-dependent cell death.

The major conceptual safeguard for RCDome is therefore:

AUTOPHAGY ≠ AUTOPHAGY-DEPENDENT CELL DEATH.

Macroautophagy normally terminates in degradation, recycling, and homeostatic adaptation. Only when cell death itself is mechanistically dependent on the autophagic machinery, demonstrated by rigorous causal perturbation and exclusion of alternative death mechanisms, should the death be classified as autophagy-dependent cell death [19,20].
