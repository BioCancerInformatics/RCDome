Lysozincrosis

1. Definition and Classification

Lysozincrosis is an emerging, non-consensus-standard regulated non-apoptotic death mechanism in which pathological lysosomal Zn2+ handling drives rapid mitochondrial dysfunction, energetic collapse, and necrotic loss of cellular integrity [1-4].

The strongest direct evidence comes from metastatic melanoma cells with high lysosomal TRPML1/MCOLN1 activity. In this model, pharmacological activation of TRPML1 promotes release of Zn2+ from lysosomes, rapidly induces mitochondrial swelling, decreases cellular ATP, and produces non-apoptotic necrotic death [1].

Lysozincrosis should therefore not currently be presented as a universally established core RCD modality equivalent in nomenclatural status to apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, or ferroptosis. The available evidence supports it more conservatively as an emerging specialized lysosome-dependent, zinc-driven regulated necrotic state [1-4].

Its defining feature is not zinc toxicity alone. Zinc overload can produce apoptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy-associated death, mitochondrial injury, or mixed phenotypes in different systems [5-10]. Likewise, lysosomal membrane permeabilization alone is insufficient because LMP participates in several other regulated-cell-death mechanisms.

The narrow mechanistic definition instead requires a causal lysosome–zinc–mitochondria axis:

pathological lysosomal Zn2+ handling → toxic Zn2+ redistribution → mitochondrial dysfunction → profound ATP depletion → irreversible homeostatic failure → necrotic death.

LMP can participate in this process, particularly in oxidative-stress and lysosomal-zinc-overload models, but complete lysosomal rupture is not necessarily the defining initiating event in the melanoma prototype. There, regulated Zn2+ efflux through the lysosomal channel TRPML1 precedes mitochondrial collapse [1].

This distinction is important for RCDome. 2. Initiation and Triggers

Several different initiating routes can disturb zinc homeostasis and converge on lysosomal Zn2+ accumulation, lysosomal Zn2+ release, or both.

The most direct lysozincrosis prototype begins with TRPML1 hyperactivation.

TRPML1, encoded by MCOLN1, is a lysosomal cation channel involved in lysosomal ion homeostasis, including Zn2+ handling [11]. In metastatic melanoma cells, pharmacological TRPML1 agonism produces lysosomal Zn2+ release and selective death of cells with high TRPML1 activity [1].

The direct sequence is:

TRPML1 activation → lysosomal Zn2+ efflux → cytosolic/mitochondrial Zn2+ redistribution → rapid mitochondrial dysfunction → ATP depletion → necrotic death.

A second route involves oxidative stress.

Reactive oxygen species can oxidize metallothioneins and liberate Zn2+ that is normally protein-bound [5,12-13]. The resulting increase in labile cytosolic Zn2+ can be redistributed into autophagic vacuoles and lysosomes.

Thus:

oxidative stress → metallothionein oxidation → Zn2+ release → cytosolic zinc elevation → lysosomal zinc loading → lysosomal dysfunction/LMP.

A third route originates from mitochondrial injury. In MPP+-associated neuronal models, mitochondrial complex I impairment increases mitochondrial ROS. Oxidative stress then mobilizes intracellular Zn2+, while zinc itself further damages mitochondria, generating a reinforcing Zn2+–ROS cycle [14].

A fourth route involves extracellular zinc entry, particularly in injured neurons. Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors, voltage-gated calcium channels, and to a lesser extent NMDA receptors can permit zinc entry and increase the intracellular labile Zn2+ pool [15-16].

A fifth route involves regulated zinc-transporter trafficking.

In mammary epithelial cells, TNFα signaling promotes post-translational modification and retargeting of ZnT2 toward lysosomes. AP-3-dependent trafficking increases lysosomal zinc accumulation and contributes to lysosome-mediated death during mammary gland involution [7,17-18].

The sequence is:

TNFα signaling → ZnT2 dephosphorylation/trafficking change → AP-3-dependent lysosomal targeting → lysosomal Zn2+ accumulation → lysosomal dysfunction → death.

A sixth route involves exogenous zinc delivery.

ZnO nanoparticles can enter acidic lysosomal compartments and release Zn2+, while zinc ionophores or zinc complexes can concentrate zinc within lysosomes [19-22]. However, these interventions can terminate in autophagy-dependent death or ferroptosis rather than narrow lysozincrosis, depending on the model.

Therefore, the upstream triggers are heterogeneous, but the mechanistic convergence point is abnormal lysosomal zinc handling.

3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

The central machinery of narrow lysozincrosis consists of the lysosomal Zn2+ pool, lysosomal zinc-handling proteins, the lysosomal membrane, and mitochondria.

TRPML1/MCOLN1 occupies the most important position in the best-supported melanoma model [1]. Its activation releases lysosomal Zn2+, generating a rapid signal from lysosome to mitochondrion.

ZnT2 represents a different zinc-handling mechanism. Instead of releasing zinc from lysosomes, ZnT2 can promote lysosomal zinc accumulation when retargeted to the lysosomal compartment [17-18].

Thus, lysozincrosis-related systems can involve two conceptually distinct processes:

lysosomal Zn2+ efflux toward the cytoplasm and mitochondria;

or excessive Zn2+ accumulation within lysosomes until lysosomal homeostasis fails.

These mechanisms are not contradictory. They represent different ways in which the lysosome can become the organizing organelle for pathological zinc redistribution.

Under physiological conditions, lysosomes can buffer zinc and contribute to zinc detoxification. Zinc can enhance lysosomal acidification, V-ATPase assembly, autophagic flux, and adaptive lysosomal responses in some settings [23]. Therefore, lysosomal zinc accumulation itself is not inherently lethal.

The transition to death occurs when zinc handling exceeds lysosomal buffering, export, repair, or degradative capacity.

In oxidative-stress models, excessive lysosomal Zn2+ is associated with LMP and cathepsin redistribution [5,12]. Experiments with isolated lysosomes show that zinc and the lipid-peroxidation product 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal can directly promote lysosomal membrane permeabilization [12].

This supports the sequence:

ROS → intracellular Zn2+ mobilization + lipid peroxidation → lysosomal Zn2+ accumulation → LMP → cathepsin release.

However, LMP is not necessarily the universal core executor of narrow lysozincrosis. In the direct melanoma prototype, toxic Zn2+ release through TRPML1 and subsequent mitochondrial injury are more central than cathepsin-dependent proteolytic execution [1].

The mitochondrial compartment therefore represents the major downstream target.

Intracellular zinc can inhibit mitochondrial energy processes, dissipate the mitochondrial membrane potential, deplete ATP, cause mitochondrial permeability transition, and induce mitochondrial fission and fragmentation [16].

The core machinery can therefore be summarized as:

abnormal lysosomal Zn2+ handling → toxic Zn2+ redistribution → mitochondrial Zn2+-associated injury → respiratory/energetic failure.

4. Propagation and Amplification

The most important amplification mechanism is reciprocal interaction between zinc dyshomeostasis and oxidative stress.

Oxidative stress releases Zn2+ from metallothioneins and other intracellular stores. Increased free Zn2+ then promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and additional ROS production [5,14].

Thus:

ROS → Zn2+ mobilization → mitochondrial dysfunction → additional ROS → additional Zn2+ mobilization.

This positive-feedback loop can progressively increase the labile zinc pool and destabilize both lysosomes and mitochondria.

Antioxidants reduce zinc accumulation and lysosomal cathepsin release in relevant neuronal models, while zinc chelation decreases ROS, supporting bidirectional coupling between zinc and oxidative injury [14].

A second amplification route involves lysosomal membrane damage.

Following exposure to H2O2 or toxic levels of zinc, lysosomal zinc rises rapidly and significantly, and then a substantial fraction of zinc-laden lysosomes undergo membrane disintegration, releasing enzymes such as cathepsins [5]. Released cathepsins and acid hydrolases can then damage cytoplasmic substrates.

In limited LMP, lysosomal cathepsins may cleave BID and activate BAX/BAK-dependent mitochondrial apoptosis [24-27]. This route represents lysosomal-apoptotic crosstalk rather than the narrow necrotic form of lysozincrosis.

With more extensive lysosomal injury, broad hydrolase release can contribute to cytoplasmic damage, intracellular acidification, and further mitochondrial impairment [24,28].

A third amplification mechanism occurs directly within mitochondria.

Zinc-induced disruptions of cellular energy processes decrease mitochondrial membrane potential, increase ROS generation, and cause mitochondrial permeability transition, resulting in the release of pro-apoptotic factors [16].

As ATP declines, energy-dependent homeostatic systems become progressively compromised. Ion pumps, membrane maintenance, organelle quality-control pathways, and recovery mechanisms become increasingly ineffective.

The physical route through which lysosomal Zn2+ reaches mitochondria in the melanoma prototype remains unresolved. Direct lysosome–mitochondria contact-site transfer has been proposed as a plausible mechanism, but it has not been established as the universal route [1].

Therefore, the best-supported propagation architecture is:

pathological Zn2+ redistribution → mitochondrial dysfunction → ROS amplification → further Zn2+ dysregulation and lysosomal stress → progressive mitochondrial energetic failure.

5. Protective / Negative Regulators

The first protective system is intracellular zinc buffering.

Metallothioneins bind labile Zn2+ and can limit toxic increases in free zinc [5,14]. Metallothionein-3 is particularly relevant in neural systems.

However, metallothioneins have a context-dependent dual role. Under normal conditions they buffer Zn2+, whereas oxidative modification can release metallothionein-bound zinc and convert the same buffering reservoir into a source of toxic free Zn2+ [5,14].

A second protective mechanism is lysosomal sequestration.

Functional lysosomes can take up excess intracellular zinc and reduce its cytosolic toxicity. Appropriate lysosomal acidification and V-ATPase function support this detoxifying capacity [23,29].

A third mechanism is lysosomal exocytosis.

VAMP7- and SYT7-associated lysosomal exocytosis allows Zn2+ stored within lysosomes to be expelled from the cell. Blocking this process increases zinc toxicity, whereas functional lysosomal exocytosis protects against excess Zn2+ [29].

Thus:

cytosolic Zn2+ excess → lysosomal sequestration → lysosomal exocytosis → Zn2+ elimination.

A fourth protective mechanism is expansion of lysosomal capacity.

EGF-induced endocytosis can transiently increase lysosomal abundance, increasing the cellular capacity to buffer zinc. LAMP1 overexpression likewise reduces zinc-induced LMP and cell death in neuronal models [30].

LAMP2 contributes to lysosomal quality control and modifies sensitivity to zinc-associated oxidative stress [31].

Lysophagy and ESCRT-associated lysosomal repair represent additional general protective systems capable of preventing limited lysosomal injury from progressing to catastrophic rupture [32].

Autophagy also has a context-dependent role. Increased autophagic flux can initially participate in zinc handling and lysosomal adaptation, but in some models autophagy promotes zinc delivery to lysosomes and contributes to death [6,19].

Therefore, autophagy cannot be classified universally as either protective or pro-death in lysozincrosis-related biology.

The main negative regulators can be summarized as:

metallothionein buffering → lysosomal sequestration → lysosomal acidification → lysosomal exocytosis → lysosomal biogenesis → LAMP1/2-associated stability → lysosomal repair and lysophagy.

6. Commitment / Point of No Return

No universal molecular point of no return has been established for lysozincrosis.

Early zinc redistribution is reversible. Likewise, limited lysosomal membrane injury can potentially be repaired or the damaged organelles removed.

The best-supported commitment model is therefore threshold-based.

The critical transition occurs when zinc sequestration and lysosomal handling fail and toxic Zn2+ redistribution drives sufficiently severe mitochondrial damage that cellular ATP production collapses [1,14,33].

In the narrow melanoma prototype, mitochondrial swelling develops rapidly after TRPML1-dependent lysosomal Zn2+ release and is accompanied by severe ATP depletion [1].

Interventions such as zinc chelation, antioxidant treatment, TRPML1 inhibition, or cathepsin inhibition in relevant LMP-associated systems are most effective before advanced mitochondrial damage [1,12,14].

Once mitochondrial swelling and energetic collapse become extensive, rescue becomes substantially less plausible.

Thus, the strongest operational point of no return is:

irreparable pathological lysosomal Zn2+ redistribution → catastrophic mitochondrial energetic failure → ATP levels insufficient to maintain cellular homeostasis.

This remains an operational rather than universally defined molecular checkpoint.

7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

What physically kills the cell in narrow lysozincrosis?

The strongest evidence supports rapid mitochondrial energetic collapse caused by pathological lysosomal Zn2+ release [1].

In the melanoma prototype, TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Zn2+ mobilization rapidly produces mitochondrial swelling and profound ATP depletion. The death is non-apoptotic and necrotic [1].

Thus, the most defensible terminal sequence is:

lysosomal Zn2+ release → toxic mitochondrial Zn2+ exposure → mitochondrial dysfunction/swelling → respiratory failure → severe ATP depletion → failure of ATP-dependent ion gradients and membrane maintenance → irreversible cellular homeostatic collapse → plasma-membrane failure and necrotic death.

This distinction is essential.

Cathepsins can contribute when lysosomal membrane permeabilization accompanies zinc toxicity. They can degrade cellular substrates or engage BID–BAX/BAK signaling [12,24-27].

However, the supplied evidence does not justify defining cathepsins as the universal terminal executors of narrow lysozincrosis.

Likewise, LMP is mechanistically important in several zinc-overload models but is not necessarily the final physical cause of death in the TRPML1-driven melanoma prototype.

The most specific terminal lesion supported by that prototype is mitochondrial energy failure.

Lysozincrosis therefore differs mechanistically from apoptosis, where caspase-dependent cellular dismantling dominates; necroptosis, where RIPK3–MLKL controls execution; pyroptosis, where gasdermin pores drive lysis; and ferroptosis, where uncontrolled phospholipid peroxidation constitutes the central lethal chemistry [2-4,34].

8. Morphological Sequence

No unique morphology has yet been established as diagnostic of lysozincrosis.

The narrow prototype displays a rapid transition from apparently viable cells to mitochondrial swelling and necrotic loss of cellular integrity.

A generalized mechanistic sequence can be represented as:

normal zinc homeostasis → abnormal lysosomal Zn2+ accumulation or release → lysosomal enlargement and/or zinc-positive vesicles → mitochondrial Zn2+-associated stress → mitochondrial swelling and fragmentation → ATP depletion → cellular swelling/homeostatic collapse → plasma-membrane failure.

In metastatic melanoma, mitochondrial swelling can occur rapidly after TRPML1 activation, within a timescale of less than approximately one hour in the supplied mechanistic summary [1].

In ammonia neurotoxicity models, glutamine mediates oxidative stress and astrocyte swelling, and ammonia induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage [40-41].

In mammary epithelial cells, ZnT2-associated lysosomal zinc accumulation is accompanied by lysosomal enlargement and cathepsin B release [7,17-18].

The terminal phenotype of narrow lysozincrosis is necrotic rather than classical apoptotic fragmentation. Plasma-membrane integrity is ultimately lost, and apoptotic-body formation is not the defining morphology [1,39].

Nevertheless, mitochondrial swelling, lysosomal enlargement, or Zn2+-positive vesicles are not specific enough to diagnose lysozincrosis in isolation.

9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

The most important differential diagnosis is lysosome-dependent apoptosis.

Zinc overload can destabilize lysosomes, release cathepsins, activate BID, promote BAX/BAK-dependent mitochondrial permeabilization, release cytochrome c, and trigger caspases [24-27,35].

Such a sequence represents zinc-associated lysosome-to-mitochondria apoptosis rather than narrow lysozincrosis when caspase-dependent apoptotic execution becomes dominant.

The distinction can be summarized as:

lysozincrosis:
lysosomal Zn2+ dysregulation → mitochondrial energetic collapse → ATP failure → necrotic death;

zinc-associated apoptosis:
lysosomal dysfunction/LMP → cathepsin–BID/BAX/BAK → MOMP → caspases → apoptotic death.

Autophagy represents another major intersection.

Oxidative injury can induce autophagy before zinc accumulates within autophagic and lysosomal compartments [6]. In some astrocytic systems, inhibition of autophagy decreases zinc accumulation, LMP, and death, suggesting that autophagic trafficking contributes to zinc delivery into lysosomes.

TRPML1-mediated zinc signaling can also interfere with autophagosome–lysosome fusion by affecting STX17–VAMP8-associated machinery in cancer cells [36].

Therefore, autophagy can either facilitate zinc trafficking or become dysfunctional downstream of lysosomal zinc disturbance.

Ferroptosis must also be separated carefully.

Zn2+ participates in some ferroptosis systems, including ZIP7-associated signaling, and lysosomal dysfunction can contribute to ferroptotic execution [8,37]. ZnO nanoparticles can trigger ferroptosis through altered ROS and iron homeostasis [10].

When the terminal mechanism is iron-dependent phospholipid-peroxidation catastrophe, the death should be classified as ferroptosis rather than lysozincrosis.

Pyroptotic signaling can also emerge under conditions of lysosomal zinc overload and oxidative stress. However, gasdermin-dependent membrane permeabilization would indicate pyroptosis or parallel pathway activation rather than core lysozincrosis.

Finally, generic lysosome-dependent cell death must be distinguished from lysozincrosis. LDCD is a broader category in which LMP and lysosomal hydrolases are causally central. Lysozincrosis is narrower and requires pathological zinc handling as the organizing mechanism, with the strongest prototype linking lysosomal Zn2+ release directly to mitochondrial energy collapse.

10. Experimental Validation

A rigorous demonstration of lysozincrosis should begin with direct measurement of zinc dynamics.

Lysosomal Zn2+ should be visualized or quantified using compartment-sensitive zinc probes or equivalent validated methods. Demonstrating movement of Zn2+ from the lysosome toward the cytosolic or mitochondrial compartment is substantially stronger than measuring total cellular zinc alone [1,38].

Second, lysosomal dependence should be demonstrated.

In the melanoma prototype, genetic or pharmacological manipulation of TRPML1 is particularly informative [1]. High TRPML1 activity should correlate with vulnerability, while TRPML1 inhibition should suppress the zinc redistribution and death phenotype.

Third, zinc dependence must be established using chelation or manipulation of zinc transport.

Protection by zinc chelation strongly supports a causal role for Zn2+ rather than nonspecific lysosomal injury.

Fourth, mitochondrial consequences should be measured directly.

Relevant readouts include mitochondrial swelling, fragmentation, membrane-potential loss, respiratory dysfunction, and ATP depletion [1,15-16].

For narrow lysozincrosis, these mitochondrial changes should temporally follow lysosomal Zn2+ mobilization and precede terminal plasma-membrane failure.

Fifth, apoptotic execution should be evaluated.

If caspase inhibition fully rescues death and canonical apoptotic signaling dominates, the phenotype is more appropriately interpreted as zinc-associated apoptosis rather than narrow lysozincrosis.

Sixth, alternative RCD pathways should be tested when biologically plausible.

RIPK3/MLKL dependence would support necroptosis.

Gasdermin dependence would support pyroptosis.

Protection by canonical ferroptosis inhibitors combined with lipid-peroxidation dependence would favor ferroptosis.

Seventh, LMP should be measured when the proposed model includes lysosomal rupture.

Cathepsin redistribution, loss of lysosomal dye retention, galectin recruitment, or direct lysosomal membrane assays can determine whether LMP is part of the mechanism [5,12,24].

However, LMP should not be assumed to be obligatory in every lysozincrosis model because regulated TRPML1-mediated Zn2+ efflux can precede catastrophic lysosomal rupture.

The strongest experimental chain for narrow lysozincrosis is therefore:

lysosomal Zn2+ flux → dependence on TRPML1 or another lysosomal zinc-handling mechanism → rescue by zinc chelation → rapid mitochondrial dysfunction → ATP collapse → caspase-independent necrotic death.

Transcriptomic evidence alone cannot demonstrate lysozincrosis. MCOLN1/TRPML1, SLC30A2/ZnT2, metallothioneins, LAMP genes, zinc transporters, or mitochondrial stress genes may indicate susceptibility or zinc-handling states but cannot prove pathological Zn2+ flux or execution.

11. Molecular Hierarchy

Initiators / sensors:

TRPML1/MCOLN1 hyperactivation; ZnT2 trafficking; redox-sensitive metallothioneins; extracellular zinc entry through neuronal channels; ZnO nanoparticle dissolution; zinc ionophores/complexes; mitochondrial ROS-associated zinc mobilization.

Early transducers:

labile Zn2+; ROS; HNE and related lipid-peroxidation products; AP-3-dependent ZnT2 trafficking; autophagic redistribution of zinc.

Core machinery:

lysosomal Zn2+ pool; lysosomal membrane; TRPML1-mediated Zn2+ release; ZnT2-mediated lysosomal loading; lysosomal buffering/export systems; mitochondria.

Execution-linked factors:

mitochondrial Zn2+ toxicity; loss of membrane potential; mitochondrial swelling; mitochondrial fragmentation; inhibition of energy metabolism; ATP depletion.

Context-dependent secondary effectors:

LMP; cathepsins; BID; BAX/BAK; oxidative amplification; autophagy-associated trafficking.

Protective regulators:

metallothionein-3; lysosomal acidification; V-ATPase function; lysosomal exocytosis involving VAMP7/SYT7; LAMP1; LAMP2; lysosomal biogenesis; lysophagy; ESCRT-associated repair.

Biomarkers:

lysosomal Zn2+-positive vesicles; altered lysosomal zinc probes; lysosomal enlargement; LysoTracker changes; cathepsin redistribution when LMP occurs; mitochondrial swelling; mitochondrial fragmentation; membrane-potential loss; ATP depletion.

None of these individual biomarkers is sufficient to establish lysozincrosis.

Terminal lesion:

irreversible mitochondrial energetic collapse severe enough to prevent maintenance of ion gradients, membrane integrity, and cellular homeostasis, culminating in necrotic death.

12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

The narrow canonical lysozincrosis sequence supported most directly by the melanoma model is:

high TRPML1/MCOLN1 activity → TRPML1 activation → pathological lysosomal Zn2+ efflux → toxic Zn2+ redistribution toward the cytoplasm/mitochondrial compartment → mitochondrial Zn2+-associated dysfunction → mitochondrial swelling + membrane-potential impairment + respiratory failure → rapid ATP depletion → failure of ATP-dependent ion homeostasis and membrane maintenance → irreversible cellular collapse → plasma-membrane failure → necrotic death.

A broader zinc–lysosome route can be represented as:

oxidative stress → metallothionein oxidation → release of bound Zn2+ → cytosolic zinc elevation → autophagic/lysosomal zinc accumulation → zinc/HNE-associated lysosomal destabilization → LMP → cathepsin release + additional zinc redistribution → mitochondrial dysfunction → ATP decline → apoptosis-like or necrotic execution depending on injury magnitude.

A ZnT2-dependent physiological/developmental route is:

TNFα signaling → ZnT2 post-translational modification → AP-3-dependent lysosomal retargeting → lysosomal Zn2+ accumulation → lysosomal enlargement/dysfunction → cathepsin release → lysosome-mediated epithelial cell death.

These pathways share zinc–lysosome biology but should not automatically be collapsed into one identical molecular mechanism.

The most defensible integrated concept is therefore:

pathological stimulus → abnormal labile Zn2+ generation or transport → lysosomal Zn2+ sequestration/release exceeds buffering capacity → zinc–ROS amplification and/or LMP → toxic lysosome-to-mitochondria signaling → mitochondrial energetic collapse → irreversible homeostatic failure → necrotic death.

13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, lysozincrosis should be classified as an emerging, non-consensus-standard, zinc-driven lysosome-centered regulated necrotic mechanism rather than as a fully established universal RCD modality.

The strongest direct prototype is TRPML1-dependent death in metastatic melanoma. In this system, lysosomal Zn2+ release is upstream, mitochondria are the major lethal target, ATP depletion is profound, and death is rapid and non-apoptotic.

This evidence supports a narrower definition than generic zinc toxicity or generic lysosomal cell death.

The core mechanistic identity is:

pathological lysosomal Zn2+ handling → toxic zinc redistribution → mitochondrial energetic collapse → necrotic death.

Importantly, LMP should not be made obligatorily synonymous with lysozincrosis. LMP is strongly supported in several zinc-overload and oxidative-stress models, but the most specific melanoma prototype relies on controlled TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Zn2+ release before the terminal mitochondrial lesion.

Likewise, cathepsins should not be designated universal lysozincrosis executors. They are mechanistically relevant in zinc-associated LMP models and can redirect death toward lysosome-dependent apoptosis, but the strongest narrow lysozincrosis evidence instead supports mitochondrial ATP failure as the proximate terminal mechanism.

The best current commitment model is the transition from reversible zinc buffering/sequestration to severe mitochondrial dysfunction with irreversible ATP collapse.

The largest mechanistic uncertainty is the physical connection between lysosomal Zn2+ mobilization and mitochondrial injury. It remains unresolved whether Zn2+ reaches mitochondria primarily through bulk cytosolic redistribution, lysosome–mitochondria contact sites, specific transport mechanisms, or a combination of these processes.

A second important uncertainty concerns generalizability. Zinc-dependent lysosomal injury has been demonstrated in several tissues and disease models, but many of these systems terminate through apoptosis, autophagy-dependent death, or ferroptosis. They therefore support individual mechanistic modules but do not establish that narrow lysozincrosis is broadly conserved as a distinct execution program.

For transcriptomic applications, high MCOLN1, zinc-transporter expression, metallothionein expression, lysosomal signatures, or mitochondrial-stress programs should therefore be labeled as lysozincrosis-associated or zinc–lysosome-associated states, not active lysozincrosis.

Direct mechanistic assignment requires evidence of pathological lysosomal Zn2+ flux, zinc dependence, lysosome-to-mitochondria causal ordering, mitochondrial energetic collapse, and exclusion of a better-established alternative execution mechanism.
