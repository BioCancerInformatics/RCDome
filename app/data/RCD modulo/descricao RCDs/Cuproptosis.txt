## Cuproptosis — Mechanistic Pathway

Cuproptosis is a copper-dependent, mitochondria-centered form of regulated cell death in which excessive bioavailable copper interacts with the lipoylated mitochondrial metabolic proteome, producing abnormal aggregation of lipoylated enzymes, destabilization of iron–sulfur proteins, severe proteotoxic stress, and ultimately failure of respiration-dependent cellular homeostasis [1–6]. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, or ferroptosis, cuproptosis is not currently defined by a dedicated protease cascade, membrane-pore-forming protein, or lipid-peroxidation executor. Instead, its defining molecular dependence lies in mitochondrial copper exposure, FDX1 activity, the mitochondrial protein-lipoylation pathway, and the presence of lipoylated tricarboxylic-acid-cycle-associated enzymes that can become toxic copper-binding substrates [1–6].

The central conceptual distinction is therefore between generic copper toxicity and cuproptosis. Copper can damage cells through numerous biochemical routes, including oxidative stress, proteasomal dysfunction, lysosomal injury, and interactions with multiple macromolecules. Cuproptosis refers specifically to the mitochondrial pathway in which copper toxicity depends on FDX1 and protein lipoylation and is associated with aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins and loss of Fe–S proteins [1–6]. Copper-induced death occurring through another dominant mechanism should not automatically be classified as cuproptosis.

Copper is an essential transition metal required for enzymes involved in mitochondrial respiration, antioxidant defense, connective-tissue biology, neurotransmitter synthesis, iron metabolism, and many other processes. Its chemical reactivity, however, means that free intracellular copper must be tightly controlled [1,7]. Mammalian cells therefore maintain copper homeostasis through coordinated uptake, intracellular transport, sequestration, and export.

SLC31A1, also known as CTR1, is one of the principal high-affinity copper-import proteins at the plasma membrane. Increased CTR1 activity or expression can raise intracellular copper availability and increase sensitivity to copper-dependent stress [1,4,7].

Conversely, ATP7A and ATP7B contribute to copper export and intracellular copper redistribution. Their activity reduces the accumulation of potentially toxic copper, whereas impaired copper export can increase the intracellular copper burden and promote susceptibility to copper-associated cell death [1,4,7].

The initiating condition for canonical experimental cuproptosis is therefore an increase in intracellular and especially mitochondrial copper exposure. This can occur through failure of copper homeostasis, excessive environmental copper, altered copper transport, or most experimentally, through copper ionophores that deliver copper into cells and mitochondria [1–6].

Elesclomol is one of the best-characterized experimental agents used to trigger this pathway. Elesclomol binds copper and facilitates its intracellular and mitochondrial delivery, thereby increasing the pool of copper capable of interacting with mitochondrial targets [1–4].

The initiating sequence can consequently be represented as increased extracellular or intracellular copper availability → copper uptake and/or ionophore-mediated copper delivery → increased mitochondrial copper exposure → engagement of FDX1 and the lipoylated mitochondrial proteome.

FDX1, ferredoxin 1, occupies a central position in cuproptosis. It is a mitochondrial iron–sulfur protein involved in mitochondrial redox reactions and is strongly linked to both copper-ionophore sensitivity and mitochondrial protein lipoylation [3,8].

FDX1 should not be considered a receptor in the conventional signaling sense. Instead, it functions as an intracellular metabolic determinant that allows copper exposure to become coupled to the lipoylated protein machinery required for cuproptosis.

The available mechanistic model places FDX1 upstream of both the copper chemistry and the lipoylation state of the relevant mitochondrial substrates. FDX1 contributes to reduction of Cu2+ toward the more reactive Cu+ state and simultaneously supports the metabolic machinery required to maintain lipoylated mitochondrial proteins [2–4,8].

Recent mutational analysis of FDX1 further supports its central mechanistic role by demonstrating that specific structural determinants of FDX1 control mitochondrial protein lipoylation and susceptibility to cuproptosis [3]. Thus, FDX1 abundance alone is not the entire mechanism; the functional integrity of FDX1-dependent mitochondrial processes is required.

The next essential mechanistic layer is protein lipoylation.

Lipoic acid is a covalently attached cofactor required by a restricted group of mitochondrial enzyme complexes involved principally in oxidative carbon metabolism. The lipoyl group is attached to specific lysine residues of selected mitochondrial proteins and functions as a mobile redox and acyl-group carrier.

Major lipoylated mitochondrial substrates include components of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex and related α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complexes. DLAT, the E2 component of pyruvate dehydrogenase, is one of the most mechanistically important cuproptosis targets. DLST, the corresponding E2 component of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, represents another important lipoylated substrate [2–6].

The mitochondrial lipoylation system involves proteins including LIAS and LIPT1, with additional dependence on the mitochondrial metabolic network containing DLD and the corresponding dehydrogenase complexes [2,5,6].

LIAS catalyzes formation of the lipoyl cofactor, whereas LIPT1 participates in transfer of the lipoyl group to target proteins. Functional FDX1 supports this lipoylation network [2,3,5].

This produces a critical principle: copper alone is insufficient to generate canonical cuproptosis with maximum efficiency. The cell must also contain an adequate pool of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins capable of binding copper.

Consequently, genetic loss or substantial suppression of FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, or other components required for formation of lipoylated targets can markedly reduce sensitivity to copper-ionophore-induced death [2,3,5].

This does not mean that loss of protein lipoylation is intrinsically cytotoxic through cuproptosis. Rather, loss of the lipoylated substrate pool removes the molecular structures through which excessive copper produces the characteristic toxic protein aggregation.

The sequence therefore proceeds as mitochondrial copper accumulation → functional FDX1/lipoylation system → presence of lipoylated DLAT, DLST, and related mitochondrial metabolic proteins → direct copper engagement of the lipoylated proteome.

The chemical interaction between copper and the lipoyl groups constitutes one of the defining molecular events in cuproptosis. Cu+ interacts strongly with lipoylated mitochondrial proteins, particularly lipoylated DLAT and DLST [2–6].

This interaction alters their normal biochemical behavior and promotes abnormal oligomerization and aggregation. DLAT progressively accumulates in higher-order or detergent-insoluble protein assemblies rather than remaining exclusively within normally functioning pyruvate dehydrogenase complexes [2,4–6].

These abnormal assemblies can include disulfide-linked higher-order structures and generate a severe protein-homeostasis burden within mitochondria.

Thus, the lipoyl modification has two opposing biological functions depending on copper availability. Under physiological conditions it is required for normal oxidative metabolism. During pathological copper overload, the same chemical modification becomes a high-affinity vulnerability through which copper converts essential metabolic proteins into aggregation-prone toxic structures.

The resulting sequence is functional mitochondrial lipoylation → copper binding to lipoylated DLAT/DLST → abnormal conformational interactions and oligomerization → insoluble aggregation of mitochondrial metabolic proteins → mitochondrial proteotoxic stress.

In parallel with lipoylated-protein aggregation, cuproptosis is associated with loss or destabilization of iron–sulfur proteins [1,2,5,6].

Fe–S clusters are essential prosthetic groups present in numerous mitochondrial proteins, including proteins involved in electron transfer, respiratory metabolism, redox reactions, and biosynthetic processes.

Disruption of this network therefore has consequences beyond the loss of a single enzyme. Destabilization of Fe–S proteins can simultaneously compromise several mitochondrial functions that depend on properly assembled iron–sulfur cofactors.

The relationship between copper exposure, FDX1, and Fe–S proteins is mechanistically important because FDX1 itself is an Fe–S-containing mitochondrial protein and because the FDX1-regulated network couples mitochondrial redox chemistry to both lipoylation and Fe–S biology [3,8].

Current evidence supports two major biochemical lesions operating in parallel during cuproptosis: toxic aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial metabolic proteins and progressive failure of Fe–S-dependent proteins.

These lesions converge on mitochondrial proteotoxic stress.

Protein aggregation challenges mitochondrial protein quality-control systems because misfolded or aggregated proteins must be refolded, degraded, segregated, or otherwise neutralized. When the generation of abnormal copper-bound protein assemblies exceeds the capacity of mitochondrial proteostasis systems, normal mitochondrial protein organization deteriorates.

At the same time, Fe–S protein loss impairs mitochondrial biochemical reactions and respiratory functions.

The combined effect is therefore not simply “copper-induced ROS.” The central event is structural and metabolic disruption of the mitochondrial protein machinery.

This distinction is particularly important because oxidative stress is frequently observed during metal toxicity but is not considered sufficient to define cuproptosis. Cuproptosis persists mechanistically as a distinct process because its strongest causal dependencies are FDX1, protein lipoylation, copper binding to lipoylated proteins, their aggregation, and Fe–S destabilization [1–6].

The mitochondrial metabolic state strongly determines susceptibility.

Cells that rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial carbon metabolism generally contain a larger functionally engaged pool of lipoylated mitochondrial enzymes and are therefore more susceptible to cuproptosis [5,9–11].

By contrast, cells that rely predominantly on glycolysis can be comparatively resistant.

This produces a metabolic vulnerability principle: the more dependent a cell is on mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and lipoylated TCA-associated enzyme complexes, the larger the biochemical target space available for copper-induced proteotoxicity.

Experimentally, suppression of mitochondrial respiration or interruption of mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism can reduce sensitivity to copper-ionophore-induced death [5,6].

Thus, mitochondrial respiration is not merely damaged after cuproptosis begins; the respiratory metabolic state helps determine whether the cell possesses the susceptible molecular architecture required for cuproptosis.

The canonical metabolic sequence can therefore be represented as OXPHOS-dependent state → abundant active lipoylated mitochondrial enzyme complexes → mitochondrial copper overload → copper binding to lipoylated enzymes → protein aggregation plus Fe–S destabilization → disruption of TCA-associated metabolism and mitochondrial respiration → progressive mitochondrial dysfunction.

Conversely, glycolytic rewiring can reduce this vulnerability.

Cancer cells frequently reprogram glucose metabolism toward glycolysis. Such metabolic reprogramming decreases dependence on mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and can lower the functional abundance or importance of lipoylated mitochondrial targets [9].

Hypoxia can produce a similar effect by suppressing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and favoring glycolytic ATP production.

The protective sequence can therefore be represented as hypoxia or glycolytic rewiring → reduced mitochondrial oxidative flux → decreased functional dependence on lipoylated TCA-associated enzyme complexes → reduced target availability for copper toxicity → increased resistance to cuproptosis.

This relationship is particularly relevant in cancer because tumors display substantial heterogeneity in mitochondrial metabolism. Oxidative tumors or oxidative tumor-cell subpopulations may be considerably more sensitive to cuproptosis than highly glycolytic populations.

Copper homeostasis creates an additional checkpoint before the mitochondrial lesion forms.

High CTR1/SLC31A1-mediated copper uptake can increase susceptibility by increasing intracellular copper exposure, whereas effective ATP7A/ATP7B-dependent export limits copper accumulation [1,4,7].

Thus, changes in copper import, export, intracellular distribution, sequestration, and mitochondrial delivery can shift the threshold required to initiate cuproptosis.

Glutathione provides another important protective layer.

GSH can bind copper and limit the availability of reactive copper capable of interacting with mitochondrial targets [4,9,12].

Consequently, high intracellular GSH can buffer copper toxicity, whereas GSH depletion increases the amount of bioavailable copper and can enhance cuproptosis.

This explains an important mechanistic connection between ferroptosis-inducing treatments and cuproptosis. Drugs such as erastin or sorafenib can inhibit cystine-dependent GSH synthesis and thereby reduce intracellular copper-buffering capacity [12].

In experimental liver-cancer systems, ferroptosis-inducing compounds enhanced copper-ionophore-induced cuproptosis [12].

The mechanistic interaction should nevertheless be described carefully. This does not demonstrate that cuproptosis and ferroptosis share the same terminal executor.

Rather, ferroptosis-associated metabolic interventions can lower GSH and thereby increase copper availability, while additional effects on FDX1 and protein lipoylation can further increase cuproptosis sensitivity [12].

The shared sequence is therefore cystine/GSH depletion → reduced copper buffering → increased copper availability → greater engagement of FDX1/lipoylated mitochondrial proteins → enhanced cuproptosis.

If iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation subsequently becomes the dominant lethal event, the process is ferroptotic. If copper-dependent lipoylated-protein aggregation and Fe–S collapse dominate, the process is cuproptotic.

The terminal execution stage of cuproptosis remains less precisely resolved than its upstream molecular chemistry.

No dedicated cuproptosis-specific pore-forming protein has been identified. There is currently no equivalent of MLKL in necroptosis or gasdermins in pyroptosis [1,4–6].

Cuproptosis is also not defined by activation of the canonical apoptotic caspase cascade.

Similarly, catastrophic lipid peroxidation and GPX4 failure are not the defining execution machinery as they are in ferroptosis.

The most strongly supported model is therefore that the cell dies after mitochondrial protein aggregation and Fe–S failure reach a level that can no longer be compensated by mitochondrial protein quality control, metabolic rerouting, copper buffering, or energetic adaptation [5,6,13].

The proximal lethal process can be conceptualized as irreversible mitochondrial proteotoxic and metabolic collapse.

Aggregation of lipoylated DLAT/DLST compromises mitochondrial enzyme organization. Fe–S destabilization compromises additional mitochondrial pathways. TCA-associated metabolism becomes impaired, electron transport and respiration deteriorate, and mitochondrial proteostasis is progressively overwhelmed.

These simultaneous defects can ultimately make it impossible to sustain the metabolic and energetic functions required for cellular viability.

Thus, the strongest current terminal sequence is lipoylated-protein aggregation + Fe–S protein destabilization → escalating mitochondrial proteotoxic stress → disruption of TCA-associated metabolic machinery and respiration → failure of mitochondrial homeostasis → cellular metabolic and proteostatic collapse → death.

This model should still be described as incompletely resolved.

The precise biochemical lesion that makes cuproptosis irreversibly lethal has not been mapped to a single molecular event.

For example, it is not yet established whether one specific aggregated protein complex is sufficient for death, whether a critical degree of Fe–S network collapse defines commitment, whether mitochondrial ATP failure is the decisive event, or whether several mitochondrial lesions must occur simultaneously.

There is therefore no universally demonstrated point of no return.

Early copper exposure remains reversible because removal or chelation of copper can suppress toxicity [1,4–6].

Likewise, interventions that decrease mitochondrial substrate use or oxidative metabolism can reduce cuproptosis before catastrophic mitochondrial failure becomes established [5,6].

The most plausible commitment transition occurs when copper-induced lipoylated-protein aggregation and Fe–S destabilization cross a threshold beyond which mitochondrial proteostasis and metabolic function cannot be restored.

However, this remains an inference from the current mechanistic framework rather than a precisely measured universal checkpoint.

Thus, early sequence reversibility can be represented as mitochondrial copper exposure → reversible stress while copper can still be removed or buffered.

The candidate commitment transition can be represented as persistent copper exposure → progressive DLAT/DLST aggregation + widespread Fe–S failure → inability to restore mitochondrial protein and metabolic homeostasis.

The terminal phase can then be represented as irreversible mitochondrial dysfunction → failure of respiration-dependent homeostasis → cellular death.

Copper chelation is consequently one of the most direct mechanistic rescue approaches.

Chelators decrease bioavailable copper before it can continue interacting with lipoylated proteins and can protect cells from copper-ionophore-associated toxicity [1,4–6].

Genetic disruption of FDX1 provides an even stronger mechanistic test because it interrupts the molecular bridge between mitochondrial copper exposure and the lipoylated substrate system [2,3].

Loss of LIAS or LIPT1 similarly reduces formation of the lipoylated target pool and protects against canonical cuproptosis [5,6,12].

These genetic relationships establish a hierarchy in which copper exposure is the trigger but FDX1 and lipoylation are necessary biochemical licensing mechanisms.

FDX1 therefore occupies a particularly important position within the pathway.

It contributes to the redox state of copper, influences the abundance of lipoylated target proteins, and strongly determines susceptibility to cuproptotic stress [3,8].

Deep mutational scanning supports this interpretation by demonstrating that structural alterations in FDX1 can selectively change its ability to support lipoylation and cuproptosis [3].

Nevertheless, FDX1 abundance should not be treated as a standalone diagnostic biomarker. A cell can express FDX1 but remain resistant because copper never reaches the mitochondria, lipoylated target abundance is low, copper export is efficient, glutathione buffering is strong, or the metabolic state is predominantly glycolytic.

Cuproptosis is therefore produced by a network rather than one marker.

p53 has also been proposed as a regulator of cuproptosis through its effects on mitochondrial metabolism and copper-associated pathways [14].

However, p53 is not required as part of the canonical cuproptosis core in the same way as FDX1 or protein lipoylation.

Its effects appear to be context dependent and can involve metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial function, redox control, and regulation of genes that indirectly influence cuproptosis sensitivity [14].

p53 should therefore be placed in the regulatory layer rather than the defining execution machinery.

Similarly, genes such as MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A have been associated with cuproptosis-related genetic signatures and sensitivity patterns [2,5,6]. These associations should not automatically be converted into canonical cuproptosis executors.

The strongest mechanistic genes remain those directly tied to copper homeostasis, FDX1, mitochondrial lipoylation, lipoylated TCA-associated proteins, and Fe–S biology.

A separate copper-dependent proteotoxic mechanism involves disulfiram–copper.

Disulfiram-derived copper complexes can interact with the p97/VCP–NPL4 protein-quality-control machinery and induce NPL4 aggregation, disrupting proteostasis [4,6,15].

This pathway is mechanistically interesting because it also represents copper-dependent proteotoxic stress.

However, NPL4 aggregation occurs in the cytoplasmic protein-quality-control system rather than through the defining FDX1–lipoylated mitochondrial protein pathway.

Therefore, disulfiram–Cu-induced NPL4 aggregation should not automatically be classified as canonical cuproptosis.

The conservative interpretation is copper toxicity through p97/NPL4 disruption → proteasome/protein-quality-control failure → cytoplasmic proteotoxic stress → death, representing either a parallel copper-dependent death route or mechanistic crosstalk with cuproptosis.

The distinction matters when constructing an RCD pathway because otherwise multiple chemically distinct copper-toxic mechanisms would be collapsed into one category.

Cuproptosis can also interact with antitumor immunity.

Recent evidence indicates that immune signaling can modify tumor-cell cuproptosis sensitivity. CD8+ T-cell-derived IFN-γ can activate STAT1–IRF1 signaling in tumor cells and increase FDX1 transcription [16].

The corresponding immune-regulatory sequence is activated CD8+ T cell → IFN-γ release → IFN-γ receptor signaling in tumor cell → STAT1 activation → IRF1-associated transcriptional response → increased FDX1 expression → increased susceptibility of mitochondrial lipoylated proteins to copper-induced cuproptosis [16].

This represents regulation of the cuproptosis threshold rather than replacement of the core mitochondrial mechanism.

Cuproptotic tumor cells can also exhibit immunogenic properties and release damage-associated signals capable of stimulating immune responses [16,17].

Nanomaterial-based approaches have attempted to exploit this principle by increasing tumor copper exposure, triggering cuproptosis, and simultaneously promoting immunogenic cell-death-associated signaling [17].

However, immunogenicity should not be considered part of the universal core execution mechanism. DAMP release and immune activation occur downstream or in parallel depending on cellular and therapeutic context.

The terminal membrane behavior of cuproptotic cells remains incompletely characterized.

Unlike apoptosis, there is no well-established sequence of caspase-dependent shrinkage, blebbing, chromatin fragmentation, and apoptotic-body production.

Unlike pyroptosis, there is no characteristic gasdermin-pore-driven swelling sequence.

Unlike necroptosis, there is no established MLKL-dependent membrane rupture mechanism.

And unlike ferroptosis, there is no validated morphology linked specifically to phospholipid-peroxidation-mediated membrane nanolesions.

Published copper-exposure models can display mitochondrial abnormalities, cytoplasmic vacuolization, cell swelling, nuclear condensation, tissue injury, and eventually loss of membrane integrity [4,18,19].

These morphological observations are not sufficiently specific to define cuproptosis.

Consequently, cuproptosis should be identified through molecular dependency rather than morphology.

The strongest experimental strategy begins by demonstrating copper dependence.

Copper ionophore treatment should induce death, and copper chelation should suppress or substantially reduce the phenotype.

The second requirement is FDX1 dependence.

CRISPR-mediated deletion, knockdown, or functionally disruptive alteration of FDX1 should decrease copper-ionophore-induced death and reduce the downstream lipoylated-protein abnormalities [2,3].

The third requirement is dependence on mitochondrial protein lipoylation.

Loss of LIAS, LIPT1, or related machinery should reduce the abundance of lipoylated mitochondrial substrates and confer protection against canonical cuproptosis [5,6,12].

Direct demonstration of DLAT or DLST modification provides an important biochemical layer.

Investigators can measure accumulation of lipoylated DLAT, formation of DLAT oligomers or high-molecular-weight complexes, redistribution into insoluble protein fractions, and copper-dependent aggregation.

A particularly strong mechanistic experiment combines copper exposure with loss of FDX1 or lipoylation machinery and demonstrates simultaneous suppression of both DLAT aggregation and death.

Fe–S protein destabilization provides a second molecular hallmark.

A reduction in selected Fe–S-containing mitochondrial proteins following copper exposure, particularly when this change is FDX1 dependent, strengthens mechanistic evidence for cuproptosis.

Mitochondrial functional readouts can then establish the physiological consequences.

These may include oxygen-consumption measurements, respiratory-chain function, mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP generation, pyruvate oxidation, TCA-associated metabolite changes, and mitochondrial proteotoxic-stress responses.

However, mitochondrial dysfunction alone does not diagnose cuproptosis because it occurs in numerous cell-death mechanisms.

The critical feature is its causal linkage to copper, FDX1, lipoylated-protein aggregation, and Fe–S destabilization.

Competing forms of regulated cell death should also be evaluated.

Pan-caspase inhibition failing to rescue argues against classical apoptosis as the dominant mechanism.

Lack of rescue by necroptosis inhibitors argues against RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-dependent death.

Failure of ferroptosis-specific interventions to prevent the phenotype despite FDX1/lipoylation dependence argues that the dominant pathway is not conventional ferroptosis.

Likewise, absence of gasdermin dependence distinguishes canonical cuproptosis from pyroptosis.

These exclusion experiments should complement, rather than replace, positive demonstration of the cuproptosis machinery.

Metabolic dependency is another useful experimental dimension.

Cells cultured or manipulated toward greater mitochondrial respiration should generally display greater sensitivity, whereas suppression of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism may reduce cuproptosis [5,9].

This provides functional validation that the death pathway is tightly coupled to oxidative mitochondrial metabolism.

The molecular hierarchy can therefore be divided into several levels.

The initiating stimulus is excessive intracellular and mitochondrial copper.

Copper transport and homeostasis regulators such as SLC31A1/CTR1, ATP7A, ATP7B, glutathione, and copper ionophores determine the amount of bioavailable copper reaching susceptible cellular compartments.

FDX1 acts as a central mitochondrial metabolic converter that links copper exposure to the lipoylation-competent state.

LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, and related mitochondrial components maintain the lipoylated target network.

Lipoylated DLAT, DLST, and related TCA-associated proteins constitute the direct toxic copper-binding substrates.

Copper binding to these proteins drives abnormal oligomerization and aggregation.

Fe–S proteins form the second major vulnerable mitochondrial protein class.

Together, lipoylated-protein aggregation and Fe–S destabilization generate mitochondrial proteotoxic stress.

The downstream lethal state consists of progressive failure of mitochondrial protein homeostasis, oxidative carbon metabolism, respiratory function, and metabolic viability.

ATP7A/ATP7B-mediated export, glutathione-dependent copper buffering, reduced mitochondrial respiration, hypoxic adaptation, and glycolytic reprogramming act as important resistance mechanisms.

Importantly, these categories should not be confused with conventional signaling hierarchies based on receptors and kinase cascades. Cuproptosis is fundamentally a metabolic and chemical cell-death pathway in which a transition metal interacts directly with modified mitochondrial proteins.

There is therefore no classical extracellular death receptor.

FDX1 is not a receptor.

DLAT is not a conventional signaling transducer.

And no final membrane-pore executor is currently established.

The pathway is better conceptualized as metal accumulation → biochemical substrate licensing → toxic protein–metal interaction → proteome collapse → metabolic failure.

The canonical pathway can therefore be represented as excessive copper availability or copper-ionophore treatment → increased intracellular copper → mitochondrial copper accumulation → FDX1-dependent copper reduction and maintenance of mitochondrial protein lipoylation → Cu+ interaction with lipoylated DLAT, DLST, and related mitochondrial proteins → abnormal oligomerization and aggregation of lipoylated enzyme complexes → parallel destabilization of Fe–S proteins → increasing mitochondrial proteotoxic stress → impairment of pyruvate/TCA-associated metabolism and Fe–S-dependent respiratory functions → progressive mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction → failure of proteostatic and metabolic compensation → irreversible loss of cellular viability.

A copper-homeostasis resistance branch can be represented as ATP7A/ATP7B-dependent copper export + GSH-mediated copper buffering → reduced bioavailable mitochondrial copper → reduced interaction with lipoylated proteins → suppression of cuproptosis.

A metabolic resistance branch can be represented as hypoxia or glycolytic reprogramming → decreased oxidative phosphorylation and reduced dependence on lipoylated mitochondrial metabolism → reduced cuproptosis susceptibility.

A sensitization branch can be represented as inhibition of cystine uptake or GSH synthesis → reduced copper buffering → increased bioavailable copper → increased FDX1/lipoylated-protein engagement → enhanced cuproptosis.

An immune-sensitization branch can be represented as CD8+ T-cell IFN-γ → STAT1–IRF1 signaling → increased FDX1 transcription → increased copper-sensitive lipoylated mitochondrial target state → enhanced cuproptosis [16].

The most integrated sequence is therefore copper overload, produced by increased uptake, reduced export, ionophore-mediated transport, or failure of intracellular buffering → increased mitochondrial copper exposure → FDX1-mediated establishment of the appropriate copper redox environment together with maintenance of mitochondrial protein lipoylation → direct Cu+ engagement of lipoylated DLAT/DLST and related mitochondrial metabolic proteins → lipoylation-dependent protein oligomerization and insoluble aggregation → simultaneous destabilization of Fe–S-containing proteins → escalating mitochondrial proteotoxic stress → collapse of TCA-associated metabolic organization, electron-transfer capacity, and respiration-dependent homeostasis → inability of copper-buffering, protein-quality-control, and metabolic adaptive systems to restore mitochondrial function → irreversible mitochondrial proteostatic and bioenergetic failure → cuproptotic cell death.

Thus, cuproptosis is best understood as an FDX1- and lipoylation-dependent copper poisoning of the mitochondrial metabolic proteome.

Its defining mechanistic signature is not simply copper accumulation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, or cell death. The strongest mechanistic combination is copper dependence + FDX1 dependence + functional protein lipoylation + aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins + Fe–S protein destabilization.

The lipoylated-protein aggregation step provides the most specific biochemical lesion currently known, while the simultaneous Fe–S defect expands mitochondrial dysfunction across multiple pathways.

The final execution mechanism remains unresolved at molecular resolution. No dedicated cuproptosis-specific protease, pore, phospholipid lesion, or membrane-disrupting protein has yet been established. The best-supported current interpretation is that a threshold of lipoylated-protein aggregation and Fe–S network disruption drives mitochondrial proteotoxic and metabolic dysfunction beyond the cell's capacity for recovery.

Accordingly, the principal unresolved question is what precise event converts reversible copper-induced mitochondrial stress into irreversible cellular death. Whether this transition is defined by aggregate burden, loss of one or more critical Fe–S functions, energetic collapse, failure of mitochondrial proteostasis, or a combination of these events remains an active mechanistic problem.
