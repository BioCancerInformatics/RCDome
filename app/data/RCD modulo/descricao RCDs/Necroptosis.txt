Necroptosis

1. Definition and Classification

Necroptosis is an established regulated cell-death modality in which diverse death-, pathogen-, or stress-sensing pathways converge on activation of receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), phosphorylation and oligomerization of mixed lineage kinase domain-like pseudokinase (MLKL), and MLKL-dependent disruption of plasma-membrane homeostasis [1-4]. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis does not execute cellular demolition primarily through a caspase-dependent proteolytic cascade. Plasma membrane translocation of MLKL or MLKL(ND) causes sodium influx, and removing sodium from the culture medium inhibits necroptosis [46].

The defining mechanistic feature is therefore not simply a necrotic morphology. Cell swelling, loss of membrane integrity, and inflammatory release are shared with other lytic death modalities. RIPK3 is activated by autophosphorylation within the necrosome and subsequently phosphorylates MLKL, and this RIPK3-mediated phosphorylation of MLKL at the necrosome precedes MLKL translocation to the plasma membrane, where it causes cell death by compromising membrane integrity [5].

RIPK1 is important in the canonical TNF-driven pathway but is not universally required for necroptosis. TLR3/TLR4-TRIF signaling, ZBP1-mediated nucleic-acid sensing, and several specialized stress or viral pathways can activate RIPK3 with limited or no requirement for RIPK1 kinase activity [2,6-9]. Accordingly, contemporary mechanistic classification should define necroptosis by RIPK3-MLKL execution rather than by RIPK1 dependence alone.

Similarly, ROS generation, mitochondrial injury, lysosomal permeabilization, inflammasome activation, and inflammatory transcription can amplify or accompany necroptosis in particular contexts but are not universal defining components [2,10-12].

2. Initiation and Triggers

Necroptosis can be initiated through several major pathways that converge on RIPK3.

The best-characterized route begins with TNF binding to TNFR1. TNFR1 engagement recruits proteins including TRADD, RIPK1, TRAF2, cIAP1/2, and LUBAC into membrane-associated complex I [2,13-14]. Within this complex, RIPK1 is extensively regulated by ubiquitination. K63- and M1-linked ubiquitin chains generated by cIAP proteins and LUBAC recruit signaling modules including TAK1, IKK, TBK1, and IKKε, thereby supporting NF-κB/MAPK signaling and preventing premature activation of RIPK1 kinase-dependent death [13-16].

Thus, TNFR1 engagement does not intrinsically commit a cell to necroptosis. The earliest critical decision is whether RIPK1 remains restrained within a pro-survival signaling state or transitions into a cytotoxic kinase-active configuration.

When ubiquitin- and phosphorylation-dependent checkpoints are disrupted, RIPK1 can leave the receptor-associated complex and participate in cytosolic death complexes [2,13]. If FADD-caspase-8 activity remains competent, these complexes frequently favor apoptosis and simultaneously suppress necroptosis. If caspase-8 activity is absent, inhibited, or insufficient, kinase-active RIPK1 can engage RIPK3 through RHIM-RHIM interactions and promote assembly of the necrosome [2,17-18,51].

A second major route originates from innate immune receptors. TLR3 sensing of dsRNA or poly(I:C), and TLR4 signaling after LPS stimulation, can recruit the RHIM-containing adaptor TRIF. TRIF directly recruits RIPK3 and initiates RIPK3-MLKL signaling [6-7]. In appropriate cellular contexts this route can proceed without a requirement for RIPK1 kinase activity, demonstrating that RIPK3 rather than RIPK1 is the universal convergence kinase of necroptosis.

A third major route is mediated by ZBP1. ZBP1 contains Zα domains that recognize Z-form nucleic acids, including viral or endogenous Z-RNA/Z-DNA structures. After sensing these ligands, ZBP1 recruits RIPK3 through RHIM-dependent interactions and promotes formation of a noncanonical RIPK3 signaling complex [8,19]. Type I interferon signaling can increase ZBP1 expression and thereby sensitize cells to this form of necroptosis, particularly when FADD, caspase-8, or protective RIPK1 functions are compromised [20-21].

Cytosolic nucleic-acid sensors can additionally promote necroptosis indirectly. cGAS-STING- or RIG-I/MAVS-associated responses can generate type I interferon and TNF signaling that subsequently engages the RIPK3-MLKL axis [22]. More recent evidence also supports STING-driven ZBP1-dependent necroptosis in contexts that do not require TNFR1 or FADD [22-23].

Specialized stress-associated mechanisms also exist. Osmotic stress can activate the Na+/H+ exchanger SLC9A1, increase cytosolic pH, and drive RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptosis through a mechanism that can bypass conventional RHIM-dependent initiation [24]. The nonstructural protein NSs of SFTSV interacts with the RIPK3 kinase domain and forms biocondensates to promote RIPK3 autophosphorylation and necroptosis activation in an RHIM-independent manner [24].

Therefore, necroptosis has multiple initiators and sensors, but they converge mechanistically on productive RIPK3 activation and subsequent MLKL execution.

3. Core Mechanistic Machinery

In canonical TNFR1-driven necroptosis, RIPK1 transitions from a heavily regulated receptor-associated protein to a kinase-active cytosolic death-signaling molecule [2,13]. RIPK1 adopts an active conformation, undergoes autophosphorylation, and binds RIPK3 through RHIM-RHIM interactions.

Auto- and cross-phosphorylations between RIPK1 and RIPK3 form and activate the canonical necrosome, which appears as an amyloid-like structure of RHIM-dependent oligomerized RIPK3 [52,59]. However, amyloid organization itself should not be considered the universal defining property. The mechanistically essential feature is productive oligomerization of RIPK3 into a signaling architecture capable of activating MLKL.

MLKL phosphoinositide binding and membrane association are involved in the execution of necroptosis [30].

RIPK3 activation requires oligomerization and phosphorylation. In human RIPK3, phosphorylation at S227 is particularly important because it creates or stabilizes the MLKL-binding competent state [26]. Members of the CK1 family are recruited to the necrosome during necroptosis and phosphorylate RIPK3 on Ser227 [49].

Activated RIPK3 subsequently binds MLKL and phosphorylates the MLKL pseudokinase domain at species-specific activation residues [27]. Phosphorylation drives a major conformational transition within MLKL, disrupting its autoinhibited architecture and exposing the N-terminal four-helix bundle (4HB), the membrane-active effector domain [27-28].

MLKL then oligomerizes. Oligomerization is firmly established as necessary for efficient execution, but the exact stoichiometry remains unresolved. Trimers, tetramers, octamers, and larger polymers have been reported depending on experimental method, species, activation state, and membrane environment [28-29]. Therefore, the mechanistic requirement is oligomer formation rather than a single universal oligomer size.

After oligomerization, activated MLKL must traffic away from the necrosome and accumulate at cellular membranes, particularly the plasma membrane [5,30]. This translocation constitutes a distinct execution checkpoint rather than an automatic consequence of MLKL phosphorylation.

MLKL oligomers translocate to the plasma membrane via interaction between phosphatidylinositol phospholipids and a cluster of positively charged residues in the 4HB domain [50]. Sequential engagement of phosphoinositide-binding surfaces stabilizes membrane association and promotes membrane-disruptive activity [30-31]. Inositol-phosphate metabolism also contributes to MLKL activation and localization: IPMK/ITPK1-dependent inositol phosphates facilitate structural transitions required for oligomerization and membrane recruitment even after MLKL has been appropriately phosphorylated [32].

Thus, the core machinery is RIPK3 as the execution-activating kinase and MLKL as the membrane-directed terminal executor.

4. Propagation and Amplification

After initiation, several processes can amplify necroptotic signaling without constituting universally required pathway components.

Mitochondrial ROS can amplify RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in selected cellular systems. Oxidation of RIPK1-associated cysteine residues and enhanced RIPK1 S161 autophosphorylation can promote RIPK1-RIPK3 interaction and necrosome formation [10]. RIPK3 activity can in turn stimulate additional ROS production, creating a positive-feedback loop.

However, this amplification is highly context dependent. Several commonly used necroptotic systems execute efficient RIPK3-MLKL signaling despite limited dependence on ROS [2,33]. ROS should therefore be classified as an amplification module in responsive cells, not as a universal necroptotic intermediate.

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species promote necroptosis, and the kinase activity of receptor interacting protein 1 is required for TNF-induced necroptosis [10]. These observations demonstrate additional metabolic and ionic branches but do not modify the core RIPK3-MLKL definition.

After MLKL activation, membrane trafficking becomes another amplification layer. Golgi-, microtubule-, and actin-dependent transport can regulate the efficiency with which activated MLKL reaches the plasma membrane [5,30]. The quantity and kinetics of MLKL accumulation at the plasma membrane strongly influence how rapidly a cell crosses the threshold from reversible membrane stress to terminal lysis [5].

MLKL can additionally damage intracellular organelles. MLKL polymerization at lysosomes can cause lysosomal membrane permeabilization and cathepsin B-dependent injury in selected cells [11]. Activated MLKL can also affect mitochondria and promote mitochondrial DNA release, stimulating cGAS-STING-mediated inflammatory signaling [12]. These events may reinforce inflammation or cellular injury but are not required to define the universal necroptotic execution mechanism.

Necroptosis also generates inflammatory amplification after membrane damage. Ionic disturbances, including K+ efflux, can promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation. However, inhibition of downstream inflammasome components does not necessarily rescue necroptotic death, indicating that inflammasome activation is generally downstream of or parallel to MLKL-mediated execution rather than the core lethal event [35,57].

5. Protective/Negative Regulators

Necroptosis is heavily restrained by endogenous checkpoints.

The most important proximal brake is the FADD-caspase-8 system. Caspase-8-FLIP heterodimers retain a low but perceptible ability to cleave RIPK1, which may prevent stable RIPK1-RIPK3 association [45]. Caspase-8 consequently acts as a major molecular switch between apoptotic and necroptotic outcomes.

RIPK1 cleavage is particularly important in vivo. Non-cleavable RIPK1 mutants produce severe inflammatory and lethal phenotypes, demonstrating that caspase-8-mediated RIPK1 processing is not merely associated with apoptosis but is an active checkpoint preventing inappropriate RIPK1 kinase signaling and necroptosis [18].

The ubiquitination state of RIPK1 provides another major regulatory layer. cIAP1/2 and LUBAC attach ubiquitin chains to components of TNFR1 complex I, creating signaling platforms that recruit TAK1, IKK, TBK1, and IKKε [13-16]. These pathways restrict RIPK1 autoactivation and favor survival/inflammatory transcription rather than cytotoxicity.

TBK1 and IKKε can directly phosphorylate RIPK1 and suppress TNF-induced cell death [16]. TAK1 and IKK signaling also restrain RIPK1-dependent cytotoxicity. Loss of these checkpoints shifts TNFR1 signaling away from survival and toward cytosolic death-complex assembly [2,15].

CYLD has a more context-dependent role. CYLD regulates RIP1 ubiquitination within the NP-40 insoluble necrosome, where RIP1 is ubiquitinated [34]. Therefore, CYLD should not simply be classified as a universal negative regulator; in appropriate contexts it promotes necrosome maturation.

A20 can restrain necroptotic signaling by modifying RIPK3 ubiquitination, whereas phosphatases such as PPM1B can suppress RIPK3 activation by dephosphorylation. OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of RIPK3 can also inhibit necrosome formation [3].

ZBP1-dependent necroptosis has dedicated brakes. ADAR1 limits the accumulation or recognition of endogenous Z-form nucleic-acid ligands and thereby suppresses inappropriate ZBP1 activation [37]. ZBP1-S suppresses ZBP1-L-mediated cell death by binding to Z-nucleic acids via its Zα domains, acting as an endogenous inhibitor that competes with full-length ZBP1-L for binding Z-nucleic acid ligands to fine-tune ZBP1-mediated cell death and inflammation [36].

Even after MLKL has been activated, cells retain a downstream protective system. ESCRT-III-dependent membrane repair can remove, shed, or repair membrane regions damaged by MLKL and delay terminal rupture [39]. This repair capacity explains why MLKL activation is not immediately synonymous with irreversible cell death.

6. Commitment / Point of No Return

MLKL phosphorylation alone is not the point of no return.

Experiments using MLKL-targeting monobodies show that MLKL can become phosphorylated and oligomerized while cell death is still prevented if membrane translocation is blocked [30]. This demonstrates that biochemical MLKL activation precedes irreversible execution.

Similarly, ESCRT-III-mediated repair can temporarily compensate for membrane injury caused by activated MLKL [39]. Cells can therefore remain viable for a period after initial MLKL membrane engagement if membrane damage does not exceed repair capacity.

The best-supported commitment step is sustained accumulation of activated MLKL at the plasma membrane above a threshold that overwhelms membrane-repair mechanisms [5,30,39]. Once MLKL-induced permeability becomes sufficiently extensive, transmembrane ion gradients collapse, osmotic water entry accelerates, cell swelling progresses, and restoration of membrane homeostasis becomes impossible.

Thus, the most defensible point of no return is not RIPK3 activation, MLKL phosphorylation, or MLKL oligomerization independently, but terminal plasma-membrane accumulation and activity of MLKL sufficient to exceed the cell's compensatory repair capacity.

The exact quantitative MLKL density, pore number, ionic threshold, or membrane-damage threshold defining irreversibility remains unresolved.

7. Execution and Terminal Cause of Death

The core executor of necroptosis is activated MLKL [4,27-32].

RIPK3-dependent phosphorylation causes MLKL to adopt a membrane-active conformation, oligomerize, disengage from the upstream signaling complex, and traffic to the plasma membrane [27-30]. The plasma membrane translocation of MLKL oligomers is mediated by the interaction between phosphatidylinositol phospholipids and a cluster of positively charged residues in the 4HB domain .

Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that MLKL can directly compromise membranes. Expression of the N-terminal MLKL effector region can be cytotoxic, recombinant MLKL can disrupt phosphoinositide-containing artificial membranes, and preventing ion influx or osmotically protecting cells reduces terminal damage [4,40].

What physically kills the cell is therefore MLKL-dependent loss of plasma-membrane permeability control, followed by irreversible ionic disequilibrium, osmotic water entry, cellular swelling, and membrane rupture [4,40].

Necroptotic membranes develop nanoscale permeability defects. Experimental estimates support nanopore-like necroptotic lesions of approximately several nanometers, with one study reporting a lesion of roughly 4 nm associated with terminal swelling and rupture [38].

The precise biophysical structure of the MLKL-generated lesion remains unresolved. One model proposes that MLKL oligomers themselves form pore- or carpet-like membrane lesions [4] . TRPM7, a six-transmembrane spanning domain protein permeable to Mg2+, has been reported to be a downstream target of MLKL [39]. The strongest synthesis is therefore that membrane-localized activated MLKL directly creates or drives abnormal ion permeability, but the exact molecular architecture through which MLKL produces this permeability is not yet universally settled.

This uncertainty does not undermine MLKL's executor status. It separates the established fact that MLKL is required for terminal membrane permeabilization from the unresolved structural question of precisely how MLKL disrupts the lipid bilayer.

Downstream lysosomal permeabilization, mitochondrial perturbation, mtDNA release, cGAS-STING activation, inflammasome activation, and DAMP release can amplify injury and inflammatory consequences but should not be confused with the universal terminal executor [11-12,35].

8. Morphological Sequence

Morphologically, necroptosis begins in a cell that may initially appear structurally intact despite active upstream signaling.

TNFR1, TRIF, ZBP1, or other upstream pathways activate RIPK3 and MLKL before overt plasma-membrane disruption becomes evident [2,56].

After MLKL phosphorylation and oligomerization, activated MLKL traffics toward cellular membranes. Plasma-membrane accumulation is followed by progressive changes in ion permeability [5,30].

Ionic imbalance drives water entry and increased cell volume. The cell develops necrotic swelling rather than the shrinkage characteristic of classical apoptosis [1,4,55].

As permeability increases, organelles can swell, plasma-membrane blebbing may develop, and normal ionic compartmentalization progressively deteriorates. The membrane eventually becomes permeable to normally excluded molecules, followed by abrupt or explosion-like rupture [40].

Uncleared apoptotic cells undergo secondary necrosis and release intracellular lipids, proteases, oxidized molecules, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which amplify inflammation and destabilize plaque [54,60].

The chronological morphological sequence is therefore:

Structurally intact cell → upstream necroptotic signaling → RIPK3 activation → MLKL phosphorylation and oligomerization → MLKL trafficking to membranes → progressive plasma-membrane ion permeability → ionic disequilibrium → osmotic water influx → cellular and organelle swelling → increasing membrane damage → loss of membrane barrier function → terminal rupture → release of intracellular DAMPs.

None of these morphological features alone proves necroptosis because swelling and rupture are shared by pyroptosis, NECSO, accidental necrosis, and other lytic death processes.

9. Crosstalk and Differential Diagnosis

The most important distinction is between necroptosis and apoptosis.

TNFR1 signaling can produce either outcome depending on the molecular state of the death-signaling complex. When FADD-caspase-8 signaling is active, apoptosis is favored and RIPK1/RIPK3-dependent necroptosis is suppressed. When caspase-8 activity is absent or insufficient, RIPK3 and MLKL become available for necroptotic execution [17-18,41].

Thus, caspase-8 functions as a genuine molecular switch rather than merely a biomarker distinguishing two independent pathways.

Necroptosis must also be differentiated from pyroptosis [61]. Pyroptosis is executed by gasdermin family pore-forming proteins, whereas necroptosis is executed by MLKL [42]. Both produce ionic imbalance, swelling, membrane rupture, and inflammatory release, so morphology alone cannot discriminate them. Demonstration of RIPK3-MLKL dependency versus gasdermin cleavage and dependency provides the relevant mechanistic distinction.

Inflammasome activation can occur downstream of necroptosis because MLKL-mediated ion flux and K+ loss can activate NLRP3 [35]. Therefore, caspase-1 activation during necroptosis does not necessarily mean that pyroptosis is responsible for the death event.

ZBP1 can participate in more complex mixed-death programs. In some infectious and inflammatory contexts, ZBP1 scaffolds RIPK3, caspase-8, and inflammasome-associated machinery, producing PANoptotic phenotypes rather than pure necroptosis [43]. Such systems should not be described as exclusively necroptotic unless genetic experiments demonstrate dominant RIPK3-MLKL execution.

Necroptosis also differs from NECSO. Both end in ion imbalance, swelling, and plasma-membrane rupture, but the causal lesion differs. NECSO is driven by persistent sodium overload and energetic failure, whereas necroptosis requires RIPK3-mediated activation of MLKL, which directly generates or promotes the membrane permeability defect.

Ferroptosis is defined instead by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation rather than MLKL-mediated membrane permeabilization [1]. Although some upstream stresses can activate both RIPK3-dependent necroptosis and ferroptotic processes, they remain mechanistically distinct execution programs.

10. Experimental Validation

The strongest experimental proof of necroptosis requires demonstration of causal RIPK3-MLKL dependency rather than morphology alone.

First, genetic perturbation should establish pathway necessity. RIPK3 knockout or knockdown should suppress death when RIPK3 is required. More importantly, MLKL deletion should prevent terminal membrane permeabilization in bona fide necroptosis [2-3,53].

Second, RIPK3 activation should be demonstrated biochemically through phosphorylation, oligomerization, or necrosome-associated recruitment. Human RIPK3 S227 phosphorylation is particularly informative in appropriate systems [25-26].

Third, MLKL activation should be demonstrated. Appropriate evidence includes MLKL phosphorylation, conformational activation where experimentally accessible, oligomer formation, and especially translocation or accumulation of activated MLKL at the plasma membrane [27-30].

Phospho-MLKL alone should not be treated as sufficient proof of execution because phosphorylated MLKL can remain upstream of the irreversible membrane stage.

Fourth, pharmacological rescue should be aligned to the initiating route. CK1α, CK1δ, and CK1ε are components of the necrosome and phosphorylate human RIPK3 at S227 during necroptosis activation [25,58]. Selective RIPK3 inhibitors such as GSK872 and MLKL inhibitors such as necrosulfonamide are under investigation for suppressing MLKL activation and membrane translocation to attenuate necroptotic tissue injury and reduce chronic DAMP-mediated sterile inflammation [48].

Fifth, membrane execution should be measured directly through time-resolved plasma-membrane permeability assays, ion-flux measurements, osmotic swelling, propidium iodide or related membrane-impermeant probes, LDH release, and imaging of MLKL membrane localization [4-5,30,40].

Sixth, rescue at the execution level is particularly informative. Osmoprotectants or interventions limiting relevant ion flux can delay membrane rupture, while manipulations that preserve ESCRT-dependent membrane repair can alter the kinetics of lysis [44].

Seventh, competing death mechanisms must be excluded. Caspase-dependent apoptosis should be assessed through caspase activity, apoptotic morphology, and genetic or pharmacological perturbation. Pyroptosis should be evaluated through gasdermin cleavage/dependency and inflammasome signaling [17,42,47]. Ferroptosis should be excluded with appropriate lipid-peroxidation and pathway-specific rescue assays when relevant.

Finally, transcriptomic expression of RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL, ZBP1, or a necroptosis-related signature cannot demonstrate active necroptotic death. Such data can identify a necroptosis-associated transcriptional state, inflammatory context, or susceptibility state, but active necroptosis requires functional evidence of RIPK3-MLKL pathway activation and execution.

11. Molecular Hierarchy

Initiators/sensors: TNFR1; TLR3/TLR4-TRIF; ZBP1 sensing of Z-RNA/Z-DNA; interferon-dependent induction of ZBP1; cGAS-STING/RIG-I-linked signaling in selected contexts; specialized stress pathways including SLC9A1-mediated alkalinization [6-9,20,22-24].

CK1α, CK1δ, and CK1ε are necrosome components; CK1 phosphorylates human RIPK3 at S227 and contributes to necroptosis activation [25,58].

Core machinery: productive RIPK3-containing necrosome or equivalent RIPK3-activating complex → RIPK3 kinase activation → MLKL recruitment and phosphorylation [2,25-27].

Executor: phosphorylated, conformationally activated, oligomerized, membrane-localized MLKL [4-5,27-32].

MLKL on the plasma membrane causes an overload of selected ions, which drives water influx until the cells burst [40].

Negative regulators: caspase-8/FADD, cIAP1/2, LUBAC, TAK1, IKKα/β, TBK1/IKKε, RIPK1 ubiquitination and cleavage, A20, PPM1B, OGT, ADAR1, inhibitory ZBP1 isoforms, and downstream ESCRT-III membrane repair [13-18,37-39].

Context-dependent amplifiers: mitochondrial ROS, cytosolic Ca2+, JNK, lysosomal permeabilization, mitochondrial injury, cGAS-STING, and inflammasome activation [11-12,33-35].

Biomarkers/readouts: phospho-RIPK3, phospho-MLKL, MLKL oligomerization, MLKL plasma-membrane accumulation, membrane permeability, swelling, LDH release, DAMP release, and loss of viability. Among these, causal RIPK3-MLKL dependency is mechanistically more informative than terminal morphology alone.

12. Integrated Canonical Sequence

Canonical TNFR1 route:

TNF → TNFR1 → TRADD/RIPK1/TRAF2/cIAP1/2/LUBAC-containing complex I → ubiquitination- and kinase-dependent survival checkpoints normally restrain RIPK1 → failure/removal of these restraints + insufficient caspase-8 activity → RIPK1 kinase activation and cytosolic transition → RHIM-dependent RIPK1-RIPK3 necrosome formation → RIPK3 oligomerization and phosphorylation → MLKL recruitment → RIPK3-dependent MLKL phosphorylation → MLKL conformational opening → MLKL oligomerization → disengagement/trafficking away from the necrosome → phosphoinositide-dependent accumulation at the plasma membrane → MLKL-mediated abnormal ion permeability → membrane repair attempts through ESCRT-III → MLKL accumulation exceeds repair capacity → uncontrolled ionic disequilibrium → osmotic water influx → cellular swelling → catastrophic plasma-membrane rupture → release of intracellular contents and necroptotic cell death [2,4-5,13,17,27-32,39-40].

Alternative TRIF route:

dsRNA/LPS → TLR3/TLR4 → TRIF → RHIM-dependent RIPK3 activation → MLKL phosphorylation/oligomerization → plasma-membrane accumulation → ion permeability → osmotic rupture [6-7].

Alternative ZBP1 route:

viral or endogenous Z-RNA/Z-DNA → ZBP1 Zα-domain recognition → ZBP1-RIPK3 RHIM interaction → RIPK3 activation → MLKL phosphorylation/oligomerization → membrane localization → terminal permeability defect → osmotic lysis [8,19].

13. Final Mechanistic Interpretation

For RCDome, the most defensible operational definition is:

Necroptosis is an established regulated lytic cell-death modality defined by activation of RIPK3 and subsequent phosphorylation, oligomerization, and membrane translocation of MLKL. Multiple upstream signals, including TNFR1/RIPK1, TLR3/TLR4-TRIF, and ZBP1-mediated Z-nucleic-acid sensing, can converge on RIPK3; therefore, RIPK1 is a major but non-universal upstream component. Activated MLKL is the core executioner and causes or drives abnormal plasma-membrane ion permeability, leading to ionic disequilibrium, osmotic water influx, cellular swelling, irreversible membrane rupture, and death [2,4-8].

The strongest established core is therefore RIPK3 → MLKL phosphorylation → MLKL conformational activation and oligomerization → membrane trafficking and accumulation → membrane permeabilization → osmotic lysis.

Caspase-8/FADD, RIPK1 ubiquitination, cIAP/LUBAC-dependent signaling, TAK1/IKK/TBK1 checkpoints, and ESCRT-mediated membrane repair restrain the pathway at distinct stages [13-18,39]. ROS, lysosomal damage, mitochondrial perturbation, inflammasome activation, and inflammatory transcription can amplify necroptosis in selected contexts but are not universally required.

The best-supported commitment event is sustained accumulation of activated MLKL at the plasma membrane beyond the capacity of membrane-repair systems, rather than RIPK3 activation or MLKL phosphorylation alone [5,30,39].

The terminal cause of death is catastrophic loss of plasma-membrane barrier function caused by MLKL-dependent membrane permeabilization. The molecular mechanism by which MLKL causes the loss of plasma membrane impermeability in necroptosis remains controversial, with proposals that MLKL directly or indirectly permeabilizes the plasma membrane and that calcium influx via TRPM7 is required downstream of MLKL, though the latter is challenged by evidence attributing calcium oscillations to efflux from intracellular reservoirs [38].

Transcriptomic necroptosis signatures should therefore be interpreted as necroptosis-associated or susceptibility states unless accompanied by functional evidence of RIPK3-MLKL activation and membrane execution.
