# Entotic cell death

## Definition and Classification

Entotic cell death is an established **cell-in-cell form of regulated cell death** in which one living cell actively invades a neighboring living cell and becomes enclosed within a host-cell-derived vacuole [33-35]. The internalized cell can initially remain viable, but in the canonical lethal outcome it is subsequently killed non-cell-autonomously through host-controlled vacuole maturation, lysosomal fusion, acidification, and enzymatic degradation [1-5].

The defining sequence is therefore:

**stress or deadhesion → mechanical asymmetry between neighboring cells → cadherin-mediated cell-cell junction → polarized RhoA–ROCK–actomyosin contractility in the future inner cell → active invasion into the host → complete enclosure inside a single-membrane entotic vacuole → LC3-associated vacuole maturation → lysosomal fusion → vacuolar acidification → hydrolase-mediated degradation of the inner cell → entotic cell death.**

Entotic cell death differs fundamentally from conventional phagocytosis or efferocytosis.

In efferocytosis, the phagocyte engulfs a cell that is already dying or dead.

In canonical entotic cell death, the future inner cell is alive and **actively drives its own internalization** [1,3,6].

This distinction is critical.

The host cell does not simply engulf a passive target.

The invading cell generates polarized cortical forces that push it into its neighbor.

Entotic cell death is also different from apoptosis because the predominant lethal mechanism is not an autonomous caspase cascade within the inner cell.

Instead, the canonical inner cell is destroyed by a lysosomal compartment constructed and controlled by the host [2,4].

However, the term **entotic cell death** describes a broader cell-in-cell process whose outcome is not invariably death.

An internalized cell can:

survive,

escape,

divide within the host,

or die.

Therefore, **cell-in-cell formation alone is not sufficient to establish entotic cell death** [3-5].

---

## Two Mechanistically Distinct Phases

Entotic cell death is most clearly understood as two linked but separable modules.

### Phase I: Cell-in-cell invasion

A living cell actively penetrates a neighboring cell through:

cadherin-dependent adhesion → spatially polarized RhoA signaling → ROCK activation → myosin-II-dependent cortical contraction → active internalization.

### Phase II: Host-mediated execution

After internalization:

single-membrane entotic vacuole → LC3-associated processing → lysosome recruitment/fusion → acidification → lysosomal hydrolases → destruction of the inner cell.

Thus, the machinery that **creates the cell-in-cell structure** is not identical to the machinery that **kills the inner cell**.

This separation is one of the defining mechanistic features of entotic cell death.

---

# Initiation

## Matrix Detachment as the Classical Trigger

Entotic cell death was originally identified when epithelial cells lost attachment to extracellular matrix [1].

Under adherent conditions, integrin-mediated interactions with extracellular matrix provide mechanical and signaling constraints that oppose uncontrolled cell-cell internalization.

Loss of matrix attachment alters this mechanical balance.

The classical sequence is:

**loss of integrin–ECM attachment → cell rounding and altered cortical mechanics → enhanced relative importance of cadherin-mediated cell-cell interactions → polarized RhoA–ROCK–actomyosin contractility → entotic invasion.**

Thus, matrix detachment is an important initiating context.

However, it is not absolutely required.

Entotic cell death can also occur in matrix-attached cells when other signaling or mechanical perturbations create sufficient asymmetry between neighboring cells [7].

Therefore:

**matrix detachment = prototypical trigger**

but

**mechanical asymmetry = broader mechanistic principle.**

---

## Mechanical Asymmetry as the Unifying Principle

Many apparently unrelated entotic cell death triggers converge on a common physical outcome:

**two neighboring cells acquire different cortical tension or mechanical states.**

One cell becomes relatively more contractile and mechanically disadvantaged.

That cell tends to become the **inner/loser cell**.

In entosis, the neighboring cell that engulfs the internalized cell becomes the outer host cell, while the internalized cell is the inner cell [13].

Thus:

stress heterogeneity → unequal RhoA/ROCK/myosin activity → unequal cortical tension → winner/loser specification.

The higher-tension cell often becomes internalized.

This means that entotic cell death is not simply determined by which cell is stronger in a conventional sense.

Instead, excessive contractility can paradoxically promote invasion of that cell into the less contractile neighbor.

Glucose deprivation can induce entotic cell death in some cell types [29-30].

During glucose starvation, metabolic heterogeneity emerges among neighboring cells.

AMPK activation is associated with metabolic stress.

Cells experiencing stronger energetic stress and AMPK activity can acquire the mechanically disadvantaged state associated with entotic internalization.

Thus:

**glucose deprivation → energetic stress → AMPK-associated heterogeneity → altered cortical mechanics → winner/loser asymmetry → entotic cell death.**

This finding broadened the concept of entotic cell death beyond matrix detachment.

It demonstrated that metabolic competition can be converted into physical cell competition.

The host can subsequently derive nutrients from degradation of the internalized cell, linking entotic cell death to nutrient adaptation.

---

# UV Stress

Ultraviolet radiation can also induce entotic cell death [9].

In this context, stress signaling involving JNK and p38 contributes to differentiation between neighboring cells.

Cells displaying greater stress-associated signaling are preferentially internalized.

A working sequence is:

**UV irradiation → heterogeneous stress response → JNK/p38 activation in susceptible cells → altered mechanical state → preferential internalization → entotic cell death.**

UV-induced populations can display both apoptosis and entotic cell death.

Therefore, UV exposure should not be interpreted as an entotic cell death-specific trigger.

It creates a stress environment in which multiple death programs compete.

---

# Mitosis as an Entotic cell death-Promoting State

Mitotic cells naturally detach partially from substrate and undergo cell rounding.

These physical changes can create conditions favorable for entotic cell death [10].

Prolonged or abnormal mitosis can therefore increase cell-in-cell formation.

The sequence can be represented as:

**mitotic deadhesion/rounding → altered polarity and cortical tension → RhoA-associated contractility → interaction with neighboring cell → entotic invasion.**

Perturbation of Cdc42-associated polarity can further increase susceptibility in some settings.

This demonstrates that entotic cell death can emerge from a transient mechanical state even without permanent loss of extracellular matrix adhesion.

---

# TRAIL-Induced Entotic cell death

TRAIL provides an important example of crosstalk between death-receptor signaling and entotic cell death [11].

TRAIL engages:

TRAIL → DR4/DR5.

Normally, these receptors are well known for activating extrinsic apoptosis.

However, colorectal cancer models demonstrate that TRAIL can also promote entotic cell death.

Thus, the same extracellular ligand can generate divergent cellular outcomes:

**TRAIL → DR4/DR5 → apoptotic branch**

or

**TRAIL → DR4/DR5 → entotic cell death-associated branch.**

Caspase-8 participates in this context, but its role should not be interpreted simply as conventional apoptotic execution.

The key point is that TRAIL signaling can alter the state of neighboring cells in a manner that promotes cell-in-cell invasion.

Therefore:

**DR4/DR5 are context-dependent entotic cell death initiators, not universal entotic cell death receptors.**

---

# Cadherins as the Core Cell-Cell Adhesion System

## E-Cadherin

Cadherin-mediated adhesion is central to canonical epithelial entotic cell death.

E-cadherin forms adherens junctions between neighboring epithelial cells.

Experimental introduction of epithelial cadherins into cadherin-deficient cells can promote entotic cell death [12].

Thus:

**E-cadherin engagement → adherens junction formation → mechanical coupling of neighboring cells → spatial organization of RhoA signaling → entotic invasion.**

E-cadherin does not itself generate the contractile force required for internalization.

Instead, it establishes the mechanical interface against which polarized actomyosin forces act.

---

## P-Cadherin and Context-Dependent Cadherins

P-cadherin can also support entotic cell death in appropriate epithelial contexts [12,36].

Non-epithelial tumor cells may use alternative cadherins, including N-cadherin-associated mechanisms [13].

Therefore, the essential concept is not one unique cadherin molecule.

It is:

**stable cadherin–catenin-mediated intercellular adhesion capable of mechanically coupling the two cells.**

The precise cadherin depends on cell lineage and tissue context.

---

# The Cadherin–Catenin Junction Is a Mechanical Organizer

The adherens junction is not simply molecular glue.

It establishes spatial information that polarizes the invading cell.

Cadherin complexes recruit proteins that locally regulate RhoA activity.

A particularly important mechanism involves p190A RhoGAP.

At the cell-cell junction:

**cadherin junction → p190A RhoGAP recruitment → local conversion of RhoA-GTP to RhoA-GDP → suppression of contractility at the contact interface.**

Meanwhile, the opposite cortex can retain or increase RhoA activation.

This spatial separation creates a polarized contractile cell.

Thus:

junction side → low RhoA,

distal/rear cortex → high RhoA.

This polarity is fundamental for directed invasion.

---

# PDZ-RhoGEF

PDZ-RhoGEF contributes to RhoA activation away from the adherens junction [3,14].

The resulting spatial arrangement is:

**p190A RhoGAP at junction → local RhoA inhibition**

plus

**PDZ-RhoGEF at distal cortex → RhoA activation.**

Together these create:

**polarized RhoA signaling → polarized actomyosin contraction → directional force toward the neighboring host cell.**

This provides a mechanistic explanation for how two initially adjacent cells acquire a directional inner-versus-outer relationship.

---

# RhoA as the Central Mechanical Switch

RhoA is one of the most important signal transducers in entotic invasion [3,15].

Active RhoA stimulates ROCK kinases and associated actomyosin machinery.

The canonical sequence is:

**RhoA-GTP → ROCK1/ROCK2 → increased myosin light-chain phosphorylation → actin–myosin II contraction → increased cortical tension.**

Importantly, RhoA activity is not uniform around the invading cell.

It is spatially polarized.

This localized contractility is what converts a symmetric cell-cell interaction into directional invasion.

---

# ROCK1 and ROCK2

ROCK kinases transmit RhoA signaling to the contractile apparatus.

They promote myosin activity by increasing phosphorylation of myosin light chain and by suppressing myosin phosphatase activity.

Thus:

**RhoA → ROCK1/2 → ↑ pMLC → myosin-II activation → cortical contraction.**

ROCK inhibition strongly suppresses entotic internalization in canonical models [15].

This makes RhoA–ROCK signaling one of the strongest experimentally validated parts of the invasion mechanism.

However, ROCK activity primarily drives **entry into the host**.

It does not directly perform the subsequent lysosomal killing.

---

# Myosin II and Cortical Tension

Myosin II interacts with cortical actin to generate contractile forces.

In the future inner cell:

**RhoA/ROCK → myosin light-chain phosphorylation → myosin-II contractility → high cortical tension.**

The pMLC-high cell commonly becomes the loser/internalized cell [8,13].

This mechanical asymmetry creates a striking paradox:

the more contractile cell actively drives itself into its softer neighbor.

Therefore, entotic cell death represents a form of **active invasion**, not passive engulfment.

---

# Winner and Loser Cell Specification

Neighboring cells can acquire distinct roles.

### Loser / future inner cell

typically displays:

higher cortical tension,

stronger polarized RhoA–ROCK–myosin signaling,

increased pMLC,

active invasive behavior.

### Winner / host cell

typically displays:

relatively lower contractility,

greater deformability,

ability to accommodate the invading cell.

Thus, winner/loser status is strongly influenced by relative mechanical state rather than simply by absolute expression of a single marker.

---

# Rac1 and KRAS in Winner-Cell Behavior

Rac1 can counterbalance RhoA-driven contractility.

High Rac1 activity can reduce contractile myosin behavior and favor host-cell status [3,8].

Similarly, oncogenic KRAS has been associated with winner behavior in some entotic cell death models.

Thus:

**Rac1-high / reduced contractility → greater probability of host/winner status.**

Conversely:

**high RhoA–ROCK–myosin tension → greater probability of inner/loser status.**

This illustrates that Rho and Rac signaling contribute to a mechanical competition between neighboring cells.

---

# RND3/RhoE as a Negative Regulator

RND3/RhoE restrains RhoA–ROCK-associated contractility.

Loss of RND3 can therefore enhance entotic cell death [16].

A simplified sequence is:

**RND3 loss → reduced restraint of RhoA/ROCK → increased cortical contractility → increased entotic susceptibility.**

In hepatocellular carcinoma models, RND3 loss is also associated with changes involving LAMP1 and the lysosomal phase of entotic cell death [16].

Thus, RND3 can influence both propensity for invasion and downstream processing depending on context.

---

# LPAR2–G12/13–PDZ-RhoGEF Pathway

G-protein-coupled receptor signaling provides another route toward polarized RhoA activation.

LPAR2 signaling can activate heterotrimeric G proteins of the G12/13 family [14].

The sequence is:

**LPAR2 → G12/13 → PDZ-RhoGEF → RhoA → ROCK → actomyosin contractility.**

This pathway acts primarily in the invading cell.

It demonstrates that extracellular lipid signaling can regulate the mechanical machinery responsible for cell-in-cell invasion.

---

# Orai1-Mediated Ca²⁺ Signaling

Calcium signaling provides an additional invasion mechanism.

Orai1 has been identified as an entotic cell death-associated Ca²⁺ channel [17].

Orai1 localization is spatially organized during cell-in-cell formation.

The proposed sequence is:

**polarized Orai1 activity → localized Ca²⁺ influx → calmodulin activation → MLCK activation → myosin light-chain phosphorylation → enhanced actomyosin contractility → invasion.**

Thus, Ca²⁺ signaling converges on the same mechanical output as RhoA–ROCK signaling:

**myosin activation and cortical force generation.**

---

# Septins and Orai1 Polarization

Septin-associated organization contributes to localization of Orai1-associated calcium signaling [17].

This creates another level of polarity within the invading cell.

The functional principle is:

**spatially restricted ion signaling → spatially restricted contractility.**

This supports the concept that entotic cell death depends not simply on globally increased RhoA or Ca²⁺ but on asymmetric mechanical organization.

---

# Calmodulin and MLCK

Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin.

Activated calmodulin stimulates myosin light-chain kinase.

Therefore:

**Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → MLCK → MLC phosphorylation → actomyosin contraction.**

This pathway can operate alongside RhoA–ROCK.

The two pathways converge on myosin activity and can therefore cooperate to generate the force required for invasion.

---

# Actin Cytoskeleton

Actin provides the structural framework through which entotic force is transmitted.

The invading cell requires dynamic cortical actin organization.

Thus:

RhoA/ROCK signaling + Ca²⁺/MLCK signaling → actin–myosin organization → cortical tension and directional contraction.

Pharmacological interference with actin dynamics disrupts entotic invasion.

However, actin is not the terminal lethal executor.

Its principal role occurs during internalization.

---

# mDia1

Formin-associated actin regulation, including mDia1, contributes to the architecture of the contractile cortex in entotic cell death [3,18].

This supports actin filament organization downstream of Rho-family signaling.

Thus:

**Rho signaling → mDia1-associated actin organization + ROCK-associated myosin activation → polarized contractile apparatus.**

The combined system provides both:

structural actin filaments,

and

myosin-generated contractile force.

---

# Ezrin and Entotic Blebbing

Ezrin contributes to coupling the plasma membrane to cortical actin.

During entotic invasion, the inner cell can display polarized membrane blebbing and a uropod-like rear structure [14].

The sequence can be represented as:

**Rho-dependent cortical remodeling → ezrin-associated membrane–actin coupling → polarized blebs/uropod-like structure → active cell penetration.**

This blebbing should not be confused with apoptotic blebbing.

In entotic cell death, it participates in **invasion mechanics**.

Therefore:

**blebs during entotic cell death ≠ evidence of apoptosis.**

---

# Vinculin-Rich Mechanical Ring

A specialized mechanical interface forms between adherens junctions and the actomyosin cortex [19].

Vinculin-rich structures can couple cadherin-based adhesion to contractile forces.

The functional arrangement is:

**cadherin junction → vinculin-containing mechanical interface → contractile actomyosin network.**

This ring-like organization helps transmit forces between the two cells while maintaining sufficient adhesion for one cell to penetrate the other.

Without such coupling, contractility would not necessarily be converted efficiently into directional invasion.

This represents an emerging mechanistic extension of the invasion machinery.

Because this evidence is newer than the RhoA–ROCK–cadherin framework, Plastin-3 should presently be considered a **context-dependent or emerging invasion regulator**, rather than a universal defining component [31].

---

# Completion of Internalization

As invasion progresses:

cadherin junction stabilizes cell-cell contact → polarized actomyosin force pushes the inner cell inward → host membrane wraps around the invading cell → complete enclosure occurs.

The host cell often undergoes dramatic deformation.

Its nucleus can become crescent- or sickle-shaped as it is displaced around the internalized cell [3-4].

Eventually:

**inner cell completely enclosed → entotic cell-in-cell structure.**

At this point, however, death has **not necessarily occurred**.

This is one of the most important mechanistic distinctions in the pathway.

---

# Internalization Is Not the Commitment to Death

After complete enclosure, the inner cell can remain viable for prolonged periods [4-5].

Possible outcomes include:

escape from the host,

release,

survival,

division inside the host,

or death.

Therefore:

**cell internalization ≠ point of no return.**

This distinguishes entotic cell death from mechanisms in which formation of the characteristic structural lesion itself is immediately lethal.

The internalized cell still has multiple possible fates.

---

# The Entotic Vacuole

After complete internalization, the inner cell is surrounded by a host-cell-derived membrane.

This structure is termed the **entotic vacuole**.

The vacuole originates largely from the plasma membrane of the host as it envelops the invading cell.

Critically, it is a:

**single-membrane compartment.**

It is **not** a canonical double-membrane autophagosome [2,6].

This structural distinction is essential for correct interpretation of the subsequent LC3 recruitment.

---

# Autophagy-Related Machinery Without Canonical Macroautophagy

Autophagy-related proteins participate strongly in entotic killing.

However, the pathway does not require formation of a classical double-membrane autophagosome around the inner cell.

Instead, autophagy machinery is redirected toward the pre-existing single membrane of the entotic vacuole [2].

Therefore:

**entotic vacuole → ATG-dependent LC3 lipidation directly on single membrane.**

This resembles LC3-associated processing rather than canonical macroautophagy.

---

# LC3 Recruitment

LC3 is recruited and lipidated onto the entotic vacuole [2].

This is a central step in progression toward lysosomal killing.

The sequence is:

**internalized viable cell → single-membrane entotic vacuole → LC3 conjugation to vacuolar membrane → increased capacity for lysosomal fusion and degradation.**

LC3 accumulation therefore marks an important transition from simple containment toward active vacuole maturation.

However, LC3 positivity alone should not automatically be equated with completed inner-cell death.

---

# ATG5 and ATG7

LC3 lipidation requires components of the autophagy conjugation machinery, including ATG5 and ATG7 [2].

Thus:

**ATG5/ATG7 → LC3 conjugation to entotic vacuole → maturation toward lysosomal compartment.**

Genetic disruption of these proteins interferes with canonical entotic killing.

They therefore participate directly in the execution phase.

Nevertheless, their function here differs from conventional autophagosome biogenesis.

---

# VPS34

VPS34 also participates in entotic vacuole processing [2].

The combined machinery can be represented as:

**VPS34 + ATG5/ATG7-dependent LC3 conjugation → entotic vacuole maturation → lysosomal fusion.**

VPS34 therefore belongs to the host-controlled execution machinery.

---

# ULK–ATG13–FIP200 Independence

A crucial mechanistic distinction is that entotic LC3 recruitment does not require the full upstream machinery normally used to initiate canonical macroautophagy [2,20].

In particular, the classical:

ULK → ATG13 → FIP200

initiation module is not required in the same way.

Thus:

**canonical autophagy:**

ULK complex → phagophore initiation → double-membrane autophagosome → LC3 lipidation.

**entotic death:**

pre-existing single-membrane entotic vacuole → ATG5/ATG7/VPS34-associated LC3 lipidation → lysosomal maturation.

This distinction prevents incorrect classification of entotic cell death as ordinary autophagy-dependent cell death.

---

# Lysosome Recruitment

LC3-associated processing promotes interaction of the entotic vacuole with lysosomal compartments [2].

LAMP1-positive lysosomes accumulate around or fuse with the entotic vacuole.

Thus:

**LC3-positive entotic vacuole → lysosome recruitment/fusion → LAMP1-positive degradative compartment.**

This marks progression from containment toward active host-mediated killing.

---

# LAMP1

LAMP1 serves as an important marker of lysosomal maturation.

As the entotic vacuole acquires lysosomal characteristics:

**LC3-positive vacuole → LAMP1 acquisition → acidic hydrolase-containing compartment.**

Increased LAMP1-associated processing has also been linked to regulation of entotic cell death in RND3-deficient contexts [16].

However, LAMP1 should be interpreted primarily as evidence of lysosomal identity, not as an independent lethal enzyme.

---

# Vacuolar Acidification

Acidification represents one of the strongest events associated with entotic execution [21].

While the internalized cell remains alive, vacuolar pH can remain relatively moderate.

Near the transition to death, vacuolar pH drops substantially.

Thus:

**viable internalized cell → relatively non-lethal vacuolar environment → lysosomal maturation → sharp pH reduction → strongly acidic entotic vacuole → inner-cell degradation.**

This acidification is particularly important because lysosomal hydrolases require an acidic environment for optimal activity.

---

# Acidification as a Candidate Commitment Event

Because internalization itself is reversible, the most plausible point of commitment lies later.

The strongest candidate is:

**LC3-associated maturation → lysosomal fusion → profound vacuolar acidification.**

Once the inner cell is enclosed within an acidic, hydrolase-rich lysosomal compartment, recovery becomes increasingly unlikely.

Thus:

early internalization → potentially reversible,

mature lysosomal entotic vacuole → probable commitment to death.

No single universally accepted molecular point of no return has yet been established.

Therefore, acidification should be described as the **best-supported candidate commitment transition**, not an absolutely proven universal threshold.

---

# Lysosomal Hydrolases

Once lysosomes fuse with the vacuole, acid hydrolases gain access to the compartment.

These enzymes progressively destroy the internalized cell.

The execution sequence is:

**lysosomal fusion → acidic pH → activation of lysosomal hydrolases → degradation of cellular proteins, membranes, nucleic acids and organelles → loss of structural and metabolic integrity → death.**

Unlike apoptosis, death does not primarily result from an executioner-caspase cascade generated within the inner cell.

Instead, the destructive machinery is largely supplied by the host.

This makes entotic cell death fundamentally **non-cell-autonomous**.

---

# Cathepsins

Cathepsins are candidate lysosomal effectors of terminal entotic destruction.

Cathepsin B has been implicated in some experimental models [4,11].

A context-dependent sequence is:

**lysosome fusion → acidic activation of cathepsin B and other hydrolases → degradation of inner-cell components.**

However, current evidence does not support one single cathepsin as a universal entotic cell death executor.

It is more accurate to consider the terminal machinery as a **lysosomal hydrolase network**.

---

# Proximate Cause of Entotic Death

The most defensible current model is:

**host-controlled conversion of the entotic vacuole into an acidic lysosomal compartment causes progressive enzymatic destruction of the living inner cell.**

Thus, the proximate cause is:

**lysosomal degradation within the host.**

This differs fundamentally from:

apoptosis → caspase-mediated self-destruction,

ferroptosis → lipid-peroxidative membrane damage,

necroptosis → MLKL-mediated membrane disruption,

pyroptosis → gasdermin pore formation.

In entotic cell death:

**another living cell constructs the lethal compartment.**

---

# mTORC1 and Amino-Acid Regulation

The fate of the internalized cell is also regulated by nutrient availability [22].

Amino acids and mTORC1 influence whether the entotic vacuole progresses toward efficient killing.

The supplied experimental framework indicates that amino-acid withdrawal or mTOR inhibition can interfere with LC3-associated entotic killing and shift the fate of internalized cells toward survival or alternative death outcomes.

Thus:

**nutrient availability → mTORC1-associated regulation → entotic vacuole maturation and fate selection.**

This adds a metabolic checkpoint after physical internalization.

---

# Why Engulfment Alone Is Insufficient

Entotic vacuoles mature more slowly than classical phagosomes [22].

This delay is biologically important.

It means:

**inner cell internalized**

does not automatically lead immediately to:

**inner cell degraded.**

Instead, a regulated post-engulfment interval exists during which the fate of the inner cell remains plastic.

This interval helps explain observations of:

survival,

escape,

and division within host cells.

---

# PI(4,5)P2 and Vacuole Identity

Recent work suggests that entotic vacuoles maintain an unusual phosphoinositide identity [23].

PI(4,5)P2 can remain associated with the entotic compartment for prolonged periods.

Its eventual disappearance appears to accompany progression toward lysosomal destruction.

Thus:

**newly formed entotic vacuole → persistent PI(4,5)P2 state → delayed membrane identity transition → PI(4,5)P2 loss → increased permissiveness for lysosomal killing.**

This suggests that commitment is not controlled only by LC3.

A delayed change in vacuolar membrane identity may determine when the host switches from **containment** to **destruction**.

Because this model is relatively recent, it should be regarded as an emerging refinement rather than an established universal checkpoint.

---

# mTORC1-Dependent Vacuolar Processing After Killing

After the inner cell is destroyed, the host must process the large amount of cellular material remaining inside the vacuole.

mTOR contributes to entotic vacuole fission and post-degradative processing [24].

Thus:

**inner-cell degradation → large nutrient-rich entotic vacuole → mTOR-associated vacuole fission/scission → redistribution and utilization of degraded material.**

This allows the host to recover nutrients from the killed cell.

---

# PIKfyve

PIKfyve contributes to vacuole maturation and nutrient recovery after cell engulfment [25].

The pathway involves phosphoinositide remodeling required for processing large degradative compartments.

Thus:

**post-engulfment vacuole → PIKfyve-associated maturation → lysosomal/vacuolar processing → nutrient recovery.**

PIKfyve therefore acts predominantly during or after the lysosomal phase rather than during initial invasion.

---

# TRPML1

PIKfyve-associated lysosomal signaling intersects with TRPML1-dependent processes during maturation and nutrient recovery.

This provides a mechanism by which ion signaling and phosphoinositide remodeling regulate processing of the large entotic vacuole.

Again, this is a host-cell function.

It should not be mistaken for signaling occurring autonomously within the dying inner cell.

---

# Nutrient Recovery by the Host

Entotic cell death can provide a metabolic advantage to host cells.

The killed inner cell represents a large nutrient source.

Following lysosomal degradation:

proteins → amino acids,

membranes → lipids,

other cellular macromolecules → reusable metabolites.

Thus:

**entotic killing → lysosomal digestion → nutrient release → host metabolic benefit.**

This may be particularly advantageous in nutrient-poor environments such as tumors.

Consequently, entotic cell death can function simultaneously as:

cell competition,

regulated death,

and nutrient acquisition.

---

# Early Entotic cell death Is Reversible

One of the most important experimental observations is that an internalized cell can survive [3-5].

Therefore, early entotic cell death should be considered reversible.

Possible trajectory:

**active invasion → enclosure → temporary residence → release/escape → survival.**

Alternative trajectory:

**active invasion → enclosure → division of the inner cell.**

Lethal trajectory:

**active invasion → enclosure → lysosomal maturation → acidification → degradation.**

This fate plasticity makes direct longitudinal observation particularly important.

---

# No Universal Point of No Return

The pathway does not yet have a universally demonstrated irreversible molecular checkpoint.

The strongest candidate is the transition from a relatively stable entotic vacuole to:

**LC3-positive → LAMP1/lysosome-associated → strongly acidic → hydrolase-rich.**

Once this transition is sufficiently advanced, the internalized cell is exposed to destructive lysosomal conditions and recovery appears unlikely.

Nevertheless, the exact event that converts a long-lived non-lethal entotic vacuole into a lethal one remains incompletely understood.

This is one of the major mechanistic gaps in the field.

---

# Negative Regulation Before Internalization

Several mechanisms can suppress entotic cell death by preventing the mechanical asymmetry required for invasion.

Examples include:

reduced RhoA activity,

reduced ROCK activity,

reduced myosin phosphorylation,

loss of appropriate cadherin adhesion,

increased Rac1-associated host-like behavior,

and RND3-mediated restraint of RhoA signaling.

Thus, resistance can occur at the invasion stage:

**insufficient adhesion or insufficient polarized contractility → failure of cell-in-cell formation.**

---

# Negative Regulation After Internalization

A second set of checkpoints operates after the cell is already inside.

These involve:

vacuole membrane identity,

LC3 recruitment,

lysosomal fusion,

acidification,

nutrient/mTOR signaling,

and lysosomal competence.

Therefore, an entotic event can be blocked at two fundamentally different levels:

**invasion failure**

or

**execution failure.**

This distinction is important experimentally.

---

# Crosstalk with Apoptosis

Entotic cell death is classically described as a non-apoptotic form of cell death [1-2].

Canonical entotic killing does not require the characteristic autonomous caspase-execution pathway of apoptosis.

However, entotic cell death and apoptosis can coexist.

An internalized cell may undergo apoptosis instead of lysosomal entotic death.

This becomes particularly evident when normal entotic vacuole processing is perturbed or when the initiating stimulus can engage both pathways [3,9,11].

Thus:

**cell internalization → lysosomal maturation → entotic death**

or

**cell internalization → autonomous apoptotic activation → apoptosis inside host.**

These are mechanistically different outcomes.

---

# TRAIL as an Entotic cell death–Apoptosis Branch Point

TRAIL is particularly informative because it can stimulate both programs [11].

The same ligand/receptor system can produce:

**TRAIL → DR4/DR5 → caspase-associated apoptotic pathway**

and

**TRAIL → DR4/DR5 → entotic cell death-promoting signaling → cell-in-cell invasion.**

The supplied evidence indicates a context-dependent role for caspase-8 and BAX/BAK-associated signaling in determining the relative balance among entotic killing, apoptosis, survival, and release.

Therefore, TRAIL-induced entotic cell death should be described as a specific crosstalk model rather than the canonical homeostatic definition of entotic cell death.

---

# UV-Induced Competition Between Death Modes

UV exposure similarly produces mixed outcomes [9].

Some cells undergo apoptosis.

Others become preferentially internalized through entotic cell death.

Blocking one death mode can alter the frequency of another.

Thus, entotic cell death should not be interpreted as an isolated biochemical pathway operating independently from the cellular stress landscape.

Its initiation frequently depends on **relative differences between neighboring cells**.

---

# Entotic cell death Is Not Canonical Autophagy-Dependent Cell Death

The participation of ATG proteins and LC3 can create confusion with autophagy-dependent cell death.

However, the mechanisms are distinct.

Canonical autophagy:

cytoplasmic cargo → phagophore → double-membrane autophagosome → lysosome.

Entotic cell death:

whole living neighboring cell → active invasion → host-derived single-membrane vacuole → LC3 conjugation directly onto that membrane → lysosome.

Thus, autophagy-related proteins are repurposed for processing a macroendocytic compartment [2].

The pathway should therefore remain classified as **entotic cell death**, not ordinary autophagy-dependent cell death.

---

# Entotic cell death Versus Efferocytosis

The distinction from efferocytosis is fundamental.

### Efferocytosis

dying/dead target → PS/find-me signaling → phagocyte recognizes target → phagocyte actively engulfs it.

### Entotic cell death

living target → cadherin-mediated interaction → **target itself generates RhoA–ROCK–myosin force** → target actively invades neighboring cell.

Thus:

**efferocytosis = eater-driven uptake of a dying cell.**

**entotic cell death = inner-cell-driven invasion of a living cell.**

The subsequent use of LC3 and lysosomal degradation can superficially resemble phagocytic processing, but the initiation logic is entirely different.

---

# Entotic cell death Versus Cell Cannibalism

Cell cannibalism is a broader descriptive term for one cell consuming another.

It does not automatically specify the molecular mechanism.

Entotic cell death is more restrictive.

For an event to be mechanistically assigned as entotic cell death, evidence should support:

living-cell invasion,

cadherin-mediated adhesion,

polarized RhoA–ROCK–actomyosin activity in the future inner cell,

and characteristic host-vacuole maturation.

Therefore:

**cell inside another cell ≠ automatically entotic cell death.**

---

# Entotic cell death Versus Emperipolesis

Emperipolesis describes viable cells located within other cells, often immune cells traversing or temporarily residing within a host.

The internalized cell can remain viable and exit.

Entotic cell death differs because the invading-cell contractility machinery and lysosomal killing program form a characteristic mechanistic sequence.

Nevertheless, morphology alone cannot reliably distinguish these processes.

Live-cell behavior and molecular markers are needed.

---

# Entotic cell death Versus Emperitosis

Emperitosis is particularly important because it also involves cell-in-cell structures but uses different execution machinery [26].

In emperitosis, immune killer cells can become internalized into tumor cells.

Granzyme B is then re-imported or retained in a manner that triggers:

**granzyme B → caspase-dependent apoptosis of the internalized immune cell.**

Thus:

emperitosis → apoptotic execution inside a cell-in-cell structure.

Entotic cell death → host-vacuole lysosomal execution.

This is why identifying an inner cell dying inside another cell is not sufficient to classify the mechanism.

---

# Morphological Sequence

The characteristic temporal sequence is:

normal neighboring epithelial cells → loss of matrix attachment or alternative entotic cell death-promoting stress → cell rounding → cadherin-based contact → polarization of cortical RhoA–ROCK–myosin signaling in one cell → blebbing/uropod-like rear organization → active penetration into the neighboring cell → progressive host-cell wrapping → full enclosure.

After enclosure:

viable inner cell within single-membrane vacuole → delayed vacuole maturation → LC3 recruitment → LAMP1/lysosome acquisition → sharp acidification → progressive inner-cell degradation → disappearance of nucleus and cellular architecture → residual material digested by host.

The host cell may show:

crescent-shaped or displaced nucleus,

large cytoplasmic vacuole,

and subsequent nutrient utilization.

---

# What Physically Kills the Inner Cell?

The strongest mechanistic evidence supports:

**lysosomal destruction inside an acidified entotic vacuole.**

The causal sequence is:

LC3-associated single-membrane processing → lysosome fusion → low vacuolar pH → lysosomal hydrolase activity → progressive degradation of essential cellular structures → irreversible loss of viability.

Thus, unlike apoptosis:

the inner cell does not primarily destroy itself.

The host cell builds the compartment that destroys it.

This is why entotic cell death is described as a **non-cell-autonomous regulated cell death mechanism**.

---

# Experimental Identification

The strongest evidence for entotic cell death combines several approaches.

Time-lapse microscopy was employed to corroborate the entosis staging based on sequential analysis of individual images [27,32].

This directly distinguishes entotic cell death from passive engulfment.

---

## Confocal and Ultrastructural Imaging

Confocal and electron microscopy can confirm:

complete enclosure,

cell-cell junctions,

single-membrane entotic vacuoles,

host nuclear deformation,

and subsequent lysosomal maturation.

However, static microscopy alone is weaker than live imaging for determining which cell actively drove internalization.

---

# Testing the Invasion Machinery

Strong mechanistic perturbations include:

cadherin disruption → prevents appropriate intercellular junctions.

RhoA inhibition → suppresses polarized contractility.

ROCK inhibition → suppresses myosin activation and invasion.

myosin-II inhibition → prevents force generation.

actin disruption → prevents mechanical invasion.

PDZ-RhoGEF disruption → decreases polarized RhoA activation.

Orai1/CaM/MLCK perturbation → reduces Ca²⁺-dependent contractility.

These interventions determine whether a cell-in-cell structure is generated through the canonical entotic invasion machinery.

---

# Testing the Execution Machinery

After internalization has already occurred, a different set of perturbations should be used.

ATG5 or ATG7 loss → impaired LC3 lipidation.

VPS34 perturbation → impaired vacuole processing.

lysosomal disruption → impaired entotic killing.

inhibition of vacuolar acidification → reduced lysosomal degradation.

cathepsin perturbation → tests hydrolase involvement in selected systems.

PIKfyve/mTOR-associated manipulations → test vacuole processing and nutrient recovery.

This allows researchers to distinguish:

**failure to internalize**

from

**failure to kill after internalization.**

---

# Cell-in-Cell Morphology Alone Is Insufficient

Histological cell-in-cell structures occur in many tumors.

However, morphology cannot establish mechanism.

Similar structures may arise through:

entotic cell death,

phagocytosis,

cannibalism,

emperipolesis,

or emperitosis.

Therefore, a mechanistic diagnosis should ideally demonstrate:

**living-cell invasion + entotic contractility machinery + host-derived vacuole + lysosomal execution.**

---

# Transcriptomic Evidence Requires Caution

Entotic cell death is highly dependent on:

cell-cell topology,

relative mechanical state,

subcellular RhoA polarization,

cytoskeletal force,

and host-vacuole maturation.

These features cannot be directly inferred from bulk or single-cell transcript abundance.

Expression of genes such as:

CDH1,

RHOA,

ROCK1/2,

ATG5,

ATG7,

or lysosomal genes

may indicate biological competence or an entotic cell death-associated state.

However:

**an “entotic cell death gene signature” does not demonstrate that entotic cell death is actively occurring.**

Direct spatial, morphological, live-cell, or functional evidence remains necessary to establish the process.

---

# Biological Consequences in Cancer

Entotic cell death is especially prominent in tumors.

Its biological effect can be context dependent.

By killing neighboring cells, entotic cell death can function as a form of cell competition.

The host cell may acquire nutrients from the destroyed inner cell.

Thus:

**entotic cell death → elimination of competitor + nutrient acquisition → potential selective advantage of winner cell.**

Conversely, entotic cell death can remove detached tumor cells and potentially suppress some cellular populations.

Therefore, entotic cell death is not intrinsically tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive in every context.

Its biological outcome depends on which cells become winners and which become losers.

---

# Host-Cell Genomic Consequences

Large internalized cells can mechanically distort the host.

The host nucleus may become displaced or compressed.

Cell-in-cell structures can also interfere with host-cell division.

These consequences provide additional routes through which entotic cell death may contribute to tumor heterogeneity.

However, such downstream consequences are not defining components of the lethal pathway itself.

One of the most important conceptual interpretations of entotic cell death is **mechanical cell competition** [8,28].

Neighboring cells compare relative mechanical states.

The higher-tension cell often becomes internalized.

The lower-tension cell becomes the host.

Thus:

heterogeneous population → mechanical differences → winner/loser selection → entotic cell death → loser-cell elimination.

This makes entotic cell death fundamentally relational.

Unlike many RCD pathways, susceptibility cannot always be understood from the molecular state of one isolated cell.

Its fate depends on the state of its neighbor.

---

# Protective and Resistance Mechanisms

An inner cell can avoid entotic death at several stages.

Before invasion:

weak cadherin engagement → reduced entotic interaction.

low RhoA/ROCK/myosin activity → reduced invasive force.

higher Rac1/low contractility → increased host/winner probability.

RND3 activity → restraint of RhoA-associated invasion.

After internalization:

failure of LC3 recruitment → impaired vacuole maturation.

failure of lysosomal fusion → delayed killing.

insufficient vacuolar acidification → increased probability of inner-cell survival or alternative fate.

altered amino-acid/mTOR signaling → changes in fate of internalized cell.

Therefore, protection can mean either:

**avoiding internalization**

or

**surviving after internalization.**

# Integrated Canonical Sequence

The most defensible canonical mechanism begins with two neighboring epithelial cells exposed to a condition that generates mechanical asymmetry.

A common route is:

**matrix detachment → loss of integrin–ECM mechanical opposition → increased dependence on cadherin-mediated cell-cell contacts.**

Alternative entry routes include:

glucose starvation → AMPK-associated metabolic heterogeneity,

UV stress → JNK/p38-associated stress heterogeneity,

mitotic rounding/deadhesion,

TRAIL → DR4/DR5-associated signaling,

or altered polarity/adhesion programs.

These different inputs converge on:

**unequal cortical mechanics between neighboring cells.**

Then:

cadherin-mediated junction formation → recruitment of junctional regulatory machinery → p190A RhoGAP suppresses RhoA at the cell-cell contact while PDZ-RhoGEF and related pathways activate RhoA at the opposite cortex.

Additional signaling can reinforce this polarity:

LPAR2 → G12/13 → PDZ-RhoGEF → RhoA,

and, in relevant systems:

septin-associated Orai1 → Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → MLCK.

These signals converge on:

**RhoA → ROCK1/2 → MLC phosphorylation**

plus

**Ca²⁺–CaM–MLCK → MLC phosphorylation**

→ myosin-II activation → polarized actomyosin contractility.

Actin, myosin II, mDia1, ezrin, cadherin-catenin complexes and vinculin-associated mechanical interfaces then generate and transmit the physical forces required for invasion.

The future inner cell develops high cortical tension and active rear contractility.

It pushes itself into the softer neighboring host:

**polarized contraction → rear blebbing/uropod-like force → progressive cell penetration → host membrane wrapping → complete enclosure.**

At this stage:

**entotic cell-in-cell structure exists, but the inner cell may still be alive.**

The host-derived membrane forms a:

**single-membrane entotic vacuole.**

The process then enters the fate-determination phase.

If the vacuole remains non-lethal:

inner cell may survive → divide → or escape.

If lethal maturation begins:

**VPS34 + ATG5/ATG7-associated machinery → LC3 lipidation on single entotic vacuole membrane.**

This is followed by:

**lysosome recruitment/fusion → acquisition of LAMP1-positive lysosomal characteristics → marked vacuolar acidification → activation/delivery of lysosomal hydrolases.**

The acidic lysosomal compartment then progressively destroys:

inner-cell membrane systems,

cytoplasmic proteins,

organelles,

and nuclear structures.

Thus:

**acidic entotic vacuole → hydrolase-mediated degradation → irreversible destruction of the inner cell → entotic cell death.**

After killing:

mTORC1-, PIKfyve- and associated vacuolar processing mechanisms contribute to fission, maturation and nutrient recovery.

The resulting metabolites can subsequently support the host cell.

---

# Candidate Commitment Sequence

A useful temporal model is:

### Reversible stage

neighboring cells → cadherin interaction → active invasion → complete internalization.

At this stage:

survival,

escape,

or division remain possible.

### Transition toward commitment

entotic vacuole begins membrane-identity remodeling → LC3 lipidation → lysosomal recruitment.

### Strongest candidate commitment stage

lysosomal fusion + profound vacuolar acidification → hydrolase-rich compartment.

### Terminal execution

progressive enzymatic degradation → collapse of essential inner-cell structures → irreversible loss of viability.

Thus, **full internalization should not be represented as the point of no return**.

The best current candidate is the onset of mature lysosomal vacuole function.

---

# Final Mechanistic Interpretation

Entotic cell death is best understood as a **two-cell, non-cell-autonomous regulated death system** in which the cell that ultimately dies actively participates in the first half of its own fate but does not execute the final lethal machinery itself.

The first phase is mechanical:

**stress/deadhesion → cadherin coupling → spatial RhoA polarization → ROCK/MLCK-dependent myosin activation → actomyosin contractility → active living-cell invasion.**

The second phase is lysosomal:

**entotic vacuole → ATG5/ATG7/VPS34-dependent LC3 recruitment → lysosomal fusion → vacuolar acidification → hydrolase-mediated degradation → death.**

This distinction explains why entotic cell death is fundamentally different from conventional phagocytosis.

The future inner cell does not wait to be engulfed.

It actively invades.

It also explains why entotic cell death differs from autonomous RCDs such as apoptosis.

The primary lethal compartment is built by another cell.

The defining execution lesion is therefore not:

caspase activation,

MOMP,

lipid peroxidation,

gasdermin pores,

or MLKL-mediated membrane disruption.

It is:

**host-controlled lysosomal destruction of a living internalized cell.**

The pathway also contains an unusually important period of reversibility.

A fully internalized cell can remain alive, escape, or divide.

Therefore, cell-in-cell morphology is not sufficient to diagnose entotic death.

The strongest candidate point of commitment occurs only when the host converts the initially permissive entotic vacuole into an LC3-associated, lysosome-containing, strongly acidic degradative compartment.

Even this transition is not yet represented by one universally established molecular switch.

Recent work on persistent PI(4,5)P2 identity suggests that delayed membrane remodeling may help determine when the vacuole changes from a stable containment compartment to a lethal lysosomal compartment.

Accordingly, the major unresolved question is:

**what molecular signal instructs the host to stop maintaining a potentially reversible entotic vacuole and initiate irreversible lysosomal killing?**

For RCD classification, the most defensible operational definition is:

**Entotic cell death is a non-cell-autonomous regulated cell-death process in which a living cell actively invades a neighboring cell through cadherin-coupled, polarized RhoA–ROCK–actomyosin forces and, in the lethal outcome, is subsequently destroyed inside a host-derived single-membrane vacuole through LC3-associated lysosomal fusion, acidification, and hydrolase-mediated degradation.**
