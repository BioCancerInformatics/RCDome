## Intrinsic Apoptosis

Intrinsic apoptosis is a canonical form of regulated cell death in which diverse intracellular stresses are integrated by the BCL-2 protein family at mitochondria, culminating in mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, release of apoptogenic mitochondrial proteins, apoptosome formation, caspase-9 activation, executioner caspase-3/-7 activation, and ordered cellular demolition [1-5].

The defining mechanistic event is mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, or MOMP [54]. Morphological characteristics such as cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, chromatin condensation, phosphatidylserine exposure, DNA fragmentation, and apoptotic-body formation are characteristic downstream consequences but do not by themselves define intrinsic apoptosis [1-2,5].

The canonical sequence is:

cellular stress → stress-specific signaling → activation or accumulation of BH3-only proteins → neutralization of anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins and/or activation of BAX/BAK → BAX/BAK oligomerization → MOMP → release of cytochrome c, SMAC/DIABLO and other mitochondrial proteins → APAF1 apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → extensive substrate proteolysis → apoptotic cellular dismantling.

Intrinsic apoptosis is regulated by the BCL-2 protein family, in which anti-apoptotic proteins such as MCL-1 and BCL-XL neutralize pro-apoptotic BAX and BAK, while BH3-only proteins sense cellular stress to either activate BAX/BAK and promote apoptosis or neutralize them [44].

## Initiating Cellular Stresses

Unlike extrinsic apoptosis, intrinsic apoptosis does not require activation of a plasma-membrane death receptor.

Its initiating signals arise from disturbances of intracellular or extracellular homeostasis that are interpreted as sufficiently severe or persistent to activate the mitochondrial apoptotic machinery [1-2].

Important triggers include DNA damage, replication stress, oncogenic stress, oxidative stress, growth-factor withdrawal, nutrient or metabolic stress, endoplasmic-reticulum stress, mitotic or microtubule disruption, loss of appropriate extracellular-matrix attachment, and selected infection-associated or inflammatory stresses [1-2,6].

These stimuli do not necessarily activate one universal upstream sensor.

Instead, different stresses engage distinct signaling systems that converge on BH3-only members of the BCL-2 family.

Therefore, the upstream part of intrinsic apoptosis is highly context dependent, whereas the mitochondrial BCL-2-family checkpoint and downstream caspase cascade are substantially more conserved.

## DNA-Damage-Induced Intrinsic Apoptosis

DNA damage represents one of the best-characterized initiating routes.

Double-strand breaks, replication-associated lesions, and other genotoxic insults activate DNA-damage-response proteins including ATM and ATR.

These kinases activate downstream checkpoint signaling and stabilize or activate transcription factors including p53 [7].

The pathway can be represented as:

DNA damage → ATM/ATR-associated damage response → checkpoint signaling → p53 stabilization/activation → transcription of pro-apoptotic genes.

PUMA frequently represents a dominant mediator of p53-induced mitochondrial apoptosis in several cell types, whereas NOXA can provide additional or context-specific activity [8-9,47-48,51].

p53 can also increase BAX expression in some settings.

Thus:

DNA damage → p53 → PUMA ± NOXA ± BAX → BCL-2-family imbalance → BAX/BAK activation → MOMP.

PUMA is particularly powerful because it can interact with several anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins, thereby releasing multiple restraints on mitochondrial apoptosis.

NOXA displays a more selective binding profile, with strong activity against MCL-1 and A1/BFL-1 [10].

Therefore, the precise death response reflects both which BH3-only proteins are induced and which anti-apoptotic proteins dominate in the particular cell.

p53 is nevertheless not universally required for DNA-damage-induced intrinsic apoptosis.

Alternative transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms can activate the mitochondrial pathway when p53 function is absent or compromised.

Thus, p53 should be considered an important upstream stress-response regulator rather than an obligatory component of the core intrinsic apoptosis machinery.

## ER-Stress-Induced Intrinsic Apoptosis

Endoplasmic-reticulum stress provides another major route toward mitochondrial apoptosis.

Accumulation of unfolded proteins activates the unfolded protein response.

If adaptive ER-stress signaling cannot restore proteostasis, pro-apoptotic signals can become dominant.

One experimentally established mechanism involves BIM [11].

ER stress can increase BIM activity through at least two regulatory mechanisms.

PP2A-dependent dephosphorylation can stabilize BIM by reducing its degradation.

ER-stress-associated transcriptional responses involving CHOP and C/EBPα can also increase BIM expression.

Thus:

persistent ER stress → BIM stabilization and/or transcriptional induction → increased BH3-only activity → neutralization of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins and promotion of BAX/BAK activation → MOMP.

This illustrates an important principle of intrinsic apoptosis: different stress sensors can converge on different BH3-only proteins while ultimately reaching the same mitochondrial checkpoint.

## Oxidative Stress

Reactive oxygen species can also initiate intrinsic apoptosis.

Excessive ROS can damage DNA, proteins, lipids, and mitochondria.

Depending on the cell type and insult, oxidative stress can activate p53, p63, JNK/c-Jun, PUMA, NOXA, BIM, or overlapping combinations of these pathways [12-13].

The sequence can therefore be represented as:

oxidative stress → stress-specific DNA-damage/JNK/p53/p63 signaling → BH3-only protein activation → BCL-2-family imbalance → BAX/BAK → MOMP.

There is no universal ROS-specific BH3-only protein.

For example, different neuronal stress models can engage mutually distinct combinations of PUMA, NOXA, BIM, p53, and JNK signaling [12].

Thus, ROS functions as an upstream stress rather than a defining execution mechanism of intrinsic apoptosis.

## Growth-Factor Withdrawal and Other Intracellular Stresses

Survival signals normally suppress pro-apoptotic BCL-2-family activity through kinase-dependent mechanisms and maintenance of anti-apoptotic proteins.

Growth-factor withdrawal removes these survival inputs.

Consequently, BH3-only proteins can accumulate or become activated, shifting the mitochondrial balance toward BAX/BAK-dependent permeabilization.

Similarly, mitotic catastrophe-associated stress, excessive oncogenic signaling, loss of extracellular-matrix attachment, proteotoxic stress, and several metabolic insults can converge on this mitochondrial decision system.

Intrinsic apoptosis therefore represents a common downstream response to diverse forms of irreparable cellular damage rather than a pathway triggered by one unique ligand or receptor.

## Connection with Extrinsic Apoptosis

Extrinsic death-receptor signaling can also engage the intrinsic pathway.

This occurs particularly in type II extrinsic apoptosis.

Activated caspase-8 cleaves BID.

Cleavage generates truncated BID, or tBID.

tBID translocates to mitochondria and promotes BAX/BAK activation [14,43].

The sequence is:

death receptor → FADD/DISC → caspase-8 → BID cleavage → tBID → BAX/BAK → MOMP.

This does not make intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis identical.

The initiating event remains extrinsic because the signal originated at a death receptor.

The mitochondrial pathway functions as an amplification module inserted downstream of caspase-8.

Therefore, classification should distinguish:

intrinsic apoptosis initiated directly by intracellular stress,

from

extrinsic type II apoptosis that recruits intrinsic mitochondrial machinery as a secondary amplification step.

## The BCL-2 Family as the Core Decision Network

The BCL-2 family represents the principal molecular integration system controlling MOMP [3-4,15,55].

Its members can be divided functionally into three major groups.

Pro-apoptotic effectors such as BAX and BAK directly participate in permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane [56].

Anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BCL-w, and A1/BFL-1 restrain mitochondrial apoptosis.

BH3-only proteins act primarily as stress-responsive regulators that neutralize anti-apoptotic proteins and, according to some mechanistic models, directly activate BAX or BAK.

The balance between these interacting protein groups determines mitochondrial apoptotic competence.

Thus:

pro-survival BCL-2-family dominance → BAX/BAK restrained → mitochondrial integrity → survival.

BH3-only activity exceeds pro-survival buffering capacity → BAX/BAK activation → MOMP → apoptosis.

The system behaves as a molecular threshold rather than a simple linear switch controlled by one protein.

Anti-apoptotic proteins bind BH3 domains of pore-formers and activator BH3-only proteins in their BH3 domain-binding groove, inhibiting their function by sequestering them [45].

Their relative abundance varies markedly among cell types.

Consequently, two cells exposed to the same apoptotic stress can have different sensitivities because they depend on different anti-apoptotic proteins.

For example:

BCL-2/BCL-xL-dominant survival state → sensitivity to BH3 signals targeting these proteins.

MCL-1-dominant survival state → resistance unless MCL-1 is also neutralized.

This selective dependence is one molecular basis for mitochondrial apoptotic priming.

## BH3-Only Proteins

BH3-only proteins function as stress-responsive initiators of the mitochondrial apoptotic checkpoint.

Important members include BIM, BID, PUMA, NOXA, BAD and others.

They do not all have identical binding properties.

BIM and PUMA interact relatively broadly with several anti-apoptotic family members.

BAD preferentially neutralizes proteins including BCL-2, BCL-xL and BCL-w.

NOXA preferentially targets MCL-1 and A1/BFL-1 [10].

This selective binding architecture produces functional complementarity.

For example:

BAD → inhibition of BCL-2/BCL-xL/BCL-w.

NOXA → inhibition of MCL-1/A1.

BAD + NOXA → broader removal of anti-apoptotic restraints → increased probability of BAX/BAK activation.

Therefore, apoptosis sensitivity cannot be predicted simply from the total expression of BH3-only proteins.

The identity of both BH3-only and anti-apoptotic partners matters.

## Direct-Activation Versus Derepression Models

One major mechanistic question remains incompletely resolved: whether BH3-only proteins must directly activate BAX and BAK.

The classical direct-activation model divides BH3-only proteins into activators and sensitizers.

In this framework, proteins such as BIM, BID and sometimes PUMA directly interact with BAX or BAK and induce the structural changes required for activation.

Sensitizers such as BAD and NOXA do not strongly activate BAX/BAK directly but instead neutralize anti-apoptotic proteins.

The sequence is:

stress → activator BH3-only protein → direct BAX/BAK engagement → conformational activation → mitochondrial insertion/oligomerization.

Sensitizer BH3-only proteins facilitate this process by removing inhibitory anti-apoptotic proteins.

However, genetic reconstitution experiments have challenged the necessity of obligatory direct BH3-only activation [16-17].

These studies show that BAX/BAK activation can occur even in the absence of BID, BIM, PUMA and p53 when major anti-apoptotic restraints are removed.

This supports an alternative derepression model:

stress → BH3-only proteins neutralize BCL-xL/MCL-1 and related survival proteins → BAX/BAK are released from inhibition → membrane-associated BAX/BAK activation proceeds without a mandatory direct BH3-only activator.

In some models, current evidence has been proposed to support anti-apoptotic neutralization as essential, whereas the extent to which direct BH3-only activation is universally required in living physiological cells has been suggested to remain context dependent [47-48].

A rigorous mechanistic description should therefore avoid presenting either model as universally settled.

## BAX Activation

BAX is predominantly cytosolic or only loosely mitochondrial in many unstressed cells.

During apoptosis, BAX undergoes conformational changes and accumulates at the mitochondrial outer membrane.

Activation exposes regions involved in membrane insertion and oligomerization.

Activated BAX molecules associate with one another and with membrane lipids, producing progressively larger oligomeric structures.

The sequence is:

BAX activation → mitochondrial translocation/insertion → conformational rearrangement → dimer formation → higher-order oligomerization → membrane permeabilization.

The exact architecture of the BAX pore remains an active area of investigation.

Rather than forming a conventional rigid protein channel comparable to many ion channels, BAX and BAK are thought to cooperate with the lipid bilayer to create dynamic apoptotic pores [3-4,49].

## BAK Activation

BAK is generally constitutively associated with the mitochondrial outer membrane.

In healthy cells, its activation is restrained by anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins.

After apoptotic signaling overcomes this inhibition, BAK undergoes conformational activation, dimerization, and higher-order oligomerization.

Thus:

BH3-dependent derepression/activation → BAK conformational change → BH3-domain-mediated dimerization → oligomerization → outer-membrane pore formation.

BAX and BAK have substantial functional redundancy in many contexts.

Loss of either one alone may therefore produce incomplete protection, whereas combined BAX/BAK loss can strongly suppress conventional MOMP-dependent intrinsic apoptosis.

However, relative dependence varies by cell type and stress.

## BOK and Other MOMP Regulators

BOK is another pro-apoptotic BCL-2-family protein capable of contributing to mitochondrial membrane permeabilization in selected settings [1-2].

It should not generally replace BAX/BAK as the canonical core effector.

The strongest consensus architecture remains centered on BAX and BAK.

BOK can instead be represented as a context-dependent additional mitochondrial effector.

## Mitochondrial Outer Membrane Permeabilization

Once BAX and/or BAK form sufficiently extensive membrane pores, the mitochondrial outer membrane becomes permeable to proteins normally retained within the intermembrane space.

This event is MOMP.

The core sequence is:

activated BAX/BAK → membrane oligomerization → apoptotic pore expansion → MOMP.

MOMP fundamentally changes intracellular compartmentalization.

Proteins whose functions were confined to mitochondria gain access to the cytosol.

Major released factors include cytochrome c, SMAC/DIABLO and OMI/HtrA2 [1-4].

AIF can also become mobilized in specific circumstances but represents a parallel caspase-independent mitochondrial death effector rather than the principal executor of canonical intrinsic apoptosis.

MOMP is consequently both a structural lesion and a signaling event.

## Why MOMP Is the Main Commitment Checkpoint

Widespread MOMP is generally regarded as the major commitment point of intrinsic apoptosis [1-5].

Before MOMP, removal of the initiating stress or restoration of the BCL-2-family balance can frequently preserve viability.

After widespread MOMP, several irreversible or difficult-to-reverse events occur simultaneously.

Cytochrome c leaves mitochondria.

SMAC and additional proteins enter the cytosol.

Mitochondrial respiratory functions deteriorate.

Mitochondrial membrane potential and metabolic organization can become compromised.

Executioner-caspase signaling begins.

Thus, the probability of successful cellular recovery decreases dramatically.

However, MOMP should not be described as universally irreversible in every biological context.

Partial MOMP can occur in only a fraction of mitochondria.

Sublethal mitochondrial permeabilization can generate DNA damage or signaling without immediate cell death.

Rare cells may recover after advanced apoptotic signaling.

Therefore, widespread MOMP is the canonical commitment event, but partial MOMP represents an important exception to the simple all-or-none model [4-5,43].

## MOMP Is Distinct from Mitochondrial Permeability Transition

MOMP should also be distinguished from mitochondrial permeability transition-dependent necrosis.

BAX/BAK-mediated MOMP primarily disrupts the outer mitochondrial membrane and releases intermembrane-space proteins.

Mitochondrial permeability transition involves a major permeability change in the inner mitochondrial membrane and can produce mitochondrial swelling, energetic collapse, and necrotic death.

Although mitochondrial changes can overlap experimentally, these are mechanistically distinct processes.

Therefore:

BAX/BAK → outer-membrane permeabilization → intrinsic apoptosis.

Sustained mitochondrial permeability transition → inner-membrane failure → MPT-driven necrosis.

The presence of mitochondrial dysfunction alone is not sufficient to distinguish the pathways.

## Cristae Architecture and Cytochrome c Mobilization

MOMP does not completely explain cytochrome c release because a substantial fraction of cytochrome c is localized within mitochondrial cristae rather than freely accessible in the peripheral intermembrane space.

Cristae architecture therefore provides an additional layer of regulation [18-21,46].

OPA1 is a major regulator of mitochondrial cristae organization.

OPA1 oligomers help maintain narrow crista junctions that retain cytochrome c within internal mitochondrial compartments [18-19].

During apoptosis, OPA1-containing structures are remodeled.

Crista junctions widen.

Previously sequestered cytochrome c becomes more accessible to the permeabilized outer membrane.

Thus:

apoptotic signaling → OPA1 complex remodeling → crista junction opening → mobilization of cristae-associated cytochrome c → efficient mitochondrial release.

This process helps explain how cells can rapidly release a large fraction of mitochondrial cytochrome c after MOMP.

## Relationship Between MOMP and Cristae Remodeling

The exact temporal relationship between BAX/BAK-mediated MOMP and cristae remodeling is nuanced.

Some experiments indicate that OPA1 complex disassembly and crista junction opening depend on BH3 and BAX/BAK signaling but can be experimentally separated from the physical outer-membrane permeabilization event [20].

Therefore, cristae remodeling should not simply be treated as an automatic passive consequence of MOMP.

It may occur in parallel with, immediately before, or downstream of outer-membrane permeabilization depending on the system.

This distinction matters because complete cytochrome c mobilization requires both access from the cristae compartment and an exit route across the outer membrane.

## DRP1, MiD49/51 and Mitochondrial Remodeling

Mitochondrial dynamics can also participate in apoptotic structural remodeling.

DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission involving MiD49/51 has been implicated in cristae remodeling and cytochrome c release in selected experimental models [21].

The sequence in these settings can be represented as:

apoptotic signaling → mitochondrial-dynamics remodeling → DRP1/MiD49/51-associated changes → cristae reorganization → facilitated cytochrome c release.

However, DRP1 is not universally required for intrinsic apoptosis.

It should therefore be treated as a context-dependent mitochondrial architecture regulator rather than part of the minimal defining machinery.

## Cytochrome c Release

Cytochrome c normally participates in mitochondrial electron transport.

After MOMP and cristae mobilization, cytosolic cytochrome c acquires a second function as a death signal.

Its release creates the critical molecular link between mitochondrial permeabilization and the caspase cascade.

Thus:

BAX/BAK-dependent MOMP + cristae mobilization → cytosolic cytochrome c → APAF1 activation.

The presence of cytochrome c in the cytosol is therefore a strong biochemical indicator of mitochondrial apoptotic signaling.

However, cytochrome c release alone does not fully determine execution because downstream apoptosome and IAP regulation remain important.

## Apoptosome Formation

APAF1 is maintained in an autoinhibited conformation in healthy cells.

Cytosolic cytochrome c binds APAF1.

ATP or dATP participates in the conformational transition that allows APAF1 oligomerization [22-25].

Multiple activated APAF1 molecules assemble into a heptameric wheel-like complex known as the apoptosome.

The sequence is:

cytochrome c → APAF1 binding → nucleotide-dependent APAF1 activation → APAF1 oligomerization → heptameric apoptosome.

The apoptosome provides a molecular platform for recruitment and activation of procaspase-9 [53].

CARD domains in APAF1 interact with the CARD of procaspase-9.

This concentrates procaspase-9 molecules within the apoptosome and promotes their catalytic activity.

Therefore:

APAF1–cytochrome c apoptosome → procaspase-9 recruitment → caspase-9 activation.

## Caspase-9

Caspase-9 is the principal initiator caspase of canonical intrinsic apoptosis.

Its function is downstream of MOMP and apoptosome formation.

Activated caspase-9 cleaves executioner procaspases, especially procaspase-3 and procaspase-7 [22-23].

Thus:

MOMP → cytochrome c → APAF1 → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7.

Caspase-9 activation is highly regulated.

Its activity depends on apoptosome assembly and remains susceptible to inhibition by XIAP.

Therefore, mitochondrial permeabilization does not simply generate an uncontrolled protease cascade; additional post-mitochondrial checkpoints determine the efficiency of execution.

## XIAP as a Post-Mitochondrial Brake

XIAP is one of the most important endogenous inhibitors of post-mitochondrial caspase signaling.

XIAP can bind and inhibit processed caspase-9 and executioner caspase-3/-7 [26-28].

Thus:

MOMP → apoptosome → caspase activation

can still be restrained by:

XIAP → inhibition of caspase-9 and caspase-3/7.

This creates a second major survival checkpoint downstream of the BCL-2-family-controlled mitochondrial checkpoint.

Cells can therefore resist death either by preventing MOMP or by suppressing the caspase cascade after MOMP.

## SMAC/DIABLO

SMAC/DIABLO is released from mitochondria during MOMP.

Its principal apoptotic function is antagonism of IAP proteins, particularly XIAP [26-28].

SMAC contains an N-terminal IAP-binding motif that interacts with XIAP BIR domains.

This interaction relieves XIAP-mediated suppression of caspases.

The sequence is:

MOMP → SMAC release → SMAC–XIAP interaction → decreased XIAP caspase inhibition → enhanced caspase-9 and caspase-3/7 activity.

Therefore, mitochondrial permeabilization activates apoptosis through two complementary mechanisms:

cytochrome c generates new caspase activity through the apoptosome,

while SMAC removes an inhibitory brake on this caspase activity.

## OMI/HtrA2

OMI/HtrA2 is another mitochondrial protein released during apoptosis [29-31].

Like SMAC, HtrA2 can interact with IAP proteins.

In addition, HtrA2 possesses serine protease activity capable of promoting IAP degradation or cleavage.

Thus:

MOMP → HtrA2 release → IAP binding and proteolytic inactivation → reduced caspase inhibition.

HtrA2 therefore reinforces the post-mitochondrial commitment to caspase activation.

However, it is not the primary canonical executioner of intrinsic apoptosis.

## Regulation of the Apoptosome

Apoptosome signaling is itself subject to regulation.

Cytochrome c can facilitate APAF1 activation not only through direct binding but also by relieving inhibitory interactions involving proteins such as 14-3-3ε [32].

Protein kinase A phosphorylation can inhibit caspase-9 activation [50,52].

Therefore, the post-MOMP pathway is not simply:

cytochrome c → automatic caspase activation.

Instead:

cytochrome c → regulated APAF1 assembly → regulated caspase-9 activity → executioner-caspase threshold.

## Caspase-3 and Caspase-7

Caspase-3 and caspase-7 are the principal executioner proteases.

Once sufficiently activated, they cleave hundreds to more than a thousand cellular substrates [33-34].

These substrates include proteins involved in nuclear architecture, cytoskeletal organization, DNA repair, signaling, metabolism, RNA processing, membrane organization and cell adhesion.

The execution phase can therefore be represented as:

caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → broad proteolytic substrate cleavage → coordinated destruction of cellular organization.

Caspase-3 is particularly important for several classical apoptotic phenotypes.

However, caspase-3 and caspase-7 have partially distinct substrate preferences and cannot always be treated as completely interchangeable.

## ICAD–CAD and DNA Fragmentation

One important caspase substrate is ICAD, the inhibitor of caspase-activated DNase.

In viable cells, ICAD binds CAD and prevents its nuclease activity.

Executioner caspases cleave ICAD.

CAD is then released and enters or acts within the nucleus.

The sequence is:

caspase-3 → ICAD cleavage → CAD activation → internucleosomal chromosomal DNA fragmentation.

This generates the characteristic nucleosomal DNA fragmentation associated with apoptosis.

Thus, DNA laddering is downstream of executioner-caspase activity rather than the mechanism that initiates mitochondrial apoptosis.

Loss of DNA fragmentation does not necessarily mean that MOMP or other lethal apoptotic processes did not occur.

## Nuclear and Cytoskeletal Dismantling

Executioner caspases also cleave structural proteins that maintain nuclear and cellular architecture.

Nuclear-lamina disruption facilitates nuclear condensation and fragmentation.

Cytoskeletal protein cleavage contributes to cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, detachment, and eventual formation of apoptotic bodies.

The sequence is:

executioner caspases → cleavage of nuclear/cytoskeletal substrates → loss of structural integrity → chromatin condensation + nuclear fragmentation + membrane blebbing.

These events represent controlled demolition rather than catastrophic primary membrane rupture.

## AIF as a Parallel Mitochondrial Death Effector

Apoptosis-inducing factor, or AIF, is a mitochondrial flavoprotein that can participate in caspase-independent nuclear injury [35-38].

Following appropriate mitochondrial permeabilization and processing, AIF can translocate from mitochondria to the nucleus.

Nuclear AIF contributes to chromatin condensation and large-scale DNA fragmentation.

Thus:

mitochondrial injury/MOMP → AIF mobilization → cytosolic transit → nuclear translocation → chromatin condensation and large-scale DNA damage.

AIF should nevertheless not be described as the principal executor of canonical caspase-dependent intrinsic apoptosis.

The core canonical pathway remains:

MOMP → cytochrome c → APAF1 → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7.

AIF represents a parallel mitochondrial death output that can reinforce death when caspase signaling is limited or operate prominently in selected cellular contexts.

It should also be distinguished from PARP1-dependent parthanatos, where AIF participates in a mechanistically distinct pathway.

## Phosphatidylserine Exposure

Apoptotic cells commonly externalize phosphatidylserine from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane.

Externalized phosphatidylserine provides an important signal for recognition and engulfment by phagocytes.

However, the source literature indicates that phosphatidylserine exposure and DNA fragmentation do not necessarily share identical caspase dependence in every cell type [39-40].

Some primary cells retain substantial phosphatidylserine externalization even when caspase activity is suppressed.

Therefore, phosphatidylserine exposure is a characteristic apoptotic phenotype but is not sufficient by itself to diagnose intrinsic apoptosis.

The defining mechanism remains mitochondrial BCL-2-family-dependent permeabilization.

## Apoptotic Morphology

Morphology develops downstream of the molecular execution machinery.

The general sequence is:

intracellular stress → no immediate visible change → MOMP and caspase activation → cell shrinkage → chromatin condensation → membrane blebbing → nuclear fragmentation → formation of membrane-bound apoptotic bodies.

The plasma membrane initially remains largely intact.

This permits apoptotic material to be contained and removed by neighboring cells or professional phagocytes.

The resulting clearance is generally less inflammatory than death pathways characterized by early membrane rupture.

If apoptotic cells are not cleared, secondary necrosis can eventually occur.

That later membrane rupture is not the defining terminal mechanism of intrinsic apoptosis.

## Physical Cause of Death

The proximate cause of canonical intrinsic apoptotic death is not one isolated membrane lesion.

Instead, death results from the combination of widespread MOMP-associated mitochondrial failure and extensive caspase-dependent destruction of essential cellular structures and functions.

After commitment:

MOMP → mitochondrial functional deterioration + release of apoptogenic proteins → caspase cascade → widespread proteolytic dismantling → irreversible loss of integrated cellular organization.

Caspases destroy structural, regulatory, metabolic and repair systems faster than the cell can rebuild them.

The nucleus is fragmented.

Cytoskeletal organization collapses.

Cellular signaling networks are dismantled.

Mitochondrial integrity has already been compromised.

The cell therefore becomes unable to restore homeostasis.

## Partial MOMP and Sublethal Apoptotic Signaling

A major refinement of the classical model is recognition that MOMP is not always perfectly synchronous across every mitochondrion.

In partial or minority MOMP, only a subset of mitochondria becomes permeabilized.

This may generate limited caspase activity insufficient to kill the cell.

Such cells can survive but may accumulate DNA damage or other long-term consequences.

Therefore:

limited stress → partial mitochondrial permeabilization → sublethal caspase activity → survival possible.

By contrast:

strong sustained stress → widespread MOMP → large-scale apoptogenic-factor release → robust caspase activation → apoptosis.

This distinction helps explain why the apoptotic pathway can sometimes generate non-lethal biological effects.

## Anastasis and Reversibility

Cells can occasionally recover from surprisingly advanced stages of apoptotic signaling.

This phenomenon is termed anastasis.

Recovery has been observed after transient caspase activation and in situations where mitochondrial damage is incomplete.

Such observations demonstrate that biochemical markers traditionally considered synonymous with death do not always identify absolute irreversibility in every single cell.

Nevertheless, these situations are exceptions to efficient canonical intrinsic apoptosis.

In most cells, widespread MOMP followed by sustained executioner-caspase activation represents an overwhelmingly lethal transition.

Thus, the most defensible formulation is:

widespread MOMP = principal commitment checkpoint,

sustained caspase-3/7 activity = execution commitment/amplification,

but neither should be described as an absolutely universal irreversible event under every experimental condition.

## Resistance Through BCL-2-Family Proteins

Cancer and other pathological cells can suppress intrinsic apoptosis through increased anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family activity.

Elevated BCL-2 can sequester apoptotic BH3 signals.

Elevated BCL-xL can suppress BAX/BAK activation.

Elevated MCL-1 can provide particularly strong resistance in cells dependent on this protein for survival.

Therefore:

BCL-2/BCL-xL/MCL-1 dominance → increased BH3-buffering capacity → BAX/BAK remain restrained → MOMP prevented → apoptosis resistance.

This mitochondrial blockade is one of the major mechanisms by which damaged cells can evade death.

## Resistance Downstream of MOMP

Resistance can also occur after mitochondrial permeabilization.

High XIAP can inhibit caspase-9 and caspase-3/7.

Reduced SMAC release or ineffective IAP neutralization can further suppress execution.

Altered APAF1 abundance or function can decrease apoptosome assembly.

Regulatory phosphorylation of caspase-9 or components of the apoptosome can modify pathway activity.

Therefore, mitochondrial priming and post-mitochondrial execution competence are separate biological variables.

A cell may undergo MOMP relatively easily but still have a high threshold for efficient caspase execution.

## Experimental Validation

Strong demonstration of intrinsic apoptosis requires establishing mitochondrial pathway dependence rather than relying on morphology alone.

The initiating stress should first be linked temporally to activation of appropriate BH3-only or BCL-2-family changes.

BAX and BAK activation can be evaluated through conformational assays, mitochondrial translocation, oligomerization studies, or genetic perturbation.

MOMP can be measured through release of cytochrome c or other intermembrane-space proteins from mitochondria into the cytosol.

The strongest mechanistic evidence combines these observations with BAX/BAK perturbation.

If BAX/BAK loss prevents cytochrome c release and death, this strongly supports canonical mitochondrial apoptosis.

Downstream evidence can include APAF1 apoptosome formation, caspase-9 activation, caspase-3/7 activation, ICAD cleavage, CAD-mediated DNA fragmentation, and characteristic apoptotic morphology.

However, each individual marker has limitations.

Cleaved caspase-3 alone does not identify the initiating pathway.

TUNEL is not specific for apoptosis.

Annexin V positivity is not sufficient to establish mitochondrial apoptosis.

Cytochrome c release is much closer to the defining mitochondrial event but should ideally be linked to BAX/BAK dependence.

## Testing BH3 Dependence

Genetic deletion or suppression of individual BH3-only proteins can identify the upstream stress-sensing route.

For genotoxic p53-dependent apoptosis, PUMA loss frequently provides substantial protection [8-9,47].

NOXA loss can provide additional protection in selected contexts.

For ER-stress-associated apoptosis, BIM perturbation can test the proposed pathway [11].

However, redundancy among BH3-only proteins must be considered.

Failure of one knockout to abolish apoptosis does not necessarily mean the BCL-2 pathway is not involved.

Multiple BH3-only proteins may collaborate.

## Testing Anti-Apoptotic Dependence

BH3 profiling or selective manipulation of anti-apoptotic proteins can identify the survival proteins restraining MOMP.

Cells strongly dependent on BCL-2, BCL-xL or MCL-1 display distinct patterns of mitochondrial priming.

Removing or pharmacologically neutralizing the relevant anti-apoptotic protein can lower the threshold for BAX/BAK activation.

This provides functional evidence that the BCL-2 network controls commitment.

However, drug-induced death should still be verified through downstream BAX/BAK–MOMP machinery rather than inferred solely from drug sensitivity.

## Testing Mitochondrial Architecture

OPA1-associated cristae remodeling can be assessed through structural imaging, OPA1 oligomer analysis, crista-junction measurements and cytochrome c release.

Preservation or overexpression of OPA1 can suppress or delay cytochrome c mobilization in selected systems [18,41].

Conversely, OPA1 disruption can sensitize cells to mitochondrial apoptosis.

Recent work using OPA1-targeting small molecules further supports the concept that cristae architecture can influence apoptotic sensitivity [42].

Nevertheless, OPA1 manipulation should be interpreted as modulation of mitochondrial release efficiency rather than the universal primary trigger of intrinsic apoptosis.

## Distinction from Extrinsic Apoptosis

Intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis ultimately converge on executioner caspases but differ primarily in initiation.

Intrinsic:

intracellular stress → BH3-only/BCL-2-family checkpoint → BAX/BAK → MOMP → apoptosome → caspase-9.

Extrinsic:

death ligand → death receptor → DISC/complex II → caspase-8.

The two routes converge at caspase-3/7.

Type II extrinsic apoptosis bridges the systems through:

caspase-8 → BID → BAX/BAK → MOMP.

Thus, the presence of MOMP does not automatically prove that apoptosis was intrinsically initiated.

The upstream trigger must also be established.

## Distinction from MPT-Driven Necrosis

Both intrinsic apoptosis and mitochondrial permeability transition-dependent necrosis involve mitochondria.

However, the defining membrane lesion differs.

Intrinsic apoptosis:

BAX/BAK-mediated outer membrane permeabilization → release of apoptogenic proteins → caspase cascade.

MPT-driven necrosis:

sustained inner mitochondrial membrane permeability transition → membrane-potential collapse → ATP depletion → osmotic and metabolic failure → necrotic death.

Therefore, mitochondrial swelling or depolarization alone cannot classify the pathway.

## Distinction from Parthanatos

AIF release can occur during mitochondrial apoptosis but AIF is also a major component of parthanatos.

In canonical intrinsic apoptosis, the defining route is:

BAX/BAK → MOMP → cytochrome c → apoptosome → caspase cascade.

In parthanatos:

severe DNA damage → PARP1 hyperactivation → PAR signaling/NAD+ disturbance → AIF mobilization → nuclear injury.

Thus, AIF nuclear translocation alone does not establish intrinsic apoptosis.

Its upstream molecular context must be determined.

## Molecular Hierarchy

The initiating stresses include DNA damage, replication stress, oxidative stress, ER stress, growth-factor withdrawal, metabolic perturbation, mitotic disruption, anoikis, and related intracellular injuries.

Stress sensors and transducers are context dependent and include ATM/ATR, p53, JNK/c-Jun, ER-stress regulators and other pathways.

BH3-only proteins such as PUMA, NOXA, BIM, BID and BAD translate these stress signals toward the mitochondrial checkpoint.

BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BCL-w and A1/BFL-1 constitute the major anti-apoptotic restraint system.

BAX and BAK are the canonical mitochondrial pore-forming effectors.

MOMP represents the defining mitochondrial execution checkpoint.

OPA1-regulated cristae remodeling controls availability of mitochondrial cytochrome c for release.

Cytochrome c activates APAF1.

APAF1 forms the apoptosome.

Caspase-9 is the intrinsic initiator caspase.

Caspase-3 and caspase-7 are the principal executioner caspases.

SMAC/DIABLO and HtrA2 antagonize IAP-dependent suppression.

XIAP is a major post-mitochondrial inhibitor.

CAD is a downstream nuclease activated after ICAD cleavage.

AIF is a parallel mitochondrial caspase-independent nuclear death effector rather than the principal canonical executor.

## Integrated DNA-Damage Route

The best-supported genotoxic sequence is:

DNA damage → ATM/ATR-associated damage response → p53 activation → PUMA and context-dependent NOXA induction → neutralization of anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins ± direct BAX/BAK activation → BAX/BAK mitochondrial oligomerization → MOMP → OPA1/cristae remodeling facilitates complete cytochrome c mobilization → cytochrome c + APAF1 + ATP/dATP → apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → extensive substrate cleavage → ICAD cleavage → CAD activation → DNA fragmentation + nuclear and cytoskeletal dismantling → apoptotic death.

## Integrated ER-Stress Route

Persistent ER stress → unfolded-protein-response signaling → BIM stabilization and/or transcriptional induction → anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family neutralization → BAX/BAK activation → MOMP → cytochrome c/SMAC release → apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → apoptosis.

## Integrated Oxidative-Stress Route

Excessive ROS → DNA/protein/mitochondrial stress → context-dependent p53/p63/JNK signaling → PUMA/NOXA/BIM or related BH3-only activation → BCL-2-family threshold crossed → BAX/BAK → MOMP → apoptosome → executioner caspases → apoptotic dismantling.

## Integrated Type II Crosstalk Route

FasL/TRAIL or another death-receptor stimulus → DISC → caspase-8 → BID cleavage → tBID → mitochondrial BAX/BAK activation → MOMP → cytochrome c + SMAC → apoptosome + XIAP relief → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7 → apoptosis.

This route should still be classified as extrinsically initiated apoptosis with mitochondrial amplification rather than primary intrinsic apoptosis.

## Integrated Core Sequence

The most general mechanism can therefore be represented as:

irreparable intracellular stress → stress-specific sensing and signaling → activation/accumulation of BH3-only proteins → selective neutralization of BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1 and related anti-apoptotic restraints and, in some models, direct BAX/BAK activation → BAX/BAK conformational activation, mitochondrial insertion and oligomerization → formation and expansion of apoptotic outer-membrane pores → widespread MOMP → remodeling of OPA1-regulated cristae and mobilization of sequestered cytochrome c → release of cytochrome c, SMAC/DIABLO, HtrA2 and other mitochondrial factors → cytochrome c–APAF1–ATP/dATP apoptosome assembly → caspase-9 activation → caspase-3/7 activation while SMAC/HtrA2 relieve XIAP-mediated inhibition → broad proteolytic destruction of nuclear, cytoskeletal, repair, signaling and membrane-associated proteins → ICAD cleavage and CAD-dependent DNA fragmentation → chromatin condensation, cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, nuclear fragmentation and apoptotic-body formation → phosphatidylserine-associated recognition and clearance → irreversible loss of integrated cellular function and death.

## Final Mechanistic Interpretation

Intrinsic apoptosis is best understood as a BCL-2-family-controlled mitochondrial cell-death program whose defining transition is BAX/BAK-dependent mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization.

Its strongest mechanistic signature is not apoptosis-associated morphology alone.

The most specific causal chain is:

stress-dependent BH3 signaling + BCL-2-family imbalance → BAX/BAK activation → MOMP → cytochrome c release → APAF1 apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3/7.

The mitochondrial decision is shaped by selective interactions among BH3-only proteins and anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins.

A major unresolved mechanistic issue remains whether physiological BAX/BAK activation universally requires direct engagement by BH3-only activators or whether removal of anti-apoptotic restraints can itself permit spontaneous membrane-dependent activation.

After BAX/BAK activation, mitochondrial architecture introduces another regulatory layer because OPA1-controlled cristae remodeling influences the efficiency and completeness of cytochrome c release.

Following MOMP, the pathway remains regulated by the balance between apoptosome-generated caspase activity and IAP-mediated inhibition.

SMAC/DIABLO and HtrA2 help shift this balance toward execution.

The principal terminal executors of canonical intrinsic apoptosis are caspase-3 and caspase-7.

AIF represents a parallel mitochondrial death effector rather than the defining executor of the canonical pathway.

Widespread MOMP remains the most defensible physiological commitment checkpoint, although partial MOMP and rare recovery demonstrate that it should not be represented as absolutely irreversible in every single-cell context.

Accordingly, intrinsic apoptosis is not simply a linear sequence from cellular damage to caspases. It is a multilayered mitochondrial decision system in which stress sensing, BH3-only proteins, anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins, BAX/BAK pore formation, cristae architecture, apoptosome assembly, IAP regulation, and executioner caspases collectively determine whether mitochondrial stress remains reversible or progresses into apoptotic cell death.
