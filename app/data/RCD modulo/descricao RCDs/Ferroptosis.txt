## Ferroptosis — Mechanistic Pathway

Ferroptosis is an established form of regulated necrotic cell death characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of oxidized phospholipids in cellular membranes until lipid peroxide production exceeds the capacity of endogenous antioxidant systems and causes irreversible membrane dysfunction. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, ferroptosis is not executed primarily by caspase-mediated proteolysis, MLKL oligomerization, or gasdermin pore formation. Instead, its defining biochemical event is excessive peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids, particularly membrane phospholipids containing arachidonic acid and adrenic acid, in an iron-dependent environment [1–6]. Ferroptosis therefore represents a metabolic and redox-controlled form of regulated cell death in which iron metabolism, lipid composition, antioxidant capacity, and membrane repair mechanisms collectively determine whether a cell survives oxidative stress or crosses the threshold toward irreversible membrane failure.

The pathway was initially defined through studies of erastin, a small molecule capable of inducing a non-apoptotic and iron-dependent form of cell death in oncogenic RAS-expressing cancer cells [1]. Subsequent studies established that inhibition of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xc−, depletion of intracellular glutathione, or direct inhibition of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) can initiate a characteristic form of lipid peroxidation-dependent cell death that is suppressible by iron chelators and lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidants [1–5]. The current mechanistic model therefore does not define ferroptosis simply by the presence of reactive oxygen species. Instead, ferroptosis requires a specific imbalance in which iron-dependent phospholipid oxidation overwhelms the systems that normally detoxify phospholipid hydroperoxides and terminate lipid-radical chain reactions [3–6].

One of the principal routes leading to ferroptosis begins at system xc−, a heterodimeric amino acid antiporter composed primarily of the light-chain transporter SLC7A11 and the heavy-chain component SLC3A2. System xc− imports extracellular cystine in exchange for intracellular glutamate. Once imported, cystine is reduced intracellularly to cysteine, which is required for synthesis of glutathione (GSH). GSH subsequently functions as the reducing cofactor used by GPX4 to detoxify membrane phospholipid hydroperoxides [1,3,7]. Consequently, inhibition of system xc− by erastin, extracellular glutamate, cystine starvation, or transcriptional repression of SLC7A11 reduces intracellular cysteine availability, limits GSH synthesis, and progressively compromises GPX4-dependent phospholipid peroxide detoxification [1,3,7].

The biochemical consequence of this pathway can therefore be represented as cystine limitation or system xc− inhibition → reduced SLC7A11-dependent cystine uptake → intracellular cysteine depletion → decreased glutathione synthesis → insufficient GPX4 reducing capacity → accumulation of phospholipid hydroperoxides. Importantly, GPX4 protein itself does not necessarily disappear during this route. Instead, its ability to reduce phospholipid hydroperoxides becomes functionally constrained because the glutathione required for its catalytic cycle becomes limiting. This distinguishes system xc−-dependent ferroptosis from direct GPX4 inhibition, although both routes converge on the same fundamental biochemical defect: failure to remove phospholipid hydroperoxides before they propagate through cellular membranes [3–5,7].

A second canonical route bypasses cysteine and glutathione metabolism and directly targets GPX4. Compounds such as RSL3 covalently inhibit the catalytic selenocysteine-containing active site of GPX4, producing rapid loss of phospholipid hydroperoxide-reducing activity even when intracellular cysteine and glutathione remain available [3–5]. Genetic inactivation of GPX4 similarly causes widespread phospholipid peroxidation and can trigger ferroptotic tissue injury in vivo, demonstrating that GPX4 is not merely associated with ferroptosis but constitutes one of its central negative regulators [8]. Selenium availability is also important because GPX4 is a selenoprotein, and efficient incorporation of selenium into GPX4 is required for maximal resistance to hydroperoxide-induced ferroptosis [9].

GPX4 catalyzes the reduction of phospholipid hydroperoxides, commonly represented as PLOOH, into their corresponding non-toxic phospholipid alcohols, PLOH, using reducing equivalents derived from GSH. Through this reaction, GPX4 prevents membrane lipid hydroperoxides from decomposing into lipid alkoxyl and lipid peroxyl radicals capable of initiating new rounds of oxidation [5,10]. Consequently, the GSH–GPX4 system functions as the principal enzymatic barrier separating physiologically manageable lipid oxidation from uncontrolled ferroptotic lipid peroxidation.

GPX4 abundance is also linked to cellular nutrient sensing. Cyst(e)ine availability can influence GPX4 protein synthesis through Rag-dependent mTORC1 signaling and regulation of 4EBP-mediated translation [11]. Thus, cysteine availability affects ferroptosis at more than one biochemical level: it controls glutathione production required for GPX4 catalytic activity and can also influence GPX4 protein synthesis itself. This provides an additional connection between amino acid availability, metabolic signaling, and ferroptosis susceptibility.

However, disabling GPX4 is insufficient to explain the entire pathway because ferroptosis also requires an appropriate pool of oxidizable membrane phospholipids. This substrate pool is generated principally through lipid remodeling involving ACSL4 and LPCAT3 [5,12,13]. ACSL4 activates long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly arachidonic acid and adrenic acid, by converting them into their corresponding acyl-CoA derivatives. LPCAT3 subsequently incorporates these activated polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids, including phosphatidylethanolamine species. The resulting PUFA-containing phospholipids are highly susceptible to hydrogen abstraction and subsequent radical-chain oxidation.

Genetic depletion of ACSL4 markedly reduces ferroptosis sensitivity because the membrane contains fewer phospholipid substrates capable of supporting lethal lipid peroxidation. Similarly, alterations in LPCAT3 activity modify the amount of peroxidizable PUFA-containing phospholipids present within cellular membranes [5,12,13]. ACSL4 and LPCAT3 should therefore be regarded as substrate-generating components of the ferroptotic machinery. They do not themselves execute membrane destruction, but they establish the membrane composition necessary for ferroptotic lipid oxidation to occur efficiently.

Once incorporated into membrane phospholipids, PUFAs can undergo oxidation through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms. Lipoxygenases such as members of the ALOX family can catalyze oxidation of PUFA-containing lipids in specific cellular contexts, while cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase-associated processes and other redox enzymes can promote lipid peroxide formation [5,12,14]. At the same time, redox-active iron supports non-enzymatic lipid autoxidation. Ferrous iron can participate in Fenton and Fenton-like reactions that generate highly reactive radical species capable of initiating hydrogen abstraction from PUFA-containing phospholipids. Once a phospholipid radical is generated, reaction with molecular oxygen produces a phospholipid peroxyl radical, which can abstract hydrogen from a neighboring phospholipid and propagate a self-amplifying lipid-radical chain reaction [5,10,14].

Iron availability is therefore a second fundamental determinant of ferroptosis. Cellular iron homeostasis controls the size of the labile intracellular Fe2+ pool capable of promoting oxidative chemistry. Transferrin-bound iron can be internalized through transferrin receptor-dependent pathways, processed within endosomal compartments, and released into intracellular iron pools. Conversely, excess iron can be stored safely within ferritin complexes. Ferroptosis susceptibility increases when this balance shifts toward expansion of the redox-active labile iron pool [3–6].

One important mechanism connecting intracellular iron storage with ferroptosis is ferritinophagy. In this process, the selective autophagy receptor NCOA4 recognizes ferritin and promotes its delivery to lysosomes, where ferritin is degraded and stored iron is released [15]. Increased NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy can therefore elevate the labile Fe2+ pool and enhance the radical chemistry required for lipid peroxidation. In relevant experimental systems, inhibition of NCOA4 or disruption of autophagic ferritin degradation suppresses iron accumulation, lipid ROS formation, and ferroptotic death [15]. Ferritinophagy thus represents a mechanistic bridge between autophagy, iron homeostasis, and ferroptosis, although autophagy itself is not universally required for every mode of ferroptosis induction.

A third important regulatory entry route involves the tumor suppressor p53. Cellular stress can activate p53, which under specific conditions represses transcription of SLC7A11 and thereby decreases cystine uptake [16]. Reduced SLC7A11 expression lowers the capacity of the cell to maintain glutathione-dependent antioxidant defense and increases sensitivity to ferroptosis. This ferroptotic function of p53 can occur independently of its classical ability to activate apoptosis. Acetylation of p53 is important for this tumor-suppressive ferroptotic activity, indicating that p53 post-translational state influences whether p53 can efficiently control ferroptosis [17].

The p53 pathway is nevertheless context dependent and cannot be reduced to SLC7A11 repression alone. A distinct p53-dependent ferroptotic pathway involves ALOX12. ALOX12 activity is required for ferroptosis triggered by specific p53 tumor-suppressive programs but is not universally required for ferroptosis initiated through direct GPX4 inhibition or other ferroptosis-inducing compounds [18]. Therefore, p53 and ALOX12 should be regarded as stimulus-dependent regulatory components rather than universal core executioners.

When the production of oxidized phospholipids increases, several endogenous defense systems attempt to prevent ferroptotic commitment. The canonical defense pathway is system xc− → cysteine → glutathione → GPX4. However, the discovery of GPX4-independent antioxidant systems demonstrated that ferroptosis resistance is distributed across several cellular compartments rather than controlled by GPX4 alone.

One of the most important parallel defense systems is ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1). FSP1 is recruited to cellular membranes through N-terminal myristoylation and functions as an NAD(P)H-dependent oxidoreductase that regenerates reduced coenzyme Q, or ubiquinol, from ubiquinone [19,20]. Ubiquinol acts as a lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidant capable of intercepting lipid peroxyl radicals and terminating membrane lipid-radical chain reactions. FSP1 therefore suppresses ferroptosis independently of GSH and GPX4. High FSP1 activity can protect cells in which GPX4 activity is compromised, whereas FSP1 loss increases susceptibility to lipid peroxidation-induced death [19,20].

A conceptually related defense operates within mitochondria through dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH). DHODH is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it participates in pyrimidine synthesis while simultaneously reducing ubiquinone to ubiquinol [21]. This reaction generates an antioxidant pool of mitochondrial ubiquinol capable of suppressing lipid peroxidation within mitochondrial membranes. DHODH therefore functions in parallel with mitochondrial GPX4, and inhibition of DHODH strongly sensitizes cells with limited GPX4 activity to ferroptosis [21]. This compartment-specific defense helps explain why ferroptosis sensitivity depends not only on overall antioxidant capacity but also on the subcellular localization of lipid peroxidation and antioxidant systems.

The GCH1–BH4 pathway provides another GPX4-independent ferroptosis defense. Tetrahydrobiopterin can function as a radical-trapping antioxidant and can influence membrane lipid composition, thereby limiting propagation of lipid peroxidation. Although this axis represents an established ferroptosis-suppressive mechanism, its relative contribution varies between cellular systems and should be considered alongside the GPX4–GSH, FSP1–CoQ, and DHODH–CoQH2 defense networks rather than as a universal replacement for them [4,12].

NRF2 acts at a broader transcriptional level to increase ferroptosis resistance. Activation of NRF2 promotes expression of multiple genes involved in antioxidant defense, glutathione metabolism, cystine transport, NADPH generation, iron storage, and quinone metabolism [22,23]. NRF2 can therefore simultaneously decrease free iron availability, strengthen GSH-dependent antioxidant capacity, increase SLC7A11-associated cystine utilization, and support FSP1/CoQ-related defenses. Recent work has additionally demonstrated that NRF2 controls iron homeostasis and ferroptosis through regulators including HERC2 and VAMP8 [22].

NRF2 signaling is nevertheless context dependent because not all NRF2 target genes suppress ferroptosis in the same manner. For example, induction of genes involved in iron sequestration and antioxidant metabolism generally opposes ferroptosis, whereas excessive HMOX1 activity can increase intracellular iron release and, under particular circumstances, promote lipid peroxidation. The final ferroptosis phenotype therefore depends on the balance among multiple NRF2-regulated metabolic outputs rather than NRF2 expression alone.

The central biochemical transition toward ferroptosis occurs when phospholipid peroxide generation becomes faster than the combined activity of GPX4, FSP1, DHODH, BH4-associated antioxidant mechanisms, NRF2-dependent defense programs, and other radical-quenching pathways. At this stage, phospholipid hydroperoxides accumulate within membranes and begin to generate secondary lipid radicals and electrophilic lipid-breakdown products. Propagation of lipid oxidation alters membrane organization, protein function, ion permeability, and membrane mechanical properties [5,10,12].

There is currently no universally defined single molecular point of no return in ferroptosis. Early events such as cysteine depletion, glutathione depletion, GPX4 inhibition, ferritin degradation, and initial lipid peroxide accumulation can remain experimentally reversible if effective ferroptosis suppressors are introduced sufficiently early. Iron chelators can remove catalytic iron, while ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1, vitamin E, and related lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidants interrupt lipid-radical propagation [3,8,10]. Ferroptotic commitment is therefore best considered a threshold phenomenon in which the rate and spatial extent of membrane phospholipid oxidation exceed both antioxidant capacity and membrane-repair capacity.

The late execution stage involves progressive physical failure of membrane systems. Lipid peroxidation can initially accumulate in intracellular organelles and later extend to the plasma membrane [6,24]. Oxidized phospholipids alter membrane packing and permeability, promoting formation of nanoscale membrane lesions or pores. These lesions permit abnormal movement of ions across the plasma membrane and are associated with a sustained increase in cytosolic Ca2+ [25].

The Ca2+ influx generated during plasma-membrane injury activates membrane-repair systems, particularly ESCRT-III. ESCRT-III is recruited to damaged membrane regions and attempts to remove or repair compromised portions of the plasma membrane [26]. Genetic or pharmacological impairment of this repair system accelerates ferroptotic cell death, whereas increased ESCRT-III activity can delay plasma-membrane failure. ESCRT-III therefore functions as a protective late-stage modifier of ferroptosis rather than as an upstream inhibitor of lipid peroxidation.

Persistent membrane damage eventually overwhelms ESCRT-mediated repair. Increased membrane permeability permits continued ion influx, followed by water entry and osmotic cellular swelling. Osmoprotective experiments demonstrate that manipulation of extracellular osmolarity can delay ferroptotic membrane rupture, supporting an important contribution of osmotic stress to the terminal phase of ferroptosis [27]. Thus, lipid peroxidation does not kill simply by creating chemically oxidized molecules; it progressively converts normally selective biological membranes into structures incapable of maintaining ionic and osmotic gradients.

More recent evidence has identified NINJ1 as an important regulator of the final plasma-membrane rupture step. During ferroptosis, NINJ1 oligomerizes and promotes catastrophic plasma-membrane rupture and release of intracellular proteins and damage-associated molecular patterns [28]. Importantly, NINJ1 is positioned downstream of the defining ferroptotic chemistry. NINJ1 deficiency does not prevent upstream phospholipid peroxidation, Ca2+ entry, or cellular swelling but can markedly reduce or delay the loss of plasma-membrane integrity and release of cytosolic macromolecules [28]. NINJ1 should therefore be considered a terminal rupture facilitator rather than the initiating executor of ferroptosis.

This distinction is mechanistically important. The fundamental execution chemistry of ferroptosis remains overwhelming phospholipid peroxidation. NINJ1 appears to convert severe membrane injury into efficient macroscopic plasma-membrane rupture, but NINJ1 activation alone does not define ferroptosis. Thus, the current late-stage model can be represented as excessive phospholipid oxidation → membrane permeability defects and nanoscale lesions → Ca2+ influx → ESCRT-III-dependent attempts at membrane repair → persistent ion imbalance and osmotic swelling → NINJ1-assisted plasma-membrane rupture → release of intracellular contents and irreversible cell death [25–28].

Mitochondria also participate in ferroptosis, although their requirement varies with the mode of induction. Characteristic mitochondrial alterations frequently include reduction in mitochondrial size, increased membrane density, loss or reduction of cristae, and occasional disruption of the outer mitochondrial membrane [29]. These morphological features are commonly associated with ferroptosis but are not themselves diagnostic because mitochondrial responses vary substantially between experimental contexts.

Mitochondrial metabolism and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species are particularly important during ferroptosis caused by cysteine deprivation, metabolic stress, or iron overload [30]. In these settings, mitochondrial respiration and metabolic flux can contribute to ROS generation and amplification of lipid peroxidation. In contrast, direct GPX4 inhibition can produce extensive ferroptotic lipid peroxidation even when mitochondrial metabolism contributes less strongly to the lethal process [30]. Therefore, mitochondrial dysfunction is a biologically relevant and context-dependent amplifier rather than a universally required ferroptotic execution mechanism.

Morphologically, ferroptosis is distinguished from apoptosis by the relative absence of classical chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, apoptotic body formation, and caspase-dependent cellular dismantling. Early ferroptotic cells may retain a relatively preserved nucleus while mitochondrial alterations and membrane lipid oxidation become prominent. During later stages, cellular and organellar swelling, plasma-membrane permeabilization, and eventual lysis develop [6,29]. Nevertheless, morphology alone is insufficient for assigning ferroptosis because similar features can occur during other forms of regulated necrosis.

The most convincing experimental identification of ferroptosis therefore combines biochemical evidence, genetic evidence, and pharmacological rescue. Demonstration of increased phospholipid peroxidation using lipid-sensitive probes such as C11-BODIPY or oxidized phospholipid lipidomics provides direct biochemical support. Measurement of malondialdehyde or 4-hydroxynonenal can provide additional evidence of lipid oxidation, although these products are not specific to ferroptosis. Increased PTGS2 or CHAC1 expression can similarly accompany ferroptosis but should be treated as supportive biomarkers rather than defining mechanistic criteria [3–6].

Pharmacological rescue provides stronger causal evidence. Ferroptotic death should be suppressible by lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidants such as ferrostatin-1 or liproxstatin-1 and by interventions that reduce iron-dependent oxidation, including iron chelators such as deferoxamine [1–4,8]. Conversely, inhibitors of caspases, RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptosis, or gasdermin-dependent pyroptosis should not eliminate the defining lipid peroxidation-driven death process. The combination of iron dependence, phospholipid peroxidation, and rescue by radical-trapping antioxidants remains one of the strongest experimental criteria for distinguishing ferroptosis from other regulated cell-death pathways.

Genetic perturbation can further establish pathway specificity. GPX4 deletion strongly promotes ferroptosis, whereas restoration or increased expression of GPX4 suppresses it [8]. SLC7A11 overexpression can protect against ferroptosis initiated through cystine limitation, while ACSL4 or LPCAT3 depletion reduces the pool of oxidizable membrane PUFAs and therefore decreases ferroptosis sensitivity [12,13]. FSP1 overexpression can suppress ferroptosis independently of GPX4 [19,20], while NCOA4 depletion can prevent ferroptosis in contexts dependent on ferritinophagy-mediated iron release [15]. The relative requirement of individual components therefore depends on the upstream ferroptosis-inducing stimulus, but all of these routes ultimately converge on the balance between phospholipid peroxide generation and phospholipid peroxide elimination.

Ferroptosis can interact with other regulated cell-death pathways without sharing their core execution mechanisms. In acute kidney injury models, necroptosis and ferroptosis can behave as alternative mechanisms of tissue injury, and suppression of one pathway may modify the relative contribution of the other [31]. Nevertheless, ferroptosis does not require RIPK3 or MLKL activation as its defining execution system.

Ferroptosis also shares aspects of plasma-membrane repair with pyroptosis. Ca2+-dependent recruitment of ESCRT machinery can limit plasma-membrane injury during both ferroptosis and gasdermin-mediated pyroptosis [25,26,32]. However, this represents convergence at the level of cellular membrane repair rather than convergence of the death-execution machinery itself. Gasdermin pore formation remains characteristic of pyroptosis, whereas excessive phospholipid peroxidation remains the defining biochemical event in ferroptosis [33].

The biological consequences of ferroptosis extend far beyond experimental cancer models. Ferroptotic mechanisms have been implicated in acute kidney injury, ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurodegenerative diseases, liver injury, inflammatory disorders, immune responses, and multiple cancers [3,4,34]. In tumors, ferroptosis is particularly relevant because many cancer cells exhibit high iron requirements, altered PUFA metabolism, oxidative stress, and dependency on SLC7A11, GPX4, NRF2, FSP1, or related antioxidant systems. These features can create therapeutic vulnerabilities in tumors that have become resistant to classical apoptosis-based treatments [34].

Overall, the ferroptosis pathway can be reconstructed as a continuous sequence beginning with disruption of antioxidant defense, increased iron availability, or expansion of oxidizable phospholipid substrates. Cystine deprivation or inhibition of system xc− reduces SLC7A11/SLC3A2-dependent cystine uptake, leading to cysteine deficiency, glutathione depletion, and loss of effective GPX4 activity. Alternatively, compounds such as RSL3 directly inhibit GPX4 and bypass cysteine metabolism. In parallel, ACSL4 activates polyunsaturated fatty acids and LPCAT3 incorporates them into membrane phospholipids, generating the PUFA-phospholipid pool required for ferroptosis. Transferrin-dependent iron uptake, iron overload, or NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy can increase the intracellular labile Fe2+ pool and accelerate radical generation. Enzymatic lipid oxidation and iron-dependent autoxidation then initiate phospholipid peroxyl-radical chain reactions.

Under physiological conditions, GPX4–GSH, FSP1–CoQH2, mitochondrial DHODH–CoQH2, GCH1–BH4, NRF2-controlled antioxidant programs, and other redox defense systems suppress this process. Ferroptosis occurs when these protective systems can no longer compensate for phospholipid peroxide production. Phospholipid hydroperoxides and propagating lipid radicals then accumulate throughout cellular membranes, altering membrane architecture and permeability. At the plasma membrane, oxidized phospholipids promote nanoscale lesions, ion dysregulation, and Ca2+ influx. Ca2+-dependent ESCRT-III recruitment attempts to repair these lesions, but persistent peroxidation eventually exceeds membrane-repair capacity. Continued ion imbalance produces osmotic water entry and cellular swelling, after which NINJ1 can facilitate terminal plasma-membrane rupture and release of intracellular contents [3–6,19–28].

The integrated mechanistic sequence can therefore be represented as cystine starvation/system xc− inhibition, direct GPX4 inhibition, p53-dependent SLC7A11 repression, ferritinophagy, or iron overload → decreased GSH–GPX4 activity and/or expansion of the labile Fe2+ pool → ACSL4/LPCAT3-dependent enrichment of membranes with PUFA-containing phospholipids → iron-dependent and enzymatic phospholipid oxidation → accumulation of phospholipid hydroperoxides and propagating lipid radicals → failure of GPX4-, FSP1-, DHODH-, BH4-, and NRF2-associated antioxidant defenses → progressive membrane lipid peroxidation → plasma-membrane permeability defects and nanoscale pore formation → Ca2+ influx and ESCRT-III-mediated repair attempts → persistent ion dysregulation and osmotic swelling → NINJ1-assisted plasma-membrane rupture → DAMP release and irreversible regulated necrotic cell death.

Thus, the defining mechanistic feature of ferroptosis is not simply oxidative stress, iron accumulation, GPX4 reduction, mitochondrial alteration, or expression of individual biomarkers. Ferroptosis is specifically defined by the failure to control iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation. GPX4 and the parallel antioxidant systems determine whether oxidized phospholipids can be detoxified, ACSL4 and LPCAT3 determine whether membranes contain sufficient oxidizable PUFA substrates, cellular iron metabolism determines the capacity for radical generation, and late membrane-repair and rupture mechanisms determine how rapidly accumulated lipid damage is converted into irreversible plasma-membrane failure. The precise molecular and biophysical transition through which oxidized phospholipids generate the membrane lesions that initiate terminal ion dysregulation remains incompletely resolved, but current evidence supports a model in which catastrophic phospholipid peroxidation is the central lethal event and membrane rupture is its terminal cellular consequence.
